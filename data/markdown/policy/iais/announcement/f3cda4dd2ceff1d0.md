About the IAIS 
The International Association of Insurance Supervisors (IAIS) is a voluntary membership 
organisation of insurance supervisors and regulators from more than 200 jurisdictions. The mission 
of the IAIS is to promote effective and globally consistent supervisi on of the insurance industry in 
order to develop and maintain fair, safe and stable insurance markets for the benefit and protection 
of policyholders and to contribute to global financial stability.  
Established in 1994, the IAIS is the international standard -setting body responsible for developing 
principles, standards and other supporting material for the supervision of the insurance sector and 
assisting in their implementation. The IAIS also provide s a forum for Members to share their 
experiences and understanding of insurance supervision and insurance markets.  
The IAIS coordinates its work with other international financial policymakers and associations of 
supervisors or regulators, and assists in shaping financial systems globally. In particular, the IAIS is 
a member of the Financial Stability Board (FSB), member of the Standards Advisory Council of the 
International Accounting Standards Board (IASB), and partner in the Access to Insurance Initiative 
(A2ii). In recognition of its collective expertise, the IAIS also is routinely called upon by the G20 
leaders and other international standard-setting bodies for input on insurance issues as well as on 
issues related to the regulation and supervision of the global financial sector.

Introduction 
To support its mission of effective and globally consistent supervision to protect policyholders and to 
contribute to global financial stability, the IAIS adopted in November 2019 the Holistic Framework 
for the assessment and mitigation of systemic risk in the global insurance sector (HF)1.  
A key element of the HF is the Global Monitoring Exercise (GME), designed to detect key risks and 
trends and the potential build-up of systemic risk in the global insurance sector. The GME includes 
an annual assessment by the IAIS of:  
• Potential systemic risk arising from sector -wide trends with regard to specific activities and 
exposures (Sector-Wide Monitoring, or SWM); and  
• The possible concentration of systemic risks at an individual insurer level, using an assessment 
methodology as one of the inputs (Individual Insurer Monitoring, or IIM).  
As stated in the 2023 GME document,2 the GME will continue to evolve, including through a review 
of the IIM assessment methodology and SWM every three years, in order to capture:  
• Improvements suggested by IAIS members;  
• Developments in the insurance sector;  
• Changes in insurers’ activities or products;  
• Growth in the global insurance market; and  
• Improvements in methods and approaches for measuring systemic importance in the insurance 
sector and the broader financial sector.  
The objective of this public consultation is to seek stakeholders’ input on the review of the IIM 
assessment methodology for the next three -year cycle of application (2026 –2028). Input received 
through this consultation will be considered in the finalisation of the IIM assessment methodology 
and will be reflected in an updated public GME document.  
The remainder of the document is structured as follows: 
• Section 2 starts with a description of the 2023–2025 IIM absolute assessment methodology and 
illustrates the proposed changes to some of the IIM scoring indicators, including (i) a revision of 
the Level 3 assets indicator; (ii) a proposed methodology for adjusting the scores of the indicators 
belonging to the Asset Liquidation category ; and (iii) some proposals to simplify the Intra -
Financial Assets and Intra-Financial Liabilities indicators. 
• Section 3 discusses proposed adjustments to the indicators’ weighting.  
• Section 4 contains a proposal for updating the denominators. 
• Section 5 concludes. 
A stakeholder session on the public consultation will take place on 27 June 2025. The deadline for 
providing input to this public consultation is 18 August 2025.

2.1 Level 3 assets indicator 
2.1.1 Background 
The objective of the level 3 indicator aims to measure the potential scale of fire sales of illiquid assets 
by an insurer in distressed financial market situations. 
In the current IIM assessment methodology, the score of the level 3 assets indicator is calculated by 
dividing values reported in row 30.3, “Level 3 financial assets”, by the indicator denominator. In the 
current technical specifications on the classes of financial assets, it is clarified that , in the data 
template rows for level 1, level 2 and level 3 financial assets, the gross fair value of all assets that 
are valued on a recurring basis using fair value hierarchy levels 1, 2 and 3 should be reported. This 
includes assets that can be valued under another basis, but which the insurer chooses to hold at fair 
value.3 Assets valued using other methods in the financial statements, such as amortised cost , are 
currently not included. In addition to the accounting guidance, it is noted that, for the purpose of this 
data collection, insurers should exclude any direct holdings of physical real estate, and include other 
holdings of all real estate-related assets as financial instruments in the relevant class of assets. 
However, in recent years, some issues have arisen: 
• Firstly, the current methodology is influenced by accounting where reclassifications of assets 
from amortised cost to fair value has increased the amount of level 3 asset s. This issue was 
especially prominent in the 2024 GME , where the implementation of IFRS 17 and delayed 
implementation of IFRS 9 (2018 –2023) led to the significant reclassifications of assets from 
amortised cost to fair value, strongly influencing the level 3 asset indicator.  Although for some 
insurers recent increases to the level 3 asset indicator have been driven by asset allocation, a 
significant amount has been due to accounting reclassifications. These reclassifications have 
raised the amount of level 3 asset s, thereby increasing the score of the indicator without any 
actual change in the underlying assets held. This has contributed to a potentially misleading 
representation of insurer holdings of level 3 assets.  More specifically, the GIMAR reports show 
notable increases in the level 3 indicator’s contribution in the total systemic risk scores over the 
years. In the most recent year s, the contribution to total IIM score was 22.9% ( year-end 2023 
data), up from 17.7% (year-end 2022 data) and 17% (year-end 2021 data), in comparison with 
a contribution of 4.8% based on year-end 2017 data.  
• Secondly, there are differences in accounting treatments  among insurers, both within and 
between jurisdictions. Inconsistent accounting treatments for the same asset s by different 
insurers have distorted comparisons across time periods for the same insurer, between insurers, 
and between jurisdictions.

• Thirdly, the level 3 indicator may fall short as a measure of asset liquidation as it may not

standards. Additionally, the current indicator reflects valuation uncertainty only if the asset is 
booked at fair value.

2.2 Asset liquidation indicators’ adjustment factors 
2.2.1 Background 
Over the years, a concern has emerged that the systemic risk scores for asset liquidation indicators5 
may not sufficiently take into account insurers’ asset liability management practices. In particular, life 
insurers are important providers of credit to the economy and their business model is well positioned 
to safely provide funding to less liquid markets. Additionally, by investing in long -duration illiquid 
assets to back their long -duration illiquid liabilities, life insurers naturally hedge the liquidity risk in 
their asset and liability portfolios. However, in the current methodology, the asset liquid ation 
indicators are based on the overall size of an insurer’s illiquid assets and liabilities , and therefore 
this natural hedging and other liquidity risk management practices are not explicitly considered when 
calculating the systemic risk scores for the asset liquidation category.  
2.2.2 Proposed changes 
A proposal to address this concern is to adjust the asset liquidation indicators’ scores based on each 
insurer’s Insurance Liquidity Ratio (ILR), which is currently an ancillary indicator.6 The IAIS recently 
consulted on the ancillary risk indicators and is currently assessing this feedback, and giving 
consideration to the complexity of the ILR and the availability of required data. Under this proposal, 
insurers whose ILR falls below a given threshold would have a multiplier applied to their asset 
liquidation indicator scores, reflecting higher liquidity risk. On the contrary, insurers whose ILR sits 
above a given threshold would have a haircut applied to their asset liquidation indicators’ scores, 
reflecting lower liquidity risk. Using the ILR allows for comparability across the global Insurer Pool, 
while limiting additional reporting burden for participating insurers. 
The process would work as follows: 
1. Set a Liquidity Ratio Threshold (LRT) based on the ILR. 
2. Set haircut(s) for insurers whose ILR > LRT and multiplier(s) for insurers whose ILR < LRT. 
3. Apply a Liquidity Risk Adjustment (LRA) based on the multiplier(s) or haircut(s) to the asset 
liquidation indicators: 
Risk score = (Short-term funding, level 3 assets, and liability liquidity) x LRA.

2.3  Intra-financial Assets and Intra-financial Liabilities indicators  
2.3.1 Background 
Intra-financial assets and liabilities are important indicators for the IIM methodology, monitoring the 
interconnectedness of an insurer with the broader financial sector. Many different aspects of 
interconnectedness need to be reflected in these indicators.  
Stakeholders have requested a reduction of the IIM data collection several times over the past years. 
In response to these requests, the IAIS is proposing a simplification of the Intra-financial Assets (IFA) 
and Intra-financial Liabilities (IFL) indicators. This simplification would enable the removal of certain 
detailed data elements from the IIM data collection.  
2.3.2 Proposed changes 
2.3.2.1 Simplification of derivatives data collection  
Derivatives are currently included in several indicators of the IIM methodology: 
• Over-the-counter (OTC) derivatives with financial institutions are included in IFA (when net 
positive) and IFL (when net negative), with both their net value and their potential future exposure 
(PFE). 
• The PFE of exchange-traded derivatives is also included in the short-term funding indicator by 
deducting the PFE of OTC derivatives from the total. 
• In addition, the gross notional amount of OTC derivatives forms the Derivatives indicator. 
It is proposed to:  
• Remove the PFE of derivatives from the IFA/IFL indicators , to allow the removal of the s plit of 
derivatives with a financial counterparty (Rows 40.B.1.a.1 and 40.B.2.a.1). 
• Include in the short-term funding indicator the part of the PFE that in the current methodology 
lies in the IFA/IFL indicators, to allow the removal of the split of PFE for OTC derivatives (Rows 
40.B.1.a and 40.B.2.a). 
• Keep the split between OTC and exchange-traded derivatives for the gross notional amount, to 
continue monitoring of the corresponding developments. 
2.3.2.2 Simplification of deductions from total borrowing  
In the current methodology , exclusions from borrowing in the IFL indicator are collected in four 
different rows, split by: 
• Central bank or public sector entity; and 
• Certificate of deposit or other borrowing. 
It is proposed to combine t hese four rows into one single row to be deducted from total borrowing 
for the purpose of the IFL indicator. 
This simplification would also have a minor effect on the calculation of the Insurance Liquidity Ratio 
(ILR), one of the ancillary indicators for liquidity risk. In the current ILR calculation, certificates of 
deposit are included  in the liquidity needs ( with haircuts depending on further specificities ), while 
other borrowing is excluded. Removing the split between certificates of deposits and other borrowing 
in the data collection would lead to other borrowing being included in the ILR as well. 
As IFA, banking deposits and holdings of certificates of deposit of central banks and public sector 
entities are already combined in one row.

Indicators’ weighting 
3.1 Background 
Weights are applied to each indicator’s score to calculate the total systemic risk scores of insurers. 
The indicators are spread across five categories: Size, Global activity, Interconnectedness, Asset 
liquidation, and Substitutability. Each category aims to measure a specific aspect of systemic risk. 
Over time, the weights assigned to each indicator may become less suitable due to various factors. 
These factors could include the evolution of insurance markets, changes in accounting regimes, 
updates to solvency frameworks, shifts in the composition of  the IIM Insurer Pool, and changes in 
the nature of systemic risk overall. The table below shows the current indicators and their respective 
weights.

3.2 Proposed changes 
As the weights are only one component of the IIM scoring calculation, any changes to the indicators’ 
weighting should be evaluated in conjunction with other proposed revisions outlined i n this 
document. Important considerations are (i) maintaining comparability of future IIM scoring results 
with those from prior years, and (ii) ensuring the weights appropriately reflect the relative importance 
of the indicators.

The IAIS is evaluating changes to the indicators’ weighting that would entail (i) reducing the weight 
of the level 3 assets indicator, (ii) balancing such reduction within the asset liquidation category by 
increasing the weight of the short-term funding indicator, and (iii) shift part of the weight of the number 
of countries indicator to the revenues outside of home country indicator. 
As a simplification of the scoring methodology, the IAIS is also reviewing the possibility to remove 
the rescaling factor between the short-term funding and liability liquidity indicators.  
Finally, as indicated in paragraphs 53 and 54 of the current GME document, the IAIS is closely 
monitoring developments in the derivatives market as part of the review of the Absolute Reference 
Value (ARV) for the derivatives trading indicator.

 Denominators 
4.1 Background 
The IIM assessment methodology is predominantly based on an absolute assessment approach (ie 
calculating scores of insurers against a fixed benchmark based on the sample total in a defined base 
year). The base year for the absolute methodology for the 2023 –2025 GME cycle was set using 
denominators from the data exercise year 2018. 
A comparison of the denominators of the absolute assessment approach and of the GME 2024 
relative approach is available in the Annex of the 2024 Global Insurance Market Report (GIMAR). 
4.2 Proposed changes 
To reflect recent developments in the insurance sector and growth in the global insurance market, 
the IAIS is considering updating the denominators  of the absolute assessment approach. Updates 
to the denominators would be aligned with revisions to the definitions of the IIM indicators proposed 
in this document.

systemic risk analysis. 
40. In addition, if a certain jurisdiction has no or a limited amount of insurer(s) represented in the 
Insurer Pool, the supervisor is encouraged to apply supervisory discretion to include insurers into 
the Insurer Pool, in particular for insurance groups approaching 65 billion in total assets, in order to 
enhance the regional balance and diversity of the IIM.” 
5.2 Proposed changes 
The IAIS proposes the following changes to the Insurer Pool selection criteria:  
• Increasing asset thresholds to account for inflation (paragraph 38): It is proposed to increase the 
“total assets” thresholds to USD 70 billion and USD 235 billion, respectively, based on the IMF 
inflation rate for advanced economies .7 These thresholds may be reviewed again in the next 
triennial review cycle. 
The IAIS will also be assessing whether the definition of “premiums from jurisdictions outside the 
home jurisdiction” could be aligned further with the IIM technical specifications, to also capture 
premiums assumed through reinsurance.  
• Enhancing regional balance and diversity (revised paragraph 40): 
“40. In addition, if a certain jurisdiction has no or a limited amount of insurer(s) represented in 
the Insurer Pool  and participation would improve the Insurer Pool’s  regional balance and 
diversity, the supervisor is encouraged to apply supervisory discretion to include insurers into the 
Insurer Pool. This applies in particular for insurance groups approaching the criteria above, with 
at least USD 55 billion of total assets and a ratio of premiums from jurisdictions outside the home 
jurisdiction to total premiums of 5% or more.”

o Climate related risks 
Climate change is recognised as a source of financial risk. It may have an impact on the resilience 
of individual financial institutions, including insurers, as well as on financial stability through physical 
and transition risks. Physical risk refers to increased damage and losses from physical phenomena 
associated with climate-related trends (eg changing weather patterns or rising sea levels) and events 
(eg natural disasters or extreme weather). Transition risk refers to disruptions and shifts associated 
with a disorde rly transition to a low -carbon economy, which may affect the value of assets or the 
costs of doing business.  
The IIM data collection and analysis on sectoral and NatCat exposures therefore supports the 
broader objective of the GME: assessing global insurance market trends and developments and 
detecting the possible build-up of systemic risk in the global insurance sector.
