Executive Summary 
Equity market structures have continued to evolve in recent years, with notable 
shifts in trading activity across the different phases of the trading day. In many 
jurisdictions there has been an increase in the share of total daily trading value 
executed during end-of-day auctions1.  
This Consultation Report presents IOSCO’s observations of recent trends 
based on a comprehensive stocktake primarily informed by a survey of IOSCO 
ordinary members and, where appropriate, supplemented by engagement with 
equity trading venues. The Consultation Report examines global market 
dynamics and changing liquidity profiles throughout the trading day, the 
potential challenges and risks associated with these developments, and the 
regulatory and supervisory approaches adopted across jurisdictions. It als o 
outlines a set of proposed good practices aimed at trading venues and/or 
regulators, as well as consultation questions intended to inform potential future 
IOSCO work in this area. 
In most jurisdictions equity trading venues have in place end -of-day auction 
mechanisms, with the  share of liquidity on these  varying greatly across 
jurisdictions. Between 2020 and 2025 , the value traded at closing auctions 
generally increased, with equity market liquidity increasingly concentrating at 
the end of the day. Furthermore, in many jurisdictions there is an upward trend 
in mechanisms offering investors execution at the closing price without routing 
trading through the closing auction. IOSCO also found that equity derivative 
markets generally exhibit liquidity and volatility characteristics similar to those 
of their underlying equity markets. 
Changes in liquidity distribution throughout the trading day may create 
potential risks and challenges including, for example, risks to operational 
resilience, market manipulation, and fair and orderly market conditions.  
In particular, high-liquidity trading periods and intra-day liquidity variation may 
pose potential challenges to trading venues’ operational resilience, cyber 
security, and ability to ensure fair and orderly market conditions. Where a 
closing auction cannot be run on the scheduled time –  for example, because 
of a market outage –  this may also present challenges when establishing 
closing prices. To address these risks, many jurisdictions highlight the

Chapter 1. Introduction 
1.1 Background 
Equity market structures have continued to evolve in recent years, with notable 
shifts in trading activity across the different phases of the trading day. In many 
jurisdictions there has been an increase in the share of total daily trading value 
executed during end-of-day auctions. 
This development appears to be influenced by  a combination of interrelated 
factors affecting trading behaviour and liquidity dynamics, which are discussed 
in further details below. 
These evolving market dynamics present an opportunity to evaluate impacts 
on liquidity during continuous trading  sessions, and to assess potential 
challenges such as market manipulation risks (such as “marking the close”), and 
operational resilience pressures during concentrated trading windows. 
Given these developments, and their potential implications for market integrity, 
operational resilience and investor protection, IOSCO initiated work in March 
2025 to examine this trend. 
1.2 Objective of this Consultation Report 
Consistent with the mandate approved by the IOSCO Board, this Consultation 
Report presents IOSCO’s findings on: 
• how liquidity is distributed throughout the trading day in equity markets 
globally, including the increasing concentration of trading at the end of 
the trading day observed  in some juris dictions, and the resulting 
implications for market dynamic and liquidity profiles; 
 
• the potential risks and challenges  associated with evolving liquidity 
patterns and closing auction designs, including operational and market 
manipulation risks – such as “marking the close”; and 
 
•  the regulatory and supervisory approaches employed by regulators to 
respond to these developments.

Chapter 2. Market Dynamics 
and Liquidity Proﬁles 
This chapter outlines key findings from the IOSCO survey responses on how 
end-of-day auction mechanisms operate, the relative share of liquidity in end-
of-day auctions, as well as the importance and implications of end -of-day 
auctions in different jurisdictions3.  
It also considers the interconnections between equity and equity derivative 
markets.  
Finally, it identifies alternative ways in which investors may access the closing 
price, beyond the end-of-day auction. These include ‘post-close sessions’ and 
other mechanisms by which investors are guaranteed that their order will  be 
executed at the closing price. 
2.1 The prevalence of closing auctions  
Almost all surveyed regulators reported that at least some equity trading 
venues in their jurisdictions operate end-of-day auction mechanisms. The only 
jurisdiction that do es not currently have such  a mechanism said that the 
regulator is reviewing the possibility of introducing one. 
The operation of end-of-day auction mechanisms 
Timing 
In terms of the timing of end-of-day auction mechanisms, most trading venues 
reported to have an order input or call period where orders are entered, may 
be amended and collectively contribute to the indicative closing price. 
For some trading venues, the order input period runs parallel to the continuous 
trading session, whereas for others , it commences after the end of the 
continuous session. The indicative closing price is the price at which orders 
would execute if the auction ended at that moment. In terms of pre -trade

transparency, the indicative closing price is disseminated to all auction

The order input period is followed by the execution of orders (i.e. buy and sell 
orders in the auction ‘ uncross’ or are matched) at the closing price. Typically, 
the closing price is generated using an uncrossing algorithm, as explained in 
the section below.  This is different from the last traded price during the 
continuous trading session. 
In many jurisdictions, the exact time of this closing period is randomised within 
a specified interval, to avoid market participants knowing exactly when trades 
will be ultimately executed and, thus, reduce the potential for manipulation of 
the closing price.  
Certain trading venues also have additional phases during the end -of-day 
auction mechanism. For instance, the order input period may be preceded by 
a period where a reference price for the closing auction is set, which sets a 
range for the closing price. Trading venues may also have a no-cancellation or 
pre-closing adjustment period following the order input period but before 
orders are executed. During such periods, auction participants can still enter 
orders, but generally they cannot cancel or deactivate pending orders or make 
certain types of adjustments. 
On many trading venues, the order input period may also be extended, prior to 
the execution of orders, in certain circumstances. These circumstances include 
if the closing price is outside a set range (to ensure orderly trading conditions) 
or if uncrossing orders at the closing price would leave unexecuted market 
orders. The duration of these extensions is often randomised.   
Once orders are executed, trading venues in some jurisdictions also have a 
short – typically 5 to 10 minutes – post-close trading session, when trades may 
execute at the closing price only.

Closing price determination 
The closing price is generally determined by an algorithm as the price at which 
there is the maximum tradeable volume. Algorithms may also seek to minimise 
the imbalance between executed buy and sell orders, the variation of the 
closing price from a reference price (i.e. the last traded price) and/or the best

price (i.e. the highest bid and lowest ask). In certain jurisdictions, the algorithm 
is set so that all market orders and limit orders that are more competitive than 
the closing price are executed. Some trading venues have designated 
members, such as designated market makers responsible for running the end-
of-day auction.  
Where a closing price cannot be determined as above (for instance, if there 
are no trades in the closing auction), trading venues have a variety of fallback 
options – including using the last traded price during the continuous trading 
session as the closing price.  
Order types  
Trading venues have a range of permitted order types for end-of-day auctions.  
Generally, trading venues permit both market and limit orders to be entered in 
the end-of-day auction. Some trading venues also permit iceberg (i.e. partially 
hidden) orders and pegged orders, while others prohibit these order types and 
orders with a minimum fill requirement.  
On some trading venues, market and limit orders calibrated for execution 
during the end -of-day auction may be also entered during the earlier 
continuous trading sessions. This includes market orders to execute at the 
closing price (on close orders) and limit orders that execute at the closing price, 
but only if that is equal to or better than a specified limit price (limit on close 
orders). Some trading venues also allow imbalance orders, which can only be 
entered, if there is an imbalance between bids and offers in the end -of-day 
auction.  
In addition, certain trading venues also allow stop orders to be entered during 
the call period of the end -of-day auction, but they do not contribute to the 
formation of the closing price. Instead, if the market price hits the stop price, 
the stop order is then entered into the post-close session for execution.  
Execution priority rules 
A common theme reported across jurisdictions is that trading venues prioritise 
orders by price and then time. Accordingly, market orders (which will execute 
at any closing price) are generally prioritised over limit orders.  
In some jurisdictions, unexecuted limit orders submitted during continuous 
trading sessions are automatically entered into the end-of-day auction, where 
they benefit from higher time priority. Some trading venues specify that, in case 
of an order modification (unless it is purely a decrease in order value), a new 
timestamp for determining order prioritisation is applied.

Some trading venues also use other factors to determine order priority –  
including whether a participant has orders that can internally cross, and 
whether orders are visible in the order book or are iceberg orders.  
On some trading venues, unmatched orders during  the end -of-day auction 
may be entered into the post-close trading session, and for one trading venue, 
this constitutes an automatic process for all order types.  
Other trading venues noted that only unmatched market orders, or orders that 
are ‘opted in’, are entered into the post-close period.  
Moreover, on certain trading venues, some types of unmatched orders may 
also be transferred to the next trading day.  
Auction participants  
A wide range of participants take part in  closing auctions. In most cases, all 
members of a trading venue may participate, including market participants that 
service both domestic and international investors. In o ne jurisdiction, certain 
international investors can participate in the end-of-day auction but are only 
permitted to enter at-auction limit orders.

The importance and implications of end-of-day auctions 
The IOSCO survey sought data on the proportion of trades occurring on end-
of-day auctions, with reference to the number of trades executed and the value 
traded (defined as the quantity multiplied by the price)  during end -of-day 
auctions in equity markets, compared to the rest of the trading day. The survey 
asked jurisdiction-level estimates between 2020 and 2025. To supplement this 
data, the survey also asked how any observed trends in end -of-day auctions 
impacted on shares’ overall liquidity and volatility. 
Share of liquidity on end-of-day auctions  
Survey responses indicated that the share of liquidity on end-of-day auctions 
varied greatly across different jurisdictions (and, in some cases, within a specific 
region) between 2020 and 2025. 
Measured as the value traded in end -of-day auctions relative to total daily 
trading, the reported proportions ranged from 6.5% to over 45% in 2025. 
However, when considering only the number of transactions executed , these 
ratios are almost always lower.  
In general, the value traded in end -of-day auctions is highest in some 
European jurisdictions. It is also significant in North American jurisdictions and 
Australia. However, few European regulators did not report significant volumes 
traded in end-of-day auctions. 
Between 2020 and 2025, the share of closing auctions generally increased (in 
terms of value traded), even  in those jurisdictions where this share is not 
significant. In certain jurisdictions (notably in Europe), the proportion of the 
amount executed during the end-of-day closing auction relative to total daily 
trading value has risen by as much as 10%. 
However, this trend was not universal. In a small number of jurisdictions, the 
share of trading conducted during closing auctions declined slightly — by a few 
percentage points —  since 2020. This downward trend is only observed in 
markets where closing auc tions represented a relatively small share of total 
trading activity.

3.2.1 Time-of-Day Manipulation Trends  
Most regulators did not report a systematic increase or decrease in 
manipulation linked to liquidity changes. However, responses reveal distinct 
patterns associated with specific phases of the trading day. 
During low- liquidity periods, such as intra-day lulls, reduced market depth 
creates opportunities for manipulative practices like cornering or squeezing,

36 
 
  
where traders acquire dominant positions to restrict supply and drive prices 
higher. Illiquidity was repeatedly cited as a significant enabler of manipulation, 
including strategies aimed at gaining control of freely tradable shares over 
multiple days, driving retail interest, and coordinating sales at inflated prices. 
In high-liquidity periods, manipulators may employ more complex strategies, 
including spoofing, layering, and momentum ignition. These techniques exploit 
deep order books and active trading to create false signals or trigger rapid 
price movements, often du ring continuous trading sessions rather than 
auctions. 
During auction phases —  pre-opening and closing sessions — marking the 
prices (strategic order placement) was noted as a concern. This involves 
submitting orders intended to influence the equilibrium price without genuine 
trading intent, creating an artificial impression of supply or demand. Regulators 
noted that robust auction design features mitigate this risk. SC Malaysia 
reported a decline in such activity after introducing last price limits during end-
of-day auctions and trading -at-last sessions. These  limits impose real-time 
price boundaries, rejecting or halting orders that would push indicative prices 
beyond predefined thresholds. In Europe, some trading venues put in place a 
random-end auction mechanisms as effective safeguards. In addition, German 
markets put in place  random-end periods at market close  to reduce 
susceptibility for manipulation. 
Cross-market manipulation risks were also highlighted, particularly between 
equities and related derivatives. When stock trading pauses during auction

As differences in the approaches to managing excessive volatility reflect

In accordance with the applicable regulatory frameworks, t rading venues 
implement VCMs to maintain orderly markets and ensure fair price formation. 
These mechanisms typically involve dynamic and static price thresholds, 
volatility interruptions or pauses, and auction -based processes, whereby a 
pause or interruption triggers a call or volatility auction during which orders are 
collected, and the price is re -established before trading resumes. While the 
core principles are substantially aligned, implementatio n details vary across 
jurisdictions.

thresholds. Collars are calibrated as both dynamic (based on last traded price) 
and static measures (based on reference price). Breaches of the volatility

exchanges in the US are required by the Limit  Up Limit Down ( LULD) Plan

trading venues.

Chapter 4. Supervisory and 
Regulatory Approaches 
Considering the responses to the survey, regulators worldwide have reinforced 
their commitment to financial stability, market integrity and operational 
resilience through measures that emphasise proactive supervision, 
collaboration, and technological robustness. Supervisory approaches adopted 
in the jurisdictions covered by the surveyed regulators incorporate enhanced 
market integrity rules, resilience testing, and contingency planning. Liquidity 
monitoring and thematic reviews are integrated into regulatory frameworks, to 
ensure that trading venues allocate sufficient resources for surveillance and 
fair and orderly trading. 
Collaboration and coordinated oversight have become central to risk 
management strategies. Regulators work closely with trading venues, clearing 
agencies, and other stakeholders through dedicated committees and periodic 
engagements. These forums enable ear ly risk detection, rapid response, and 
systemic risk mitigation. Cross-border coordination is also strengthened 
through real-time data sharing and joint responses to emerging risks. 
Market resilience is supported by mechanisms such as VCMs, circuit breakers, 
and price collars, widely implemented under global standards and national 
rules. Current supervisory expectations, in light of the requirements set out 
within various jurisdictions’ regulatory frameworks include comprehensive 
business continuity planning, annual resilience testing, and stress response 
protocols to ensure uninterrupted trading during periods of market stress. 
Technology and cyber risk management are addressed through robust 
information and communication technology ( ICT) risk frameworks, incident 
detection, and cybersecurity controls, according to survey responses. 
Regulatory requirements mandate system capacity reviews, data security 
measures, and continuous improvement following major outages. These efforts 
aim to safeg uard critical infrastructure and maintain confidence in market 
operations. 
Cross-market surveillance and manipulation prevention remain priorities. 
Regulators actively monitor for market manipulation, large short positions, and 
irregular buildups across markets.  In some jurisdictions  covered by the 
surveyed regulators, trading venues are required to self -assess compliance 
with algorithmic trading safeguards and maintain real -time monitoring 
capabilities to ensure orderly markets.

Regulators broadly confirmed that market liquidity profiles form part of their 
supervisory discussions with trading venues, reflecting the relevance of 
liquidity as a core indicator of market quality and resilience. Supervisory 
engagement varies in depth, ranging from high -level monitoring to detailed 
analysis of intraday patterns, auction dynamics, and concentration risks. In 
some cases, regulators review monthly reports and historical data to identify 
outliers, assess order book resilience under stress, a nd detect structural 
changes that could affect liquidity. 
Metrics and analytical approaches are central to these discussions. Liquidity 
assessments often include turnover shifts, closing auction reliability, and the 
impact of post -close sessions, alongside statistical benchmarks used to 
calibrate alerts and circu it breakers. These methodologies aim to capture 
evolving market conditions and ensure that safeguards remain effective. In 
certain frameworks, trading venues are required to document calibration 
processes for volatility controls, incorporating liquidity and volatility profiles as 
key parameters. 
The policy dimension is evident where regulators link liquidity monitoring to 
broader market structure reforms and consultations. Supervisory dialogues 
frequently address how proposed interventions might influence liquidity 
distribution and trading behaviour, with insights feeding into policy papers and 
thematic reviews. This integration of supervisory intelligence and policy 
development underscores a commitment to maintaining fair and efficient 
markets. 
Risk-based approaches underpin these practices, with liquidity profiles serving 
as indicators for potential misconduct or systemic vulnerabilities. In some  
jurisdictions covered by the surveyed regulators , surveillance systems 
dynamically adjust alert thresholds based on liquidity changes, while cross -
asset monitoring ensures that liquidity-related risks are considered alongside 
other market integrity concerns. These measures are complemented by 
regulatory frameworks that mandate resilience testing, circuit breaker 
calibration, and continuous oversight of liquidity trends. 
In light of the various jurisdictions’ regulatory requirements and supervisory 
experience described above, as well as in compliance with the 
Recommendations contained in the 2018 IOSCO Report on Volatility Control 
Mechanisms, it could be useful to take advantage of the experience and tools 
adopted by regulators so far. 
Surveillance and market integrity measures remain central for business 
continuity, and more broadly to guarantee fair and orderly trading conditions, 
transparency, and efficiency. In some regulatory frameworks, trading venues are 
expected to deploy advance d surveillance systems with dynamic alerts

Technology and cyber risk management is another critical area. Under many 
regulatory frameworks, trading venues are required to implement strong ICT 
controls, information security measures, and cyber resilience frameworks,

Governance and accountability underpin all risk management practices. 
Broadly speaking, the responses highlighted a global trend toward integrated

Trading venues and regulators may consider

Operational Risk and Resilience 
2 Trading 
Venues  
Trading venues could consider implementing 
mechanisms to support fair and orderly trading that 
are suitable for all liquidity conditions. For example, 
this could include: 
• Developing business continuity and disaster 
recovery plans that account for the potential 
risks of high concentrations of liquidity. This 
could include, for example, performing  regular 
stress tests that simulate scenarios where 
trading volumes may be higher than peak 
volumes (i.e. end -of-day auctions and periods 
of high volatility) and implementing disaster

recovery measures involving targeted back -up 
systems.  
• Designing trading systems with sufficient 
capacity headroom and scalability. This could 
include, for example, implementing system 
capacity management tools.   
• Implementing robust cyber -security 
programmes – including within the business 
continuity framework – that consider any 
increased risks  during concentrated trading 
periods. This could include, for example, 
appropriate governance arrangements, systems 
access, data management and storage, as well 
as penetration and vulnerability testing. 
• Carrying out continuous capacity monitoring 
and planning  including, for example, real time 
monitoring of latency, throughput and system 
load.  
Market Manipulation Monitoring  
3 Trading 
Venues and 
Regulators 
Trading venues may consider designing risk-based 
and proportionate market surveillance frameworks 
that account for the risks posed by intraday 
variations in liquidity and differences across trading 
phases. This could include, if appropriate: 
• Incorporating intraday liquidity metrics such as 
volume, bid-ask spreads, and order book depth, 
into their market surveillance systems. This 
could help trading venues effectively calibrate 
their real-time alerts and market oversight for 
different trading periods. 
• During closing auctions and on index 
rebalancing or derivatives expiry days,  where 
possible, trading venues may consider the risk 
of cross-asset manipulation, especially between 
cash equity and related derivative markets. 
Where appropriate, trading venues and regulators 
may consider leveraging on technological 
developments and data -driven frameworks and 
tools, to help detect anomalies during periods of 
concentrated liquidity.
