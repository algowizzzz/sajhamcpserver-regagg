Executive Summary 
The ESG Indices as Benchmarks report (the Report) was produced by IOSCO’s 
Sustainable Finance Taskforce (STF) 1  and provides a comparative analysis 
between IOSCO’s Principles for Financial Benchmarks (PFBs) and ESG indices 
that are used as benchmarks. For the purpose of this report, ESG indices used 
as benchmarks , or “ESG benchmarks”,  are defined as: indices specifically 
constructed to reflect ESG factors according to its publicly disclosed 
methodology, and which is used as a reference for assessing ESG risk exposure 
or ESG impact. Similar to traditional financial benchmarks, ESG benchmarks are 
used to anchor  financial instruments, guid e capital allocation and  enable 
performance comparison, but their distinct feature is that the selection, 
weighting or exclusion of constituents is based on ESG data and 
methodologies in addition to financial metrics. Throughout the report the terms 
“ESG indices used as benchmarks” and “ESG benchmarks” will refer to the same 
concept and may be used interchangeably. 
The Report compares key characteristics and vulnerabilities of ESG indices 
relative to traditional financial benchmarks . This Report focuses on 
greenwashing vulnerabilities  as well as on existing market and regulatory 
initiatives aimed at addressing these vulnerabilities.  
The assessment is structured to focus on the core pillars of benchmarks, 
examining how the relevant PFBs apply and whether ESG benchmark 
administrators should consider any additional factors when embedding these 
pillars into benchmark design and administra tion to maintain transparency, 
consistency, and reliability. 
Specifically, the exercise addresses the four core pillars: 
• Governance and the Role and Responsibility of the Administrators : 
including overall responsibilities, conflict of interest and internal and 
third parties oversight functions. 
• Quality and Integrity of ESG Benchmarks 
• Methodology: including aspects around transparency, data inputs , 
verification of submissions, changes to methodology, and contingency

Definitions and key characteristics 
From a regulatory standpoint, both the EU 6  and the UK 7  Benchmark 
Regulations (BMR) and IOSCO’s PFB define benchmarks to include indices. The 
BMR broadly define an index as any publicly available calculated figure based 
on underlying assets, prices, or assessments.  
An index becomes a benchmark when it is used for reference purposes, such 
as determining the amount payable under a financial instrument or contract, 
the value of a financial instrument, or measuring the performance of an 
investment fund or portfolio against the benchmark.

Greenwashing risks in the context of ESG benchmarks 
ESMA’s Final Report “CSA on BMR ESG Disclosure ”(ESMA CSA) identifies several key areas where 
greenwashing risks may arise in the context of ESG Benchmarks. These areas include: 
The misleading use of ESG -related terms in benchmark names without corresponding ESG factors in the 
methodology. 
While the majority of the administrators in the scope of the CSA confirmed that there were no ESG references in 
the names or methodologies, two competent authorities (CAs) identified some ESG -related terms used in 
benchmarks names where administrators did not disclose ESG factors. Some of these ESG-related terms used in 
benchmarks names may be deemed as misleading, for example the use of the term ‘Green’ or ‘Climate 
change’ or ‘ESG’. For these benchmarks, administrators were requested to disclose the relevant ESG factors. 
Benchmarks presented as ESG despite only applying minimal exclusion criteria  
Some benchmarks only apply high-level exclusions criteria for the selection of the constituents, according to 
ESMA’s report these benchmarks should not be considered as ESG but may be labelled misleadingly. 
Inadequate transparency regarding low data coverage for key social indicators such as gender pay gap or 
labour due diligence. 
ESMA’s report identifies that the most common ESG factors with low data coverage of the constituents refer to 
social factors. Moreover, CAs reported that the calculation of the ESG factors when data was not available for 
specific constituents impaired the representativeness of the ESG factor compared to the benchmark. 
According to ESMA’s views, when administrators set up an ESG benchmark, they must do their best efforts to 
get the data required for the ESG calculation. While the practice of reweighting of the portfolio is needed to be 
able to calculate the factors when data for some constituents of the benchmark is not available, ESMA stresses 
the need for administrators to be more transparent on the calculation made. When the ESG factor calculation 
considers a subset of the constituents, users of benchmarks should be aw are of the percentage of the 
constituents taken into account.  
For that purpose, a good practice could be for administrators to enhance transparency and disclose the data 
coverage for all ESG factors calculated. In addition, when the data coverage is low, administrators could explain 
the reason behind such a low coverage. 
Inconsistent treatment of Scope 3 emissions, which undermines comparability and reliability. 
CAs have reported to ESMA that all administrators in the scope of the CSA exercise disclose the GHG intensity. 
However, the approach to Scope 3 emissions varies between administrators. 
The fact that Scope 3 emissions are not systematically reported as part of the GHG intensity of benchmarks 
undermines comparability across benchmarks, especially if the various Scopes are reported in an aggregate 
manner, with no visibility on the individual type of Scope.

Use and types of ESG indices as benchmarks 
Feedback from IOSCO’s AMCC highlights a dynamic and evolving landscape 
for the use of ESG indices as benchmarks. The responses illustrate both the 
growing importance and the nuanced use of ESG indices as benchmarks 
across regions and investment strategies. 
Among European asset managers, the use of benchmark indices is well 
established, with some firms focusing on specific types such as S&P Climate 
and Paris-Aligned Benchmark (PAB) indices. These ESG indices are specifically 
designed to address bespoke investment solutions and meet the distinct 
needs of various clients. They are applied across both equity and fixed income 
exposures. 
Asset managers typically employ ESG indices used as benchmarks in several 
contexts. Within active management, these indices serve as reference points 
for ESG-related active funds, helping to define investment universes and set 
performance objectives. In the passive investment space, ESG indices  used as 
benchmarks are commonly used as  reference benchmarks for ETFs and index 
funds.  
Across these approaches, asset managers reported using a range of 
benchmark types, including best -in-class indices tailored to different client 
needs, broad ESG benchmarks that maintain a low tracking error relative to 
parent indices, and stricter socially  responsible investing benchmarks that 
accept a higher tracking error in exchange for more rigorous ESG criteria.

2.1. Responsibility of the Administrator 
Principle 1, of the PFB, on Overall Responsibility of the Administrator , 
establishes that benchmark administrators must retain overall responsibility for 
all aspects of the benchmark determination process. This includes the 
development, issuance, and ongoing maintenance of benchmark 
methodologies, as well as implementation of governance and control measures 
that ensure benchmark integrity. 
In the ESG context, this overall responsibility becomes particularly critical. 
Administrators must navigate complex ESG -related issues such as the 
integration of qualitative data, forward -looking estimates, and evolving 
sustainability standards. They must ensure that the methodology aligns with 
the benchmark’s stated sustainability objectives and remains fit for purpose 
over time.  
Control frameworks help ensure that the administrator’s internal policies and 
processes safeguard benchmark integrity. Some jurisdictions have highlighted 
the importance of establishing verification mechanisms to detect outliers which 
might skew results, assess the plausibility of submissions, maintain audit trails, 
and request supporting documentatio n where necessary. These procedures 
are especially critical in protecting benchmarks from manipulation or error 
when data provision is voluntary. 
An a ppropriate control framework for the process of determining and 
distributing the benchmark is necessary in the context of ESG Benchmarks, as 
is the case for all financial benchmarks . Principle 4 of the PFBs,  on Control 
Framework for Administrators, outlines that the control framework should be 
appropriately tailored to the materiality of the potential or existing conflicts of 
interest, the extent of the use of discretion in the benchmark setting process 
and to the nature of benchmark inputs and outputs.  The control framework 
should be documented and available to relevant regulatory authorities, if any. 
The control framework should also be reviewed periodically and updated as 
appropriate. Principle 4 requires the framework to address several areas 
including conflicts of interest, integrity and quality of benchmark determination, 
whistleblowing mechanism, and expertise.  
Some jurisdictions have introduced specific expectations or regulatory 
requirements in this regard. Thailand, for instance, places strong emphasis on 
the credibility of ESG benchmark administrators by evaluating their reputation

performance in delivering ESG scores, ratings,11 or benchmarks.

2.3. Internal Oversight Function 
The oversight function plays a central role in reinforcing the accountability and 
credibility of ESG benchmarks. Unlike traditional benchmarks where oversight 
is primarily concerned with consistency and data integrity, ESG benchmarks 
require broader scrutiny due to the interpretive nature of ESG indicators and 
the ongoing evolution of ESG expectations. Oversight functions must ensure 
that the benchmark’s methodology, including any ESG -related criteria, is 
applied consistently as defined; that conflicts of i nterest and input data are 
properly managed; and that changes and decision -making processes are 
subject to transparent and periodic review. 
Across jurisdictions, the design and formalisation of oversight functions vary. 
Some mandate oversight structures that are functionally independent from 
day-to-day operations. These oversight bodies are responsible for supervising 
benchmark governance, including methodology, policy review, and compliance 
processes (e.g., EU). In some cases, administrators are also required to maintain 
comprehensive internal control systems, conduct periodic compliance reviews, 
and establish committees that oversee the adequ acy of methodologies and 
internal procedures (e.g., Thailand).  
Other jurisdictions have proposed or encouraged the formation of oversight 
bodies that are independent and capable of reviewing discretionary ESG 
methodologies, especially in response to stakeholder concerns or significant 
market developments (e.g., Colomb ia). These bodies may include external 
members or advisory panels with expertise in sustainability, to enhance 
credibility and reduce potential bias in applying normative ESG judgements. In 
this context, valuable lessons can be drawn from the regulation of ESG ratings

2.4. Oversight of Third Parties 
Principle 2 of the PFB, on  Oversight of Third Parties, requires benchmark 
administrators to establish robust oversight over third parties that contribute 
data or services essential to benchmark construction. This oversight must be 
sufficient to ensure that inputs are accurate, consistent with the benchmark's 
objectives, and that the third party has appropriate governance. This Principle 
applies only where activities relating to the benchmark determination process 
are undertaken by third parties, for example with respect to collection of inputs, 
or where a third party acts as the calculation agent or publisher of the 
benchmark. 
In ESG benchmarks, third-party data providers - including ESG rating agencies 
and consultancy groups - play a substantial role in supplying non -financial 
information. These sources are often less transparent and lack standardised 
methodologies, increasing the risk of inconsistent or biased inputs. 
Administrators must define and document their criteria for engaging third -
party providers. To be in adherence with Principle 2, oversight mechanisms 
should ensure that third-party inputs are not only methodologically sound but 
also aligned with the ESG characteristics promoted by the benchmark.

Chapter 5 – Accountability 
Ensuring robust accountability mechanisms in the design, implementation and 
oversight of ESG benchmarks is essential. Accountability provides the 
foundation for credible benchmark use, enabling users, supervisors, and other 
stakeholders to assess whether ESG benchmarks are constructed and applied 
in a fair, consistent and transparent manner.  
Compared to traditional financial benchmarks, ESG benchmarks raise specific 
accountability challenges stemming from their reliance on non -financial and 
often subjective criteria. These elements complicate the detection of errors, 
the attribution of responsibility for inconsistencies, and the implementation of 
effective remediation when issues arise. 
Currently, many accountability safeguards applied to ESG benchmarks are 
inherited from frameworks designed for financial benchmarks. These 
mechanisms, covering complaint handling, audits, documentation, and 
supervisory cooperation, were not developed with the specific complexities of 
ESG in mind. As a result, they often fail to ensure visibility over how ESG-related 
decisions are taken, how methodologies are adjusted over time, and how issues 
are escalated or resolved. 
5.1.  Complaints Mechanisms and Escalation Channels 
Accessible and reliable complaints mechanisms are a key element. They 
provide users and stakeholders with a formal channel to raise concerns, 
challenge methodologies or outputs, and contribute to continuous 
improvement. Complaints mechanisms can also help identify potential 
misalignments with disclosed practices, especially given the evolving nature of 
ESG methodologies. 
Complaints may relate to controversial inclusion or exclusion decisions, alleged 
greenwashing, or inconsistencies between the published ESG methodology 
and its application. However, the mechanisms available to raise and resolve 
such complaints are generally underdeveloped or not tailored to these ESG -
specific issues. 
Under the EU  BMR and similar frameworks (e.g., UK BMR), administrators are 
required to implement complaints -handling procedures (e.g., Article 9 BMR), 
but these provisions are designed with traditional financial benchmarks in mind. 
They offer general safeguards, such as i ndependence of the reviewer, timely

and rectify the issue.

5.3.  Traceability of ESG Decisions and Methodology 
Audit trails are essential for ensuring accountability and transparency in ESG 
benchmarks. They play a crucial role in reinforcing replicability and enabling 
supervisory scrutiny of benchmark determinations. Unlike traditional financial 
benchmarks, ESG benchmarks rely heavily on qualitative, forward-looking, and 
often subjective data. This makes it more difficult to trace how inclusion or 
exclusion decisions are made, how ESG scores are constructed, or how 
methodologies are applied. However, survey response s and stakeholder 
feedback indicate that record -keeping practices for ESG benchmarks are 
uneven and often insufficient. In many cases, documentation is only retained 
for traditional financial inputs, with limited traceability of ESG -specific 
judgments, data transformations, or methodological overrides.  
Under traditional financial benchmark frameworks, such as the EU Benchmark 
Regulation (Articles 7 and 8), administrators are required to maintain detailed 
records of benchmark determinations, including input data, methodologies, 
discretionary decisions, an d communications with submitters. These 
requirements are intended to ensure replicability and enable effective 
supervision and audit. However, while these general frameworks ensure robust 
record-keeping principles, they do not specifically address the complexities of 
ESG benchmarks. 
In contrast, regulatory efforts targeting ESG Ratings regulation offer more 
tailored guidance. For instance, Regulation (EU) 2024/3005  on ESG ratings 
includes record-keeping obligations in Article 18, requiring documentation of 
internal and external communications, data sources, methodological decisions, 
and any changes or deviations from standard practices. While this regulation 
does not apply to ESG benchmarks, it may serve as a useful reference for 
designing ESG-specific audit trail requirements for benchmark administrators.  
Under Principle 18 of the PFB, on Audit Trail, benchmark administrators should 
retain records of relevant inputs, calculations, and decisions to enable 
independent review and verification of the benchmark determination. While

5.4.  Fragmented and Non-Speciﬁc Regulatory 
Cooperation Frameworks 
Because ESG benchmarks often rely on methodologies that incorporate non-
financial, subjective, and evolving standards, regulatory engagement is key to 
ensure credibility, oversight, and resilience of ESG benchmarks, particularly in 
areas such as data integ rity, classification decisions, and methodological 
transparency. 
Robust cooperation frameworks between benchmark administrators and 
supervisors could contribute  to ensure proper oversight of how ESG 
methodologies are developed, implemented and updated over time. This is 
particularly relevant given the pace of innovation in ESG products and the 
increasing reliance on data and methodologies.  However, ESG benchmarks 
often fall under the same generic frameworks, without additional mechanisms 
tailored to their ESG- specific risks. Most jurisdictions have not introduced 
targeted supervisory cooperation arrangements for ESG benchmarks.  
For instance, under frameworks like the EU BMR (Articles 7 and 8), competent 
authorities are granted access to documentation, audit results, and internal 
reviews, enabling them to supervise data contributions, methodological 
compliance, and market conduct. These mechanisms support regular oversight, 
with clear documentation and record-keeping obligations for administrators. 
Regulatory authorities typically do not require the submission of ESG 
methodologies, nor do they systematically monitor the implementation of ESG-
related changes. Several respondents identified barriers to accessing 
benchmark data, citing proprietary ESG m ethodologies and a lack of 
standardisation as key challenges.

46 
 
An exception is the EU’s CSA  launched by ESMA in 202 3, focused on ESG 
benchmark disclosures. This initiative reflects growing regulatory interest in 
monitoring ESG benchmark methodologies and consistency with public 
statements, marking an important step toward deeper regulatory cooperation. 
In the absence of explicit requirements or reporting duties for ESG benchmark 
administrators, regulatory cooperation remains ad hoc and fragmented. To 
close this gap, jurisdictions could draw inspiration from Regulation (EU) 
2024/3005, which mandates ESG rating providers to grant supervisors access 
to decision -making records and methodologies. Similar provisions for ESG 
benchmarks would ensure that supervisory frameworks keep pace with market 
evolution. Some authorities have also suggested fostering cross -border 
interoperability to improve supervisory coordination in the absence of binding 
global standards. 
Under Principle 19 of the PFB, on Cooperation with Regulatory Authorities, 
administrators should have procedures in place to cooperate with relevant 
authorities and share information on material developments. While this 
principle is broadly applicable, it does not specifically address the unique 
supervisory challenges posed by ESG benchmarks, such as evolving 
methodologies, reliance on third- party ESG data, or subjective inclusion 
criteria. As such, its effective implementation may require additional 
clarifications or adaptations in the ESG context. 
Overall, ESG benchmarks currently lack dedicated frameworks for regulatory 
cooperation. There are no standardized mechanisms to facilitate the regular 
exchange of information, methodological oversight, or coordinated supervision 
across jurisdictions. This limits regulatory visibility and weakens the integrity of 
ESG benchmarks as tools for investment decisions and sustainability goals. As 
ESG benchmarks gain importance, supervisors may need clear guidance on 
information-sharing procedures, periodic engageme nt with benchmark 
administrators, and protocols for reviewing ESG methodologies and updates.  
Regulators might consider adjusting their frameworks to ensure that 
supervisory cooperation is proportionate to the specific risks posed by ESG 
methodologies and data frameworks.

Principle 16. 
Complaints 
Procedures. 
 
ESG benchmarks introduce 
additional complexities 
around qualitative data, 
value judgements and 
evolving standards that 
require more transparent 
mechanisms. 
Additionally, there is a high 
asymmetry of 
responsibilities and 
reputational risk between 
benchmark users and 
administrators. 
ESG benchmark 
administrators could 
consider, reflecting the 
specificities of ESG 
benchmarks, to allow 
stakeholders to raise 
concerns about the quality 
or appropriateness of 
forward-looking indicators, 
clarify how disputes about 
changing ESG 
methodologies or criteria will 
be handled, and specify how 
issues related to third-party 
data accuracy, bias, or 
conflicts of interest can be 
addressed. 
Principle 17. Internal 
Controls and Audit. 
 
Due to the reliance of ESG 
benchmarks on non-
financial, forward-looking 
and often qualitative or 
non-standardised data, 
there is greater room for 
discretion and 
interpretation, increasing 
the potential for 
methodological divergence, 
lack of consistency, or 
greenwashing. 
ESG benchmark 
administrators could 
consider implementing 
internal controls and audit 
protocols that explicitly take 
into account the complexity 
and subjectivity of ESG data, 
with an emphasis on 
methodological consistency. 
Principle 18. Audit Trail. 
 
Record-keeping practices 
for ESG benchmarks are 
uneven and often 
insufficient. In many cases, 
documentation is only 
retained for traditional 
financial inputs, with limited 
traceability of ESG-specific 
judgments, data 
transformations, or 
methodological overrides. 
ESG benchmark 
administrators could 
consider addressing 
traceability of ESG-specific 
practices, such as alignment 
with external taxonomies and 
sustainability standards.

for reviewing ESG
