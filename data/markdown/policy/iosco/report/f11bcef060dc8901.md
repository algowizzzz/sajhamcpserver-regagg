1. Executive Summary 
This report examines the global single -name credit default swaps market in 
the context of recent events that raised concerns relating to its limited 
transparency and liquidity. The report assesses current levels of post -trade 
transparency in member jurisdictions, explores potential measures to 
encourage greater post -trade transparency, and considers the advantages 
and disadvantages of these measures. 
A credit default swap (“ CDS”) is a type of derivative that transfers credit risk 
relating to a reference entity or instrument from the buyer of the CDS contract 
to the seller of the CDS contract. CDS contracts are used for hedging, 
speculation, credit exposure, and arbitrage and are p rimarily entered into by 
dealers, non- dealer banks, securities firms and hedge funds. The global 
single-name CDS market is illiquid ; as a result, CDS spreads are sensitive to 
market activity in a reference entity. 
The report provides a detailed description of the different post -trade 
transparency requirements for single-name CDS transactions as implemented 
in the EU, UK and U.S. Other surveyed jurisdictions, including Australia, 
Canada, Hong Kong, Japan, Singapore and Switzerland, indicated that they 
do not have post -trade transparency requirements for single -name CDS 
contracts (excluding publication of aggregated data). The report examines the 
impact of the U.S. transparency requirements and concludes that there is no 
indication that they had a negative impact on liquidity, with trade frequency 
continuing to fluctuate according to credit market conditions. 
The report identifies potential benefits with respect to increasing post -trade 
transparency in the single-name CDS market, including reducing information 
asymmetries and implicit transaction costs, promoting greater price efficiency, 
encouraging market participation, thereby increasing liquidity and 
competition, reducing regulatory arbitrage and market fragmentation, and 
providing more comprehensive information to make trading and valuation 
determinations. The report also identifies several  potential negative impacts 
of increasing post- trade transparency, such as increasing the costs of 
hedging, notably in less liquid markets that may not be suited to benefit from 
increased post- trade transparency. The report notes that while these 
potential costs could be mitigated by implementing appropriate exemptions 
and other measures, other potential negative impacts  could include 
exacerbating market volatility, increasing opportunities for market 
manipulation, and impacting risk management practices and access to 
financing. 
After reviewing market events impacting the banking industry in March 2023, 
the report concludes that the response to these events was an increase in 
activity in the CDS market, as participants sought to hedge their portfolios 
amid a climate of uncertainty and market stress. The increase in the level of 
activity in a market that generally has low levels of liquidity likely led to a rise

5 
in CDS spreads of banks. The increase in CDS spreads, coupled with the 
decline in equity prices, served as indicators of heightened stress.  The report 
does not find evidence of causation between sharp movements in the prices 
of single-name CDS and the subsequent sudden drop in the shares of certain 
banks at the time. 
The International Organization of Securities Commissions ( “IOSCO”) 
concludes the report by encouraging each member jurisdiction to take steps 
toward enhancing post-trade transparency in the single-name CDS market in 
its jurisdiction should they conclude that such efforts would not have a 
substantial negative effect on market risk exposure or market activity. IOSCO 
reminds jurisdictions of their ability to continue to address potential 
suspicious activity in the single -name CDS markets through securities law  
enforcement regimes.

2. Introduction 
2.1. Mandate 
Improving the functioning of over -the-counter (“OTC”) derivatives markets, 
including through enhanced transparency, has been a priority of international 
regulatory bodies since the global financial crisis of 2007-2009. 1 In light of 
this objective, IOSCO has previously examined the functioning of the CDS 
market. In particular, in its 2015 report Post Trade Transparency in the Credit 
Default Swaps Market (the “2015 Report”), 2  IOSCO encouraged member 
jurisdictions to take steps towards enhancing post-trade transparency in their 
respective CDS market. 
Recent events have raised the question of whether certain characteristics of 
the global single -name CDS market, in particular, limited transparency and 
liquidity, could have detrimental effects on equity markets. 
In March 2023, against a backdrop of rising interest rates, uncertainty 
regarding the impact on the banking sector of a downturn in the commercial 
real estate market, and a number of large failures in the crypto markets, 
concerns regarding the health of a number of U.S. and European banks 
coalesced into a crisis of confidence for these institutions. Rapid withdrawals 
by depositors and investors from these banks led to intervention by regulators. 
Ultimately, three regional U.S. banks were shut down and liqui dated and one 
large European bank, Credit Suisse Group AG (“Credit Suisse”),  merged with 
its competitor, UBS Group AG (“UBS”). Additionally, Deutsche Bank AG 
(“Deutsche Bank”) experienced significant pressure with a considerable drop 
in both its equity and bond prices.

opaque, shallow and illiquid trading in the single -name CDS market was

3. CDS Market 
3.1. CDS Definition 
As discussed in the 2015 Report, a CDS is a type of derivative that transfers 
credit risk relating to a reference entity or instrument from the buyer of the 
CDS contract to the seller of the CDS contract.
5 The contract provides the 
buyer with credit protection if a specified credit event occurs. During the 
duration of the contract, the buyer makes periodic payments to compensate 
the seller for the transfer of credit risk.
6 The seller compensates the buyer if a 
credit event occurs with respect to the specified reference entity.7 
A credit event is a trigger that causes the buyer of protection to terminate and 
settle the contract. Some of the most common credit events include the 
bankruptcy of the reference entity, the reference entity’s failure to make 
payments, accelerated debt prepayment by the reference entity, government 
intervention, restructuring of an underlying loan, and a rating downgrade of the 
reference entity. These credit events determine whether the buyer of 
protection receives payments from the seller of protection, depending on the 
terms of the CDS contract. 
CDS contracts typically settle physically, in cash or through an auction, with the 
auction settlement being the most common method.
8 For physically settled 
contracts, the seller pays the buyer the par value of the reference credit 
instrument in exchange for the buyer delivering the reference credit instrument 
to the seller.9 For cash settled contracts, the seller pays the buyer the difference 
between the par value and the market value of the reference credit instrument.10 
For auction settled contracts, an auction is conducted by an auction 
administrator, which establishes a market price based on bids from participants.

3.2. Use Case of CDS 
CDS contracts were originally designed as a means for banks to transfer credit 
exposure to free up regulatory capital. 11 There are four broad use cases for 
CDS: hedging, speculation, credit exposure (bond alternative) and arbitrage.12  
Bondholders wary of deterioration in the financial condition of a corporate or 
sovereign bond could buy a CDS contract to hedge against the potential risk 
of a default. 
CDS contracts may also be used to speculate on the credit worthiness of a 
reference entity. An investor that believes an entity’s financial situation is 
deteriorating could buy a CDS without owning the underlying instrument. This is 
called a “naked” CDS. The investor benefits if there is a rise in the market value 
of the CDS that is over and  above the periodic premium payments. This 
practice is similar to short-selling and may have an adverse effect on the price 
of the underlying asset.
13 However, some argue that naked CDS result in more 
liquidity, which could promote pricing efficiency. The EU has imposed notice 
requirements and trading restrictions, including a ban in some circumstances, 
on naked sovereign CDS.14  
CDS contracts may also provide a synthetic alternative for buyside 
investors seeking credit exposures to certain reference entities, without the 
capital constraint involved in purchasing a bond. An investor could engineer a 
fixed income cash flow  profile through the sale of CDS contracts to buyers 
seeking protection. The periodic premium payment acts as an alternative to 
periodic coupon payments that would have been received if the investor had 
purchased a bond on the reference entity.  Both transactions involve the risk 
of the principal, as the CDS seller would compensate the CDS buyer in the 
case of a credit event for the difference between the par value and market 
value of the CDS. 
Finally, CDS contracts may also be used as an arbitrage opportunity. When an 
investor buys (or sells) the reference bond and at the same time buys (or sells) 
the CDS contract on the reference bond, the investor has the potential to earn 
a non -
 zero return on a default risk portfolio, generating arbitrage in the 
market. The return will be the spread between the bond coupon and the CDS 
premium.

3.3. Types of CDS 
CDS contracts can be broadly categorized as follows16: 
• standard CDS offer protection on senior or subordinate debt issued by 
corporations or sovereign entities; 
• loan CDS offer protection on leveraged loans issued by the reference 
entity; 
• municipal CDS offer protection on a reference entity that is a 
municipality, where the reference obligation is a municipal bond; and 
• asset-backed CDS offer protection on structured securities. 
The most common category of CDS is a standard CDS. In addition to standard 
single-name CDS, which is the focus of this report, there are standard multi -
name and index CDS.

3.4. CDS Market Participants 
The main types of market participants in the single -name CDS market are 
dealers, non-dealer banks, securities firms, and hedge funds. 
Central counterparties (“CCPs”) account for 59% of the GNO for single-name 
CDS. Reporting dealers represent 18% of the GNO for the single-name market. 
Non-dealer banks and securities firms represent 10% of the market. Hedge 
funds represent 4% of the single -name CDS GNO. CCPs would therefore 
appear to be the main node of concentration for CDS transactions, which 
reduce systemic, market and counterparty risk.27 
The top ten counterparties, including CCPs, represent between 27% -88% of 
the single-name CDS GNO for the members surveyed, with an average of 30% 
as of 29 December 2023. The top ten reference entities represent between 
2%-63% of the single -name CDS GNO for the responding jurisdictions, with 
an average of 5% as of 29 December 2023.28 
3.5. Single-Name CDS Liquidity 
The single-name CDS market is illiquid. Of the corporate names traded each 
quarter during the period between Q1 2018 and Q1 2023, less than 3% 
averaged more than 10 trades per day, according to the International Swaps 
and Derivatives Association (“ISDA”). In the same period, ISDA found that 1,169 
reference entities traded at least once in the same period. Of these 1,169 
reference entities, 496 reference entities traded at least once per quarter and 
represented 80% of market activity in the five -year period.

4. Legislative and Regulatory Framework 
for Post-Trade Transparency 
4.1. Post-Trade Transparency Since the 2015 Report 
The 2015 Report published by IOSCO  analyzed then- current post -trade 
transparency in the global CDS market, the potential benefits and costs of 
increased post-trade transparency, and potential ways to encourage greater 
post-trade transparency in member jurisdictions. The 2015 Report’s analysis was 
based on publicly available post-trade data on CDS transactions, relevant work 
of international bodies, academic literature, a survey of market participants and 
other market observers, and consideration of comments received on a 
consultation version of the 2015 Report. 
The term “post-trade transparency” in the 2015 Report, and as used in this report, 
refers to a regulatory system that mandates disclosure of information, widely 
accessible to the public, about the price and volume of each relevant transaction, 
but does not refer to regulatory structures that allow for voluntary or selective 
disclosure of data. Because post-trade transparency requires public dissemination 
of information about the price and volume of individual transactions, the term also 
does not apply to regulatory structures that require dissemination of data, however 
widely, only in an aggregate form. Because the information is for the benefit of 
market participants and the public generally, post -trade transparency does not 
entail disclosure of counterparty identity, which is a matter of regulatory concern.
30  
The 2015 Report concluded that greater post-trade transparency in the CDS 
market – including making the price and volume of individual transactions publicly 
available – would be valuable to market participants and observers. IOSCO, 
through the 2015 Report, encouraged each member jurisdiction to take steps 
toward enhancing post-trade transparency in the CDS market in its jurisdiction. 
IOSCO also  recognized that each member jurisdiction was best placed to 
determine the appropriate time and manner for enhancin g post- trade 
transparency for CDS that trade in its respective market.

4.3. Regulatory Consideration of Post-Trade Transparency 
The 2015 Report considered various potential benefits and costs associated with 
mandatory post-trade transparency in the CDS market. The potential benefits 
identified in the 2015 Report included reducing information asymmetries, 
promoting market confidence  and participation, increasing competition and 
liquidity provision, lowering trading costs, and lessening market fragmentation. 
The potential costs included reduced liquidity,  operational costs and 
considerations, increased market volatility and distressed  sales, particularly 
during times of market stress.
74  
In formulating this report’s consideration of the potential impacts of increasing 
post-trade transparency in the single -name CDS market, IOSCO  solicited its 
members’ views via the Survey, and a number of members provided feedback. 
Members’ responses echoed many of the considerations identified in the 2015 
Report. IOSCO also reviewed post -2015 research and academic literature to 
inform its consideration. While the literature identified is generally not focused on 
mandatory post- trade transparency for single -name CDS, it is nonetheless 
informative with respect to the potential benefits and costs of increasing post-
trade transparency in similar markets. 
Members’ responses to the Survey identified potential benefits with respect to 
increasing post-trade transparency in the single -name CDS market, including 
reducing information asymmetries and implicit transaction costs, promoting greater 
price efficiency, encouraging market participation, thereby increasing liquidity and 
competition, reducing regulatory arbitrage and market fragmentation,  and 
providing more comprehensive information to make trading and valuation 
determinations.

5.2. Market Input 
IOSCO held discussions with several market participants to understand the 
structure and characteristics of trading in the single -name CDS market and the 
liquidity crisis impacting the banking industry in March 2023. The discussion 
considered both large broker-dealers as liquidity providers of single -name CDS 
and alternative investment fund managers as users of CDS to initiate or hedge 
exposures in credit markets. 
March 2023 market events 
From the perspective of the liquidity providers to whom IOSCO spoke, it is difficult 
to determine if the changes in banks’ CDS spreads triggered the movements in 
other related trading instruments, such as equity and bond prices. The collapse 
of SVB and the loss of confidence in Credit Suisse led to a period of heightened 
volatility in the market and as a result, activity in hedging tools such as single -
name CDS increased. A weaker trend had already started in bonds and equities, 
which caused the spike in si ngle-name CDS on 24 March 2023. However, the 
relatively lower liquidity in the single -name CDS market compared to other 
financial instruments resulted in more pronounced price movements compared 
to bonds and equities. One liquidity provider, however, stated that the CDS 
spreads on Deutsche Bank at the time were tighter than the corresponding 
decreases in the value of Deutsche Bank equity and bond prices in the cash 
market.

providers and agreed that the market events in late March 2023 should be

SVB and the loss of confidence in Credit Suisse. The potential losses caused by 
these issues further increased the demand for hedging, particularly through single-

Trading single-name CDS 
According to the liquidity providers, the breadth of single-name CDS has reduced

increasing sensitivity to volatility. Liquidity providers stated that they use multiple 
sources of information to price single -name CDS, including market flows, 
movements in other related instruments and macro -economic factors. 
Information on trades captured during a period of market stress is usually treated 
with caution and supplemented with a broad er assessment of the market. 
According to one of the liquidity providers, the spike in single-name CDS spreads 
in March 2023 was preceded by pricing gaps observed in the inter-dealer pricing 
of lower-tiered Deutsche Bank bonds. 
According to the users of CDS, single-name CDS is an essential instrument for 
portfolio managers to hedge or gain exposure to certain sectors. Across the 
single-name CDS space, bank CDS are relatively more liquid and remain 
accessible especially during stress periods and their liquidity can sometimes 
surpass that of cash products, allowing investors to be more comfortable taking 
risks in banks. Without risk hedging tools like single -name CDS, portfolios would 
have to reformulate investment strategies and re allocate risk exposures, which 
would be very difficult in volatile periods. 
Transparency of single-name CDS 
Market makers are responsible for providing liquidity to the market, but as risk 
intermediaries they must consider their ability to hedge their risk in the market as 
well. In this context, the liquidity providers stated that increased transparency in 
single-name CDS could have an undesired effect on pricing and liquidity. During 
stress periods in particular, their ability to transfer risk could be limited if trade 
flows are made more transparent to the market. Moreover, one liquidity provider 
cautioned that real-time price (as opposed to volume) transparency could lead 
to market distortion. 
Although the liquidity providers agreed that market transparency is important, 
some stated that the degree of transparency should correspond to the liquidity 
in the market. In comparison to the equity market which has a wider breadth of 
investors, the sing le-name CDS market is mostly concentrated in hedging and 
risk mitigating activities and less liquid compared to the cash market. Therefore, 
most of the liquidity providers cautioned that the cost of real -time transparency 
could outweigh the benefits. Tradi ng illiquid products such as single -name CDS 
during stress periods could also trigger knock -on effects if it signals a surge in 
demand for hedging. One liquidity provider, however, stated that additional 
transparency would be beneficial and suggested real -time transparency for

5.3.3. CDS Spreads 
CDS spreads represent the price required by the seller of the CDS to bear the 
risk of the reference entity’s obligation. The CDS spread is usually expressed in 
basis points. For example, a CDS spread of 100 basis points (bp) corresponds to 
a 1% annual premium payment. 
In other words, the CDS buyer pays $1 to compensate the CDS seller for every 
$100 worth of the reference bonds they are exposed to per year. 
CDS spreads fluctuate continuously based on market forces, including bid and 
offer quotations from dealers. Additionally, CDS spreads can be used to calculate 
a firm’s probability of default.

5.3.5. Conclusion 
The response to the events in the European and U.S. banking system in early 
2023 was an increase in activity in the CDS market, as participants sought to 
hedge their portfolios amid a climate of uncertainty and market stress. The 
increase in the level of activity in a market that generally has low levels of liquidity 
likely led to a rise in CDS spreads of banks. The increase in CDS spreads, coupled 
with the decline in equity prices, served as indicators of heightened stress in the 
first quarter of 2023.

6. Conclusions 
In the 2015 Report, IOSCO encouraged member jurisdictions to take steps 
towards enhancing post- trade transparency in the CDS market, stating that 
“IOSCO believes that greater post -trade transparency in the CDS market—
including making the price and volume o f individual transactions publicly 
available—would be valuable to market participants and other market 
observers.”106 Since then, a limited number of jurisdictions (the EU, UK and U.S.) 
have implemented post -trade transparency requirements in their local CDS 
markets to varying degrees, and valuable information and data gathered by 
IOSCO members through the Survey and our analysis have enabled us to 
better understand certain particularities of the single-name CDS market. 
The data gathered through our work enabled IOSCO  to confirm that the 
single-name CDS market remains an illiquid market characterized by a limited 
number of intermediaries. Having a limited number of participating 
intermediaries in the single- name CDS space may also cause an information 
discrepancy between those involved in trades of single -name CDS and other 
market participants that are interpreting the post-trade information made 
publicly available in certain jurisdictions. 
In formulating this report’s recommendation and conclusion, IOSCO  also 
reviewed post -2015 research and academic literature to inform its 
consideration. The literature identified and reviewed was generally informative 
with respect to the potential benefits and costs of increasing post -trade 
transparency in the single -name CDS market. The academic literature and 
studies mostly provided support for several considerations such as reducing 
information asymmetries and implicit transaction costs and promoting greater 
price efficiency. 
To gather more perspective on the matter, some  IOSCO members held 
discussions with market participants to understand the market structure of 
single-name CDS and the market events in March 2023. While these market  
participants agreed that market transparency is important, they also stated 
that the degree of transparency should correspond to the liquidity of the CDS 
market. Among the users of CDS, the current transparency regime for single -
name CDS was considered to be sufficient for the healthy functioning o f the 
market. According to th e CDS users , the spike in single- name CDS that 
occurred in March 2023 was driven by short term market forces and not as a 
result of a lack in transparency (or of too much transparency). 
Based on the literature review, the analysis of the impact of U.S. SEC’s post-
trade transparency requirement for single -name CDS, and the discussions 
held with certain industry participants, there is no indication that post-trade

post-trade transparency after assessing the unique characteristics and needs 
of the ir relevant market. As we have seen through our analysis, post- trade 
transparency rules are not consistent across IOSCO member jurisdictions. 
Therefore, IOSCO encourages each member jurisdiction to take steps toward 
enhancing post -trade transparency in the single -name CDS market in its 
jurisdiction should they conclude that such efforts would not have a 
substantial negative effect on market risk exposure or market activity in their 
jurisdiction. IOSCO reminds jurisdictions of their ability to continue to address 
potential suspicious activity in the single -name CDS markets through 
securities law enforcement regimes.
