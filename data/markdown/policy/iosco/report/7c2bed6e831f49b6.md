Executive Summary 
This report presents the findings of IOSCO’s first SupTech Survey, conducted in early 2025 
and developed under FINMA’s leadership. With 49 authorities participating across all 
IOSCO regions, the survey provides a global snapshot of how technology is reshaping 
financial supervision. 
SupTech is no longer experimental. 
Across jurisdictions and market types, authorities are integrating SupTech into core 
supervisory functions. Efficiency, timeliness, and improved capabilities drive adoption, while 
artificial intelligence, data access, and cloud infrastructure are seen as critical enablers. At 
the same time, cyber risks and resource constraints remain significant hurdles. 
Strategies are forming, but implementation is uneven. 
Most authorities report being in a phase of partial implementation. Dedicated budgets and 
senior-level ownership are increasingly common, especially among small and emerging 
market regulators. Cooperation is broad but largely focused on sharing experiences rather 
than tools. 
Use cases are concentrated, but interest is expanding. 
Consumer protection and capital markets are the most developed areas of SupTech 
application. However, interest is shifting toward new domains such as digital assets, where 
usage remains limited but momentum is building. 
Technology adoption is pragmatic but aspirational. 
Authorities primarily use mid-level technologies, supported by transaction data and 
traditional fraud tools. High-tech ambitions remain strong, particularly for advanced 
analytics and machine learning, but funding gaps are slowing progress. 
Human capital strategies remain underdeveloped. 
Upskilling efforts rely heavily on online and ad hoc training. External hiring is prioritized over 
internal capability building, suggesting the need for more structured long-term workforce 
planning. 
A moment of convergence opens a window for deeper peer learning and shared 
progress.
 
The combination of growing strategic commitment, operational readiness, and shared 
challenges creates a strong case for enhanced international cooperation. The survey aims 
to support this effort by providing a baseline for benchmarking and future dialogue.

1. Background  
SupTech is rapidly becoming a defining feature of modern financial supervision, 
prompting regulators to reassess not only their toolkits but also their strategic posture. 
As the pace of innovation by market participants accelerates, supervisory authorities 
themselves are under increasing pressure to digitize their operations and modernize 
oversight processes to keep pace with market dynamics. In this context, SupTech—the 
application of technology to improve supervisory processes—has moved from 
experimentation to institutional priority. Recognizing this shift, IOSCO launched a strategic 
initiative to elevate the dialogue around SupTech to the board level. The aim is not a 
technical exchange but a strategic conversation on how to govern innovation, manage 
change, and strengthen international cooperation. 
Since its inception, the initiative has created space for informal, high-level engagement 
among IOSCO members and key international partners. 
At the IOSCO Annual Meeting in May 2024, SupTech was identified as a priority. Delegates 
highlighted the need to move beyond individual innovation efforts toward more integrated 
supervisory strategies. In November 2024, the dialogue continued at the IOSCO Board 
meeting in Madrid, where representatives from the BIS, IMF, and World Bank shared their 
perspectives. Across these discussions, it became clear that there is a demand for practical 
knowledge-sharing and strategic guidance. 
To address this gap, IOSCO launched its first SupTech Survey. 
Drawing on the FSB’s 2020 study on the Use of Supervisory and Regulatory Technology by 
Authorities and Regulated Institutions: Market developments and financial stability 
implications and the Cambridge SupTech Lab’s State of SupTech Report 2023, the 
questionnaire captured a range of institutional experiences, strategies, and challenges. The 
findings presented in this report offer a data-driven overview of current practices across 
jurisdictions and provide a basis for further discussion and collaboration. In doing so, they 
aim to support authorities in identifying priorities, tracking progress, and shaping their own 
approach to SupTech development.

2. Survey Reach and 
Representation 
The SupTech Survey reflects strong global engagement, with 49 authorities 
participating on a voluntary basis across all IOSCO regions. 
Conducted between March and May 2025, the survey was open to all IOSCO members and 
designed to capture a representative snapshot of supervisory approaches to SupTech 
adoption. Participation accounted for over 75 percent of total global securities market 
value, underscoring the reach of the initiative. Authorities were invited to share both 
quantitative data and qualitative perspectives on strategic, operational, and institutional 
dimensions of SupTech implementation. 
Geographic participation was well balanced across all regions (Figure 1). 
Roughly a quarter of responses came from each of the Americas and Europe, while nearly 
30 percent were submitted by authorities from Africa and the Middle East. Asia-Pacific 
accounted for just under one fifth of responses. This broad engagement illustrates that 
SupTech is not confined to any single region, but instead reflects a shared agenda across 
regions.

3. What’s Driving SupTech 
Adoption 
Efficiency remains the primary motivation for SupTech adoption, but the underlying 
rationale extends well beyond cost savings or automation. 
When asked why they invest in SupTech, 92 percent of authorities cited efficiency, making 
it the most frequently mentioned demand-side driver (Figure 4). Timeliness followed closely 
at 80 percent, with roughly two-thirds also highlighting the potential to enhance 
supervisory capabilities and generate deeper insights. This suggests that SupTech is 
increasingly seen as a way to modernize supervisory processes while preserving core 
functions.

Smaller authorities placed particular emphasis on efficiency and timeliness, with nearly 
96 percent and 88 percent respectively selecting these two factors. 
Cost and cybersecurity were also more frequently cited by smaller and GEM authorities

concerns. Regulatory mandates, by contrast, were cited by only 14 percent of respondents, 
underscoring that SupTech adoption is largely driven by internal priorities rather than 
external requirements. 
On the supply side, AI use and data access stand out as the most influential enablers, 
while enthusiasm for distributed ledger technology (DLT) has declined significantly.

4. Risk Landscape of SupTech 
Adoption 
Cyber and data security risks are the most frequently cited barriers to SupTech 
adoption. 
Eighty-six percent of respondents identified cyber risk as either high or critical in the 
context of SupTech implementation, making it the most prominent concern by a wide 
margin (Figure 7). This underscores the perception that while technology can strengthen 
supervisory capabilities, it also exposes institutions to new types of vulnerability. Small 
authorities and emerging markets expressed even higher levels of concern, with over 91 
percent in both groups ranking cyber risk as a top issue.

Concerns related to third parties and operational risk follow closely, particularly among 
smaller and GEM authorities.

while operational risk was identified by 49 percent. These concerns may relate to factors 
such as implementation complexity or challenges in integrating SupTech into existing 
supervisory frameworks. 
By contrast, staff redundancy was rarely cited as a risk, reinforcing the view that 
SupTech is not about displacing personnel.

5. Strategy, Institutional Set-Up, 
and Cooperation 
SupTech is broadly recognized as a strategic priority, but most strategies remain in a 
phase of partial implementation. 
Across SupTech, data, and innovation strategies, the majority of authorities report being in 
the process of implementation rather than at either end of the spectrum (Figures 8–10). 
Fully implemented strategies remain the exception, indicating that SupTech is still evolving 
as a strategic and institutional focus. 
Some patterns emerge: GEM authorities appear more advanced on SupTech, while small 
authorities show stronger progress on data. 
Nearly two-thirds of GEM authorities report partial implementation of a SupTech strategy — 
the highest share across groups. Small authorities, on the other hand, report fewer draft-
stage data strategies, suggesting more consistent progress in this area. Implementation of 
innovation strategies is generally less developed, with both GEM and small authorities 
showing wider variation.

6. Use Cases 
Consumer protection and capital markets supervision are the most common areas of 
SupTech use. 
Over 75 percent of authorities report active SupTech use in consumer protection, and 67 
percent in capital markets (Figure 16). These are the only domains where activity exceeds 
the 50 percent threshold across the total sample, highlighting their relevance as 
supervisory priorities. Small authorities report even higher levels of use in consumer 
protection (83 percent), while GEM jurisdictions show more balanced levels across both 
areas.

8. Human Resources 
Upskilling is a clear priority, with most authorities deploying a mix of online and ad hoc 
training. 
Nearly four in five authorities report using online training to develop SupTech capabilities 
(Figure 24). Ad hoc courses follow closely at 73 percent, suggesting that flexible, targeted 
interventions are a favored approach. Use of external providers (51 percent) and in-house 
training (49 percent) is also widespread, though to a lesser extent. Mentorship programs, in 
contrast, are employed by only one in four authorities, pointing to a more limited emphasis 
on structured, long-term staff development through internal knowledge sharing.

Authorities view external hiring as a high priority, while internal capability building 
remains at a medium level. 
This approach reflects both urgency and pragmatism: authorities are seeking immediate 
expertise on the external market, while concurrently investing in internal training 
mechanisms. It suggests that capacity building remains a work in progress, with a need to 
strike a balance between external recruitment and sustainable, in-house skill development.

9. Outlook 
SupTech is entering a phase of institutional consolidation, with broad-based interest 
translating into structured strategies and resource commitments. 
The survey results indicate that authorities across jurisdictions and market types have 
moved beyond experimentation. SupTech is no longer seen as a niche initiative but is 
becoming an embedded feature of supervisory frameworks. Strategic leadership, dedicated 
budgets, and cross-functional teams point to a shift toward mainstreaming technology in 
the regulatory core. 
Yet ambitions still exceed current capabilities, highlighting the need for targeted 
investment and cooperation. 
While the appetite for high-tech tools and innovative data sources is strong, gaps remain—
particularly in areas such as advanced analytics, AI adoption, and real-time surveillance. 
Budgetary limitations continue to be the most frequently cited barrier, especially for small 
and emerging market authorities. These constraints may require a recalibration of scope, 
sequencing, and external support. 
Collaboration remains essential for bridging knowledge gaps and advancing shared 
learning. 
The diversity of institutional approaches to SupTech is not a limitation but a source of 
strength. Rather than aiming for uniformity, cooperation should focus on exchanging 
experiences, lessons, and practical insights across jurisdictions. As authorities navigate 
similar challenges at different stages of maturity, peer dialogue can play a catalytic role—
fostering innovation, building confidence, and helping to avoid common pitfalls. Continued 
engagement at the international level will be key to sustaining momentum and supporting 
collective progress. 
Future SupTech progress will depend on scaling what works while enabling innovation in 
frontier areas. 
Domains like consumer protection and capital markets supervision offer mature templates 
for SupTech deployment. At the same time, fields such as digital assets continue to require 
experimentation and institutional learning. Building SupTech capacity will mean anchoring 
proven tools while supporting agile responses to emerging risks. 
The findings of this survey provide a baseline for continued dialogue, benchmarking, 
and shared progress. 
By offering a snapshot of where authorities stand and where they aim to go, this report 
supports evidence-based decision-making and peer comparison. It also underscores the 
value of continued engagement to strengthen supervisory capabilities through technology.
