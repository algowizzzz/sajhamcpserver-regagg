Recommendation of the Investor as Owner Subcommittee of the  
SEC Investor Advisory Committee Regarding  
Quarterly vs. Semi-annual Reporting 
The Securities and Exchange Commission (the “ SEC”) has proposed a rule that would 
eliminate the longstanding requirement that public companies provide periodic disclosure reports 
to investors on a quarterly basis . Instead, public companies would be subject to a semi -annual 
disclosure mandate and retain the option to provide quarterly disclosure reports  voluntarily (the 
“Proposal”).1 The SEC’s Investor Advisory Committee (the “IAC”) recently examined this topic 
at its March 12, 2026 meeting in a panel discussion entitled “Public Company Disclosure 
Reform.”2 Panelists overwhelmingly noted the structural importance of the existing quarterly 
reporting cadence to the U.S. capital markets and were skeptical that a semi -annual alternative 
would be feasible or attractive to most public companies. 
In this recommendation, the IAC evaluates the policy rationale advanced by the SEC in 
support of the proposed shift to a semi-annual reporting mandate and considers the potential impact 
of the Proposal on investors. 
We conclude that the SEC should not eliminate its quarterly reporting mandate for public 
companies, as doing so would deprive the markets of timely, material information, and thereby 
undermine informed investor decision making and the efficient allocation of capital among public 
companies. 
Brief Overview of the Existing SEC Disclosure Framework 
Mandatory disclosure is a central pillar of the federal securities laws and the foundation on 
which investor protection, capital market efficiency, and corporate accountability rest.3 Mandatory 
disclosure operates through three interlocking channels: it enhances the informational efficiency 
of securities prices, which in turn supports the efficient allocation of capital across the economy; 
it strengthens internal corporate governance by shaping managerial behavior through the iterative 
process of preparing, vetting, and certifying disclosure under threat of legal liability; and it 
strengthens external corporate governance by enabling monitoring of public companies by

Policy Rationale  
The Proposal is primarily based on two policy considerations. First, the SEC assumes that 
shifting from a quarterly reporting mandate to a semi-annual reporting mandate would address so-
called “short-termism” by reducing managerial incentives to overly focus on short -term financial 
performance at the expense of beneficial long -term investments that may negatively impact 
quarterly results .18 Second, the Proposal postul ates that reducing the frequency of mandatory 
public disclosures would substantially reduce compliance and reporting costs for public companies 
by decreasing the burden associated with preparing more frequent disclosure filings, including the 
internal resources, management attention and external service providers required to produce 
periodic reports and related investor communications .
19 The Proposal posits that these cost 
reductions, in turn, could make public companies more profitable and encourage more companies 
to go or remain public.
20  
Presumably, if public companies are better able to make profitable long-term investments 
and the cost of being a public company is lower, then investors would benefit from improved long-
term performance by public companies and higher returns on their investments. 21 Moreover, it is 
possible that more private companies would go public, since they would be better able to make 
long-term investments and the cost of being a public company would be lower.22 Shifting from a 
quarterly reporting mandate to a semi-annual reporting mandate could therefore expand investor 
opportunities in public markets by increasing the number of public companies.  
It is important to note that public companies could still choose to report on a quarterly 
basis, potentially mooting these benefits (or costs) if all companies do so. Indeed, the possibility 
that public companies would choose to voluntarily disclose on a quarterly basis is often put forward 
as a reason why eliminating the quarterly disclosure mandate would not be a fundamental change 
to U.S. capital markets. 23 The SEC itself acknowledges that the overall impact of the Proposal is 
uncertain, as it depends on the number and type of public companies that elect to shift to semi -
annual reporting and the extent to which those companies “ supplement [mandatory disclosures]

The argument that voluntary quarterly reporting can substitute for a mandate also overlooks 
a fundamental adverse selection problem. V oluntary disclosure regimes tend to produce skewed 
information environments because firms have strong incentives to disclose information that lowers 
their cost of capital and, crucially, to withhold information that does not.25 Accordingly, the firms 
with the most useful information for investors —those experiencing performance deterioration, 
emerging risks, or governance problems—are precisely the firms least likely to voluntarily opt in 
to quarterly reporting. Conversely, firms that do voluntarily report quarterly will tend to be those 
with positive or neutral news to share. The effect is that investor reliance on voluntary disclosure 
increases information asymmetry rather than reducing it,

distinguish between higher - and lower -quality firms. 27 This is precisely the type of selective 
disclosure dynamic that the federal mandatory disclosure regime was designed to prevent.

preference for quarterly reports, including in response to the SEC’s request for comment on this 
issue in 2019. 29 One can reasonably expect t hese institutional investors would  encourage public 
companies to continue to voluntarily report on a quarterly basis through direct shareholder

The Impact on Investors from Eliminating Mandatory Quarterly Reporting 
Although the benefits of a shift away from mandatory quarterly reporting are uncertain at 
best,55 the potential costs and risks for investors are clear. 
Today, the material financial and non -financial information provided in quarterly reports 
provides investors an opportunity to reallocate capital among public companies based on changes 
in performance or outlook every three months. Indeed, studies document a significant increase in 
trading volume in the days following quarterly earnings announcements, 56 indicating that both 
retail and institutional investors actively respond to these disclosures.  
By eliminating a quarterly reporting mandate, investors would have fewer  opportunities 
per year to reallocate positions based on standardized updates on company performance and 
outlook, including financial statements . Quarterly reports are particularly important to fund 
managers, who rely on timely financial information to evaluate the performance, financial 
condition, and relative risk and reward of portfolio companies.
57 In practice,  eliminating a 
quarterly reporting mandate will likely mean that investors remain invested in poorer performing 
companies or c ompanies with diminished outlooks for longer than they would today. Investors 
would have to wait longer to reallocate their capital to better-performing companies or companies 
with a more promising outlook. This reduction in the frequency of standardized material 
information could therefore negatively affect returns for investors.

Empirical studies also indicate that reduced frequency of disclosures harms price efficiency 
and valuation through two related channels. First, when firms report only semiannually, investors 
rely on information provided by peer-firms, often overreacting to those signals and leading to over 
or under valuation of public companies .68 Second, less frequent reporting reduces cross -firm 
comparability, making it harder for investors to assess relative performance and industry 
conditions.69  
A shift to semi-annual reporting would also increase insider-trading concerns because more 
frequent disclosure reduces insiders’ ability to profit from nonpublic information. By extending 
the interval between mandatory reports, semi -annual reporting would widen the gap between 
inside and public information, increasing the risk that insiders could trade while possessing 
material information not yet available to the market.
70 The Proposal acknowledges this heightened 
risk and suggests that it may warrant longer blackout periods, as the value of material non-public 
information and the potential for misuse would be higher.71 A shift to semi-annual reporting would 
also weaken corporate accountability. 72 For example, poor quarterly results present  corporate 
management with market discipline that can help them make necessary business changes to  
improve future returns. Shifting to semi -annual disclosures would therefore reduce market 
discipline.  
Recommendation 
In conclusion, the policy rationale  for the Proposal  is primarily based on concerns 
regarding “short-termism” and lowering costs for public companies. However, the evidence does 
not support the view that short -termism is a major problem for U.S. public companies today, nor 
is there strong evidence to support the view that costs for public companies would be significantly 
reduced by a shift to a semi-annual mandate. On the contrary, the evidence indicates that a shift to
