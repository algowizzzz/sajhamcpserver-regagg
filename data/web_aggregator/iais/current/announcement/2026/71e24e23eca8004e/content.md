About the IAIS 
The Inter national Association of Insurance Supervisors (IAIS) is a voluntary membership 
organisation of insurance supervisors and regulators from more than 200 jurisdictions. The mission 
of the IAIS is to promote effective and globally consistent supervision of the insurance industry in 
order to develop and maintain fair, safe and stable insurance markets for the benefit and protection 
of policyholders and to contribute to global financial stability.  
Established in 1994, the IAIS is the inter national standard-setting body responsible for developing 
principles, standards and other supporting material for the supervision of the insurance sector and 
assisting in their implementation. The IAIS also provides a forum for members to share their 
experiences and understanding of insurance supervision and insurance markets.  
The IAIS coordinates its work with other inter national financial policymakers and associations of 
supervisors or regulators, and assists in shaping financial systems globally. In particular, the IAIS is 
a member of the Financial Stability Board (FSB), member of the Standards Advisory Council of the 
International Accounting Standards Board (IASB) and partner in the Access to Insurance 
Initiative (A2ii). In recognition of its collective expertise, the IAIS also is routinely called upon by the 
G20 leaders and other international standard-setting bodies for input on insurance issues as well as 
on issues related to the regulation and supervision of the global financial sector. 
For more information, please visit www.iais.org and follow us on LinkedIn: IAIS – International 
Association of Insurance Supervisors.

Executive summary 
This document provides a detailed assessment of the regulation and supervision of the 
insurance sector in the Republic of Costa Rica. The assessment was conducted on behalf of the 
International Association of Insurance Supervisors (IAIS) at the request of the  General 
Superintendency of Insurance (Sugese), a member agency. The work was carried out between 
May 2025 and June 2026, including two weeks in person in San Jos é, Costa Rica in August 2025. 
The assessment is benchmarked against the Insurance Core Principles (ICPs) issued by the IAIS in 
October 2011, including revisions approved by the IAIS up until December 2024.   
The assessment is based on reviews of documentation and meetings in San Jos é. The work 
included reviews of laws and regulations and a self-assessment of observance of the ICPs prepared 
by Sugese, which also made available confidential supervisory documents. Meetings were held with 
the Senior Management and staff of Sugese and of the National Supervisory Council for the Financial 
System (Conassif), the Central Bank of Costa Rica (CBCR), other agencies, representative insurers 
and intermediaries, auditors and industry associations. The team of assessors, which was drawn 
from IAIS members and the Secretariat, supported by an external consultant, is grateful for the warm 
welcome and hospitality offered by the Superintendent and for the excellent cooperation extended 
by Sugese’s management and staff, and by all other parties.  
The insurance sector comprises 12 mostly foreign-owned licensed insurers, but is dominated 
by the state-owned former monopoly insurer. The market was opened to private sector (including 
foreign-owned) insurer participation only in 2008. The state-owned insurer’s market share has fallen 
as new entrants have gained  share in most lines other than compulsory business (workers ’ 
compensation and mandatory motor vehicle accidents insurance). However, it still accounts for 65% 
of gross premiums, with a smaller share of life insurance and voluntary business. There are 10 
foreign or foreign-owned insurers, with parents based elsewhere in the region or in Europe. There 
are no regulated domestic insurance groups or domestic insurers with foreign operations.   
The market is dominated by general insurance. The overall penetration ratio of 2.8% of gross 
domestic product (GDP) in 2024 reflects mostly general insurance (life penetration was only 0.5%). 
Insurers must be licensed for personal, general or mixed insurance (composites), the last accounting 
for over 90% of sector assets. The main business lines are growing strongly, led by health insurance. 
Life insurance is also growing, although savings-related products remain limited, as do “self-issued” 
policies (standardised products that can be bought directly by consumers without needing expert 
advice). Most reinsurance is sourced externally, with a market retention ratio of 80%, although with 
wide variations. The broker channel dominates distribution, accounting for over 60% of business.  
The regulatory and supervisory framework is assessed to have a good level of observance 
of the ICPs. Half of the ICPs, including those on supervision and corrective actions, are assessed 
to be either Observed or Largely Observed, reflecting an extensive, broadly risk-based framework. 
For many of the 12 ICPs assessed to be Partly Observed, planned reforms are likely to lead to a 
higher level of  observance. These include enhancement of conduct of business supervision and 
reforms to solvency requirements and macroprudential supervision. No ICPs are assessed to be Not 
Observed.  
Regulation and supervision benefit from a strong institutional framework.  Sugese is 
responsible for licensing, supervision and enforcement for the sector. Conassif, the regulatory body 
for the entire financial sector, issues regulations, has certain intervention powers ( but there is no 
resolution authority) and functions as the governing body for Sugese. Both Sugese and Conassif are

Public 
agencies attached to the CBCR, with administrative and budgetary independence. They also enjoy 
a high degree of operational independence from government, balanced by accountability. 
There is, however, scope for further strengthening the arrangements, including protection 
from legal action and resourcing. Although Sugese has clear and appropriate objectives, it would 
benefit from a more widely defined financial stability objective. It lacks powers in a small number of 
areas. The Minister of Finance’s membership of Conassif is a potential channel of political 
interference. Both Conassif and Sugese lack protection in law from legal action for damages resulting 
from decisions taken in good  faith. Government fiscal measures constrain financial management 
and recruitment , and both agencies face challenges in managing workloads  – especially in 
supervision – with current resources. 
There is an extensive framework, albeit with some gaps, on licensing and other regulatory 
transactions. Sugese evaluates licence applications fully and has both denied and approved recent 
applications. However, the exemption of mandatory life insurance for teachers means that one large 
insurer, which provides this (and only this) class of insurance, is not licensed. In addition, Sugese 
may not be able to reject licence applications from subsidiaries of foreign insurers if it has not been 
able to obtain input from the home supervisors of the parent insurer. Sugese also approves portfolio 
transfers and changes of control associated with mergers between insurers or other regulated 
financial institutions, but lacks the power to approve other changes in control.  
There are comprehensive suitability requirements, although Sugese lacks powers in respect 
of unsuitable persons. The requirements cover Boards, Senior Management and key persons in 
control functions, and there are appropriate requirements on significant owners. Sugese must be 
notified of new appointments, and supervisors review them in addition to assessing insurers’ internal 
processes. They may , and occasionally do , request the removal of persons who are no longer 
suitable, but lack the power to directly suspend or remove such persons or to require insurers to do 
so.   
Sugese takes a risk-based approach to supervision, assessing risk and allocating resources 
to supervisory work accordingly . It takes a rigorous and well-documented approach to risk 
assessment and has a framework for planning supervisory work according to risk. Off -site reviews 
are based on insurers’ extensive financial and other reporting  to Sugese . Supervision work is 
generally thorough and well  documented and leads to actions required of insurers, although the 
issuance of reports of on -site work can take many months. Sugese sends an annual letter to all 
insurers’ Boards summarising its supervisory view, outstanding actions etc.  
Sugese takes preventive and corrective actions, escalates as necessary and imposes 
sanctions. Legislation gives it extensive powers, including to restrict dividend payments or activities 
(although, as mentioned, it lacks powers to suspend or remove directors etc) , and it uses them in 
practice. It may intervene in case of unlicensed activity. Escalation is applied where necessary due 
to increasing risk. Sugese also imposes sanctions based on wide-ranging criteria. Its measures are 
published. It faces obstacles to the imposition of sanctions on individuals and has been unable to 
take such action in practice.  
There are risk-based solvency requirements, but they need to be strengthened in key aspects 
as part of planned reforms. Insurers must calculate risk-based capital requirements and maintain 
capital (in mostly high-quality form) equivalent to at least 130% of the requirements. There is also a 
mix of quantitative and (mostly) qualitative requirements on investments for solvency purposes. The 
framework will be revised to reflect the planned implementation of International Financial Reporting 
Standard (IFRS) 17 in 2028 (existing valuation requirements are broadly appropriate for the current 
business mix). In the longer term, Sugese plans further reforms to provide for a more fully risk-based

Public 
approach with calibration based on explicit target criteria. It should also implement a framework of 
solvency control levels that include an appropriately defined minimum capital requirement (MCR).   
There are gaps in the requirements on enterprise-wide risk management (ERM) and own risk 
and solvency assessment (ORSA).  There are limited requirements for quantitative tools, for 
example. The requirements on risk and solvency self-assessment (RSSA, a version of ORSA) do 
not include making it an integral part of strategic decision -making and capital management. 
However, Sugese reviews insurers’ risk management and has made requests for improvements to 
RSSAs. No pre-emptive recovery scenarios or options for recovery are required.   
Requirements on governance and risk management are mostly complete. Regulations set out 
requirements of Boards and Senior Management, and require a developed risk management system 
(RMS), internal control processes and a full set of independent control functions. There are extensive 
requirements on reinsurance risk management. There is a need to  further extend the scope of 
requirements on remuneration and to strengthen those on outsourcing. Supervision work includes a 
structured assessment of governance and risk management.   
There are extensive requirements applying to intermediaries, although supervision is limited. 
There are provisions on licensing and regulation, supervision processes and sanctioning authority. 
There are extensive requirements on governance, financial condition and protection of client monies. 
Sugese monitors intermediaries’ professional education and experience . Supervision is limited 
pending implementation of a planned risk-based conduct of business supervision framework 
(Conassif is considering a proposal), and because of limited staffing.  
There are requirements on conduct of business, but they need to be supported by the planned 
risk-based supervision framework. Laws and regulations cover many aspects of the product 
lifecycle, but they do not  require insurers and intermediaries to consider the consumer’s 
circumstances when evaluating needs . Regulations are lacking in other areas , including product 
development and claims handling. Pending the planned risk-based business conduct framework, 
supervision is mainly reactive. 
Powers and tools , as well as preparatory work for the resolution of insurers , require 
development. Extensive provisions on the voluntary exit of insurers have been tested in practice. 
However, there is no identified resolution authority , nor are there any resolution powers and 
processes (and no protection fund). Sugese would by law be involved in the initiation of bankruptcy 
proceedings, the current (and untested) approach under which high priority is given to policyholders. 
However, the point at which Sugese would trigger bankruptcy is not well  defined. Sugese has not 
yet undertaken resolution planning. 
Disclosure requirements need to be broadened. Extensive financial information on insurers is 
available, supported by the wide -ranging materials published by Sugese on its website  and by 
insurers themselves. However, there are shortfalls in the requirements , including on disclosure of 
objectives and policies on investment management.  
Sugese takes a thorough risk-based approach to anti-money laundering and combating the 
financing of terrorism ( AML/CFT) supervision, but there are limited requirements and 
supervision work on insurance fraud.  There is a robust AML/CFT regime for insurers in law. 
Sugese adopts a risk ‑based approach and is active in domestic and inter national cooperation. 
Although supervisors have a good working understanding of the risks, there is no money 
laundering/terrorism financing (ML/TF) risk assessment for the sector (one is being prepared) , and 
AML/CFT oversight of intermediaries is limited.  Although legislation criminalises insurance fraud, 
Sugese lacks a comprehensive risk assessment of the sector’s fraud exposure a s well as  a 
supervision and enforcement framework.

Public 
Although there are no regulated insurance groups at present, consolidated supervision has 
been implemented for financial groups, including for the one insurer -led group. Extensive 
requirements apply to all domestic financial groups, covering the identification of the group -wide 
supervisor and the determination of the scope of group -wide supervision. The framework would 
apply to any future regulated insurance group (there is an unregulated one now; the parent is exempt 
from regulation). Sugese applies it to the financial group whose parent is an insurer. 
Sugese exchanges confidential information with other authorities and engages in 
cross-border supervisory cooperation. It has relevant powers and a wide network of bilateral and 
regional multilateral agreements. It is not yet a signatory to the IAIS Multilateral Memorandum of 
Understanding on Cooperation and Information Exchange (MMoU), although it has recently applied. 
In practice, i t exchanges confidential information subject to appropriate safeguards. It has no 
responsibilities now as group-wide supervisor but, as an involved supervisor, it is an active member 
of supervisory colleges for five foreign-owned insurers.  
Sugese undertakes macroprudential supervision  in cooperation with the CBCR  and is 
planning enhancements. Sugese identifies and analyses insurance market developments  and 
cooperates on top-down stress tests led by the CBCR. It contributes to wider financial stability work 
through the  inter-agency Financial Stability Committee (FSC) and its working groups. It collects 
detailed information useful for macroprudential supervision.  It needs  (and plan s) to introduce a 
systemic risk identification methodology for insurers and  to further develop stress testing with the 
CBCR to include natural catastrophes.  
This report makes recommendations, addressed to Sugese, Conassif and the Government of 
Costa Rica, aimed at achieving a higher level of observance. Most of the recommendations may 
be met by Sugese and Conassif working together, as they already are in many of the areas needing 
enhancement, to strengthen regulatory or supervisory requirements. There are other areas in which 
changes to primary legislation appear necessary, including extending regulatory and supervisory 
powers, addressing exempt business and enhancing institutional arrangements.

Assessment of Insurance Core Principles 
1 Introduction and scope 
This document provides a detailed assessment of regulation and supervision of the insurance sector 
in the Republic of Costa Rica. The assessment was conducted by a team of assessors: Ian Tower, 
insurance regulation and supervision consultant; Frane Mazovec, Insurance Supervision Agency of 
Slovenia, Slovenia; Alex Romero, Alaska Division of Insurance, US; Désirée Scharbaay, Central e 
Bank van Aruba; Manuela Zweimueller (until 15 August 2025), International Association of Insurance 
Supervisors (IAIS); and Tom Wicling (from August 2025), IAIS. 
The assessment was conducted on behalf of the IAIS at the request of the General Superintendency 
of Insurance ( Superintendencia General de Seguros de Costa Rica (Sugese) ), an IAIS member 
supervisor. The work was carried out between May 2025 and June 2026, including two weeks in 
person in San José, Costa Rica from 18 to 29 August 2025, and concluded with the publication of 
this report in mid -2026. The assessment is benchmarked against the Insurance C ore Principles 
(ICPs) issued by the IAIS in October 2011, i ncluding revisions approved by the IAIS up until 
December 2024. The ICPs apply to all insurers, whether private or government -controlled, in all 
markets. Specific principles apply to the supervision of intermediaries. 
The objectives of the assessment are to enhance understanding of the ICPs, aid in their 
implementation, identify major differences between existing practices and inter national standards, 
and provide recommendations and advice to help with the future development of supervisory 
programmes. The assessment should not be construed to be part of the IMF-World Bank Financial 
Sector Assessment Program (FSAP). 
This detailed assessment included reviews of laws, regulations etc , and a full self -assessment of 
observance of the ICPs prepared by Sugese, which also made available selected confidential 
supervisory documents. Meetings were held with the Senior Management and staff of Sugese and 
of the National Supervisory Council for the Financial System (Conassif), the Central Bank of Costa 
Rica (CBCR), other agencies, representative insurers and intermediaries, auditors and industry 
associations. The purpose of these meetings was to understand the regulatory requirements and 
supervision approach and to evaluate their application in practice.  
2 Information and methodology used for the assessment 
The level of observance for each ICP reflects assessment against its standards. Each ICP is rated 
in terms of the level of observance as follows: 
• Observed (O): For a Principle Statement to be considered Observed, all the standards must be 
considered observed (except any standards that are considered not applicable). 
• Largely Observed (LO): For a Principle Statement to be considered Largely Observed, there 
must be only minor shortcomings that do not raise any concerns about the supervisor’s ability to 
achieve full observance with the Principle Statement. 
• Partly Observed (PO): – For a Principle Statement to be considered Partly Observed, there are 
sufficient shortcomings that raise doubts about the supervisor’s ability to achieve observance.

Public 
• Not Observed (NO): For a Principle Statement to be considered Not Observed, there is no 
substantive progress towards achieving observance. 
• Not Applicable: For a Principle Statement to be considered Not Applicable, all the standards must 
be considered not applicable. 
The assessment is based solely on the laws, regulations and other supervisory practices in place at 
the time of the on-site phase of the assessment (29 August 2025). Although the assessment does 
not reflect ongoing regulatory initiatives, some proposals for regulatory reform are discussed by way 
of additional comments in this report.  
The authorities provided a self-assessment, supported by examples of actual supervisory practices 
and assessments related to regulated legal entities. These examples enhanced the robustness of 
the work. Technical discussions with, and briefings by, officials from Sugese have also enriched this 
report, as have discussions with industry participants  and industry and professional associations. 
Discussions with these stakeholders were conducted on a confidential basis, without the presence 
of representatives of the supervisor. 
3 Preconditions for effective insurance supervision 
Sound and sustainable macroeconomic and financial sector policies 
The Ministry of Finance (MoF) prepares the national budget and oversees its implementation. The 
Government of Costa Rica has defined a Medium-Term Fiscal Framework for the years 2024–29. A 
fiscal rule limiting the growth of nominal total government spending in accordance with the level of 
public debt has been set out in legislation and has resulted in a reduction of the level of public debt 
(around 60% of gross domestic product (GDP)) in recent years. Costa Rica has sovereign access to 
international capital markets on favourable terms. 
The CBCR is responsible for monetary policy , which, under the Central Bank of Costa Rica Law 
(CBCRL), is aimed at controlling inflation, managing interest rates and ensuring currency stability. 
The CBCR currently targets a 3% per annum inflation rate over a  12- to 24-month period, with a 
tolerance range of ±1 percentage point. The International Monetary Fund (IMF) projected the 2025 
rate to be 0.4%. The exchange rate is allowed to move flexibly in response to market conditions (a 
managed floating regime), subject to stabilisation operations conducted by the CBCR under its policy 
framework. The US dollar (USD) is widely used, accounting for around 35% of banks’ loans and 
deposits.   
Costa Rica has received significant support from the IMF in recent years. In June 2025, the IMF 
approved a two-year arrangement under the Flexible Credit Line (FCL) for around USD 1.5 billion. 
Access to the FCL is limited to countries with strong economic fundamentals and institutional policy 
frameworks. The IMF noted the country’s track record of implementing strong policies and 
commitment to maintaining such policies in the future. In 2021, Costa Rica  was admitted to 
membership of the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) following an 
extensive accession review process.  
Under the CBCRL, the CBCR is responsible for promoting favourable conditions for the soundness, 
liquidity, solvency and proper functioning of the financial system. It exercises the regulatory function 
through Conassif, an independent body attached to the CBCR, of which the CBCR’s President is a 
member and which is charged with bringing uniformity and integration to the regulation and 
supervision activities of the financial system.

Public 
Supervision is carried out by other bodies (superintendencies) attached to the CBCR and 
responsible for the different sectors of the financial system under separate governing legislation . 
They include:  
• The General Superintendency of Financial Entities (Sugef), for the supervision of banks and 
certain non-bank financial institutions; 
• The General Superintendency of Securities (Sugeval); 
• The Superintendency of Pensions (Supen); and  
• Sugese.  
In addition to  approving all regulations, Conassif serves as the governing body of the 
superintendencies. 
The CBCR shares responsibility for macroprudential supervision with Conassif and the 
superintendencies. The Financial Stability Committee (FSC) makes recommendations on the 
management of systemic risks and the implementation of macroprudential policies, including stress 
testing, simulation exercises and crisis management. It meets quarterly, supported by the Financial 
Stability Department of the CBCR. Its members are the President of the CBCR, the Vice Minister of 
Finance, a Conassif representative and the Director of the Deposit Guarantee Fund (DGF) of the 
CBCR (the deposit insurance scheme). The C BCR issues an annual Financial Stability Report 
(FSR).  
A feature of the financial system is the significant share of state-owned financial institutions, resulting 
from a state-led economic development model of the past. Despite the growth of private sector 
institutions following the market opening, the two largest banks are state-owned and two other public 
banks were created by special laws. The state-owned former monopoly remains the largest insurer. 
A small unit within the Ministry of the Presidency (the Presidential Advisory Unit on State -Owned 
Enterprises (SOEs)) is responsible for SOE oversight with a mandate to, amongst other things, 
formalise, professionalise and make more transparent the appointment process of SOE Board 
members. Its approach is developing  (in February 2026, Sugese submitted to the Parliament 
proposed legislative measures aimed at strengthening the policy framework for the state -owned 
insurance company).  
There is also a framework for anti -money laundering and combating the financing of terrorism 
(AML/CFT) regulation and supervision. The superintendencies are responsible for AML/CFT 
supervision of supervised financial institutions. The Financial Intelligence Unit (FIU), which is part of 
the national drug authority, collects and analyses suspicious transaction reports and registers certain 
designated non-financial businesses and professional bodies (DNFBPs), amongst other functions.   
Well-developed public infrastructure 
There is a developed framework of business laws covering companies (the Commercial Code), 
insolvency (Commercial Code and Code of Civil Procedure), contracts (the Civil Code) and 
consumer protection (Law for the Promotion of Competition and Effective Consumer Protection).  
Article 9 of the Constitution affirms the principle of judicial independence and provides for rights to a 
hearing, defence, equality and procedural fairness and to be judged by impartial and independent 
courts. The Judicial Power (the judicial branch of government, with the Supreme Court of Justice at 
its head) has the duty to administer the justice system, according to guidelines established in the 
Organic Law of the Judiciary. The Supreme Court has four  chambers, one of which is the 
Constitutional Court. There are appeals courts and other lower courts. There is no specialist court 
dedicated to commercial cases.

Public 
The Constitution also creates rights to settle property disputes through arbitration, even if there is 
pending litigation. Alternative mechanisms for resolving disputes operate within the framework of the 
Law on Alternative Conflict Resolution and Promotion of Social Peace and include the Conciliation 
and Arbitration Center of the Costa Rican Chamber of Commerce. 
Costa Rica has adopted International Financial Reporting Standards (IFRS). The College of Public 
Accountants of Costa Rica (CPACR) is authorised to adopt accounting standards under Article 14 
of the Law on the Regulation of the Public Accounting Profession and Creation of the College of 
Public Accountants of Costa Rica  of 1947. It has adopted full IFRS (and adopts new standards as 
they are issued) for use in financial reporting (separate and consolidated accounts) by listed 
companies. All other companies may elect to use either full IFRS or the IFRS for SMEs Standard. 
There are no separate national accounting standards. 
For regulated financial institutions, Conassif regulations require that all regulated legal entities use 
full IFRS, with specific prudential or regulatory exceptions. 
The CPACR is also responsible for audit standards and requires the use of International Standards 
on Auditing (ISA). It is a non-government public entity and a member of the International Federation 
of Accountants (IFAC). There is an equivalent body for private accountants, the College of Private 
Accountants of Costa Rica. 
In addition to  maintaining a registry of authorised public accountants and setting both accounting 
and auditing standards, the CPACR’s functions include establishing ethical standards for authorised 
public accountants, implementing an investigation and discipline system and a quality assurance 
review system for its members, and providing input on the accountancy curricula for authorised 
public accountants. Neither the CPACR nor any other body is a member of the International Forum 
of Independent Audit Regulators (IFIAR).  
Audit firms for regulated legal entities must also be registered in the Registry of External Auditors 
administered by Sugeval. To be included in the registry, they must comply with the CPACR’s 
requirements and quality standards and with Conassif’s detailed requirements, including on 
independence and rotation of auditors.   
There is no local specialist actuarial professional body. However, actuaries are regulated by the 
Association of Professionals in Economic Sciences (CCECR). It requires registration but not specific 
qualifications (or subsequent tests) , and has no role in the issuance  of actuarial practices or 
regulatory standards. Actuaries must present evidence of their relevant training as a condition of 
registration by the CCECR.  
As of August 2025, there were 124 registered actuarial professionals, a large increase over the low 
numbers when the insurance market was opened to private sector involvement in 2008 , but not 
sufficient to meet demand. Foreign-owned insurers (the majority) often rely extensively on actuarial 
teams at other member companies of their groups to provide them with actuarial services.  
In 2012, Sugese, in collaboration with the University of Costa Rica, established a technical training 
programme in actuarial science to increase the supply of specialised professionals. An actuarial 
degree programme has recently been created at the university. 
Financial market participants have access to payment and clearing systems for the settlement of 
financial transactions. The payment system is managed by the CBCR in accordance with its 
objective in law to promote the efficiency and proper functioning of bo th domestic and international 
payment systems.

Public 
The National Electronic Payment System (SINPE) provides electronic fund transfers between 
accounts and facilitates participation in the money and foreign exchange markets organ ised by the 
CBCR, such as the Integrated Liquidity Market and Foreign Currency Market. It offers a wide range 
of payment services, including credit and debit transactions. SINPE is recogn ised as systemically 
important. The National Stock Exchange provides settlement services for other markets.  
Under the National Statistics System established by law, the National Institute of Statistics and 
Censuses (INEC) acts as the governing body for the production and publication of statistics. It works 
with the CBCR, the superintendencies and other public institutions in respect to their contribution to 
official statistics, as well as with private bodies where necessary. Data on mortality for use by insurers 
in valuation of life insurance liabilities is produced by the Central American Population Research 
Center at the University of Costa Rica, which draws on population census results.  
A wide range of statistics, including population data, economic statistics, price indices and social and 
environmental statistics, are available in practice. Censuses, including of population and housing, 
are conducted.   
Effective market discipline in financial markets 
The Commercial Code sets out detailed provisions on the operation, administration and control 
mechanisms of corporations, including special rules for joint  stock companies (Sociedades 
Anónimas). It provides a framework for the protection of shareholder rights, such as the right to 
information, the right to participate in decision -making and the right to receive dividends , with the 
objective of ensuring that shareholders are well  informed and can exercise their corporate rights 
effectively.  
The regulation on corporate governance issued by Conassif ( Conassif Agreement 04-16) sets out 
the most important governance standards in the country. Issued in 2016 and based on the 
G20/OECD Principles of Corporate Governance, the regulation applies to all legal entities operating 
in the financial markets, including banks, listed companies, state-owned banks, SOEs making bond 
offerings, pension funds, insurers, institutional investors and market operators. It has most recently 
been extended to cover holding companies and companies that are members of regulated financial 
groups and conglomerates. 
There is also a Voluntary Code of Best Corporate Governance Practices issued (before the Conassif 
regulation) by the Costa Rican Institute of Corporate Governance (ICG). The ICG is a non -profit 
association funded by members and the National Stock Exchange with a mission to promote good 
corporate governance. Given the scope of the Conassif regulation, the ICG now promotes use of the 
Voluntary Code mainly amongst non-regulated small- and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs). 
Mechanism for providing an appropriate level of policyholder protection 
There are some elements of an insurance safety net to protect policyholders in case of insurer failure. 
Policyholders have preference over most other creditors in a liquidation (see ICP 12). There is, 
however, no insurance guarantee scheme/policyholder pro tection fund that would make available 
resources for policyholders and beneficiaries who might otherwise suffer losses in case of an 
insurer’s failure.   
Although insurers should handle complaints fairly themselves, policyholders whose complaints have 
been rejected may also seek to have them addressed by policyholder advocacy offices ( insurance 
consumer protection offices), which all insurers are required to establish as bodies funded by , but 
independent of, the insurer itself. In practice, nine of the 12 insurers have collectively established a 
single body, the Insured Defence Center, to carry out this function.

Public 
Insurance consumer protection offices provide non-binding adjudications. Complainants may at any 
time take their case to the court. They may also be eligible to make a complaint to the National 
Consumer Commission (CNC), an agency under the Ministry of Economy, Industry and 
Commerce (MEIC), which has powers in respect to insurance (and consumer products and services 
generally) under consumer protection law.  
At the judicial level (as mentioned above), in addition to  the courts, there are alternative dispute 
resolution mechanisms regulated by the Law on Alternative Conflict Resolution and Promotion of 
Social Peace.  
Efficient financial markets 
The capital markets operate under the single stock exchange, the National Stock Exchange, which 
is primarily owned by brokerages. It is a self-regulated entity and offers platforms for the primary and 
secondary markets, with clearing and settlement of transactions carried out by the exchange itself 
in cooperation with the CBCR and SINPE.  
Custody services are offered by central securities depositories (CSDs), financial institutions subject 
to Sugef’s supervision and brokerages. 
Capital market activity is dominated by primary public debt issuance, with an underdeveloped 
corporate debt and equity market. Debt capital markets comprise: 
• The money market, including the Integrated Liquidity Market (ILM) organ ised by the CBCR,  
Liquidity Market (MEDI) and Repo Market (REPO) (although they are permitted, there is no 
domestic market for derivatives); and 
• The debt market, a public debt market that includes securities issued by the MoF and the CBCR 
(primarily fixed-rate, adjustable-rate and inflation-indexed bonds) and private debt (certificates of 
deposit, commercial paper and bonds). 
The main issuers in the market are the MoF and the CBCR. As of June 2024, central government 
debt was 60.9% in local currency, 32.1% in USD and 7% in other currencies. There were seven-, 
10-, 15- and 20-year issues, with around 60% of outstanding debt being over five years. Around 70% 
was fixed rate. Approximately three quarters of the public debt stock was held domestically, mainly 
by pension funds and banks. All transactions in the government bond market are carried out in the 
stock market, with limited trading in the secondary market. 
The equity market is underdeveloped because of the significance of SOEs and the prevalence of 
family-owned businesses that rely on loans from banks. As of late 2024, there were 10 listed 
companies, spanning financial institutions, construction, technology and logistics. New stock issuers 
are rare. In December 2024, the amount traded in the secondary market of shares in local currency 
was under 1% of the total traded (including debt). Market capitalisation was equivalent to 3.2% of 
GDP in 2022.

LO Conassif and Sugese are clearly identified in legislation as the 
regulatory and supervisory authorities for the insurance sector. There 
is no authority for one insurer whose business is exempted from the 
Insurance Market Regulatory Law ( IMRL) (ICP 1.1). The IMRL also 
sets out objectives, although Sugese’s objective in respect to financial 
stability is limited to the stability of the insurance sector (ICP 1.2). 
Conassif and Sugese have a wide range of powers.  
However, Sugese lacks powers to require an insurer to remove 
persons subject to the suitability requirements and to approve all 
changes in control of an insurer. Neither Sugese nor any other body 
has a full range of powers for resolution of an insurer (ICP 1.3).  
2 – Supervisor PO Conassif and Sugese enjoy a high degree of operational and financial 
independence from government. There are robust requirements on 
conflicts of interest and confidentiality of supervisory information. 
Decisions are open to appeal and are challenged in practice.  
However, the Minister of Finance’s full participation in Conassif’s work 
is a potential channel of undue political interference (ICP 2.1), even if 
there is no evidence of such in practice to date. Both Conassif and 
Sugese lack protection in law from legal actions for damages resulting 
from their decisions taken in good faith (ICP 2.2). Government fiscal 
measures are constraining financial management and recruitment. 
Both agencies face challenges in managing workloads – especially in 
supervision – with current resources (ICP 2.10).  
3 – Information 
Sharing and 
Confidentiality 
Requirements 
LO There is a well-established framework for information-sharing, subject 
to confidentiality requirements. The legislative basis is reinforced by 
Conassif processes, while a broad network of bilateral, regional and 
multilateral agreements provides effective channels for exchange of 
confidential information. Domestically, cooperation across the financial 
system is supported by the four-party MoU. 
Information exchange operates effectively where Sugese has signed 
an MoU. Requests are specific, confidentiality safeguards are 
consistently applied and internal service standards provide discipline 
in handling requests. Sugese has applied to become a signatory to the 
IAIS Multilateral Memorandum of Understanding on Cooperation and 
Information Exchange (MMoU), which would facilitate Sugese’s ability 
to exchange confidential information widely, with safeguards.

Public 
4 – Licensing PO Legislation clearly sets out a requirement for insurers to be licen sed 
and the application processes, with Sugese as the licensing authority. 
There are extensive licensing criteria and , in practice , Sugese 
evaluates applications and has both denied a nd approved them. It 
publishes a list of licensed legal entities and the scope of the licence.    
The exemption in law of the mandatory life insurance for teachers 
means that one large insurer is not licensed by Sugese under the IMRL 
or otherwise regulated, nor are there any other appropriate alternative 
safeguards for policyholders (ICP 4.1). Sugese lacks the ability to 
reject licence applications from subsidiaries of foreign insurers where 
it has been unable to obtain input from the other involved supervisors 
of the parent insurer. Sugese would always seek such input in practice.  
5 – Suitability of 
Persons 
PO The suitability regime is well developed in law and regulation and is 
applied through risk-based supervision. The design captures individual 
and collective suitability . In practice, s upervisors review appointment 
files and Board self-assessments and track corrective actions.  
However, there is no explicit duty to report adverse events affecting 
suitability (ICP 5.4), creating ambiguity over required timeliness and 
minimum evidence. There is also no statutory power for Sugese to 
order the suspension or removal of unsuitable individuals (or to require 
insurers to take such action) (ICP 5.5). 
6 – Change of 
Control and 
Portfolio 
Transfers 
PO Legislation establishes requirements and processes for certain 
changes of control and for portfolio transfers. Notification requirements 
apply to insurers in respect to proposed changes of control , but there 
is no requirement to always seek Sugese approval (ICP 6.1). Sugese 
must approve all portfolio transfers, which it most recently completed 
for a 2022 transfer.    
There are provisions in competition law for exchange of information 
and coordi nation between Sugese, Conassif and the competition 
authority, which empower Conassif and Sugese to approve changes of 
control arising from mergers  specifically. However, existing laws and 
regulations do not provide for Sugese approving all changes of control 
of insurers (ICPs 6.1 and 6.2). 
7 – Corporate 
Governance 
LO Laws and regulations provide a clear and comprehensive set of 
requirements. In practice, Sugese assesses governance through a 
structured, risk -based supervisory programme and takes actions to 
require improvements in governance where necessary.   
Areas for enhancement include broadening the scope of remuneration 
policies to cover all material risk -takers and introducing tools such as 
deferral, malus and clawback , with evidence of their use (ICP 7.6); 
extending the expectations for group and branch governance, although 
Sugese does practice group-wide supervision of the governance of the 
one insurer-led financial group and there are no insurance groups; and

Public 
strengthening supervisory work and reporting on Board effectiveness, 
especially the extent and quality of Board challenge to management. 
8 – Risk 
Management 
and Internal 
Controls 
LO There are extensive requirements on insurers’ risk management and 
internal controls. Outsourcing is permitted, but there is no requirement 
that the insurer retain at least the same degree of oversight of and 
accountability for any outsourced material activity or function as applies 
to non-outsourced activities or functions (ICP 8.8). 
9 – Supervisory 
Review and 
Reporting 
LO Sugese carries out extensive prudential supervision of insurers within 
a risk -based framework that includes thorough risk assessment, 
off-site and on -site supervisory work, reporting to the Boards and 
Senior Management of insurers and specification of required actions. 
Supervision work is carefully planned and both findings and required 
actions thoroughly documented, including in annual supervisory letters 
to the Board of each insurer. Issuance of key reports could, however, 
be faster. Sugese’s risk-based approach allows supervisors to react to 
emerging risks. It would benefit from adoption of an explicit risk appetite 
framework to guide resource allocation, given resource constraints.  
The supervisory approach excludes business conduct, although there 
are plans here (see ICP 19). Increased specialisation of supervisory 
staff (to add to the recent recruitment of actuaries and existing IT 
experts), already necessary, is likely to be come more important as 
conduct work is added to the many issues covered by supervisors.  
10 – Preventive 
Measures, 
Corrective 
Measures and 
Sanctions 
O Sugese has extensive powers, which it uses in practice, to require 
actions and impose sanctions. It may intervene in case of unlicensed 
activity. The toolkit of requirements on preventive and corrective 
actions (including dividend and activity restrictions)  is extensive . 
Sugese’s supervisory processes require verification of implementation 
and effectiveness. Escalation is applied where risks increase and 
sanctions are criteria-based. 
Sugese lacks powers to suspend or remove directors, Senior 
Management or key function holders or to require insurers to do so, 
which reduces its leverage when insurers’ remedial actions prove 
insufficient (ICP 10.5; see also ICP 5). It also faces legal obstacles to 
the imposition of sanctions on individuals as provide d for in the IMRL 
and has been unable to take such action in practice.

Public 
12 – Exit from 
the Market and 
Resolution 
PO There are extensive provisions on the voluntary exit of insurers, which 
have been tested in practice. However, there is no resolution authority 
or resolution powers and processes (including a protection fund) for 
insurers. The approach to the failure of an insurer, which has not been 
tested in practice, would rely on general bankruptcy proceedings. 
Sugese would by law be involved in the initiation of such proceedings 
(although not in the process itself) , and there are requirements in 
insurance legislation g iving high pr iority to policyholders. Sugese is 
well equipped to cooperate with other authorities.  
However, the point at which Sugese would trigger bankruptcy is not 
well defined (ICP 12.7; see also ICP 17). In the absence of a framework 
for the supervision of systemically important insurers, Sugese has not 
undertaken resolution planning or otherwise prepared itself for the 
necessary actions (ICP s 12.3–12.4). Legislative change would be 
required to equip Sugese (assuming it  is the resolution authority and 
existing bankruptcy provisions are inadequate, at least for systemically 
important insurers) with the appropriate range of tools (ICP 12.8).   
13 – 
Reinsurance 
and Other 
Forms of Risk 
Transfer 
LO There are extensive and detailed regulatory requirements on 
reinsurance, covering the impact of reinsurance on insurers’ credit, 
concentration and liquidity risks ; RM and supervisory reporting 
requirements. Sugese actively supervises the effectiveness of the 
reinsurance risk management of the insurer, including reviewing 
contracts and programmes. An assessment of the home supervision of 
foreign reinsurers is not performed in detail, with reliance placed 
instead on the financial strength ratings of the reinsurer (ICP 13.4).  
14 – Valuation LO The current valuation methodology is prescriptive and underpinned by 
IFRS 4 accounting standards (IFRS 17 is scheduled to be implemented 
date for solvency purposes from 1 January 2028).  
The valuation of liabilities does not specify the use of a prospective 
approach based on projected future cash  flows. There is no 
requirement to use unbiased cash  flows or to include margins over 
current estimate (MOCE) (ICPs 14.6–14.8). There is also no allowance 
for the cost of options and guarantees in the valuation of insurance 
liabilities (ICP 14.10). However, the insurance market does not write 
significant quantities of complex, long-term life insurance products and 
there are no products containing options or guarantees. Sugese takes 
action when insurers are not reserving adequately. 
15 – 
Investments 
LO There are extensive, mostly qualitative requirements for solvency 
purposes, including on security, settlement, custody etc, as well as 
solvency requirements that incentivise a prudent investment approach. 
Quantitative requirements are limited but support a  minimum amount 
of investment in highest-quality, relatively liquid investments.

Public 
There are no quantitative requirements on group investment risks 
(ICP 15.1) or on investments in instruments that are more complex and 
less transparent (ICP 15.5). Insurers are not explicitly required to 
assess credit risk independent of the external credit rating or to 
consider the impacts of climate change on their investments (ICP 
15.2). Sugese does, however, address investment activities and risk in 
its supervisory work, taking into account the lack of significant long-
term business and concentration of  investment portfolios in 
government and other public sector bonds.  
16 – Enterprise 
Risk 
Management for 
Solvency 
Purposes 
PO There are many requirements, including for an ERM framework and for 
insurers to have investment, underwriting and liquidity policies ; a risk 
appetite statement and an RSSA, which contains elements of what is 
expected of an own risk and solvency assessment (ORSA) in ICP 16. 
Supervisory practice is thorough.  
However, there are limited requirements for quantitative tools and in 
respect to risk interdependencies ( ICPs 16.1–16.14) and a full ALM 
policy (ICP 16.5). The requirements on RSSAs do not extend to making 
them an integral part of strategic decision-making, capital management 
and the RM process, nor do they require insurers to build in a longer -
term perspective (ICPs  16.10–16.14). No pre -emptive recovery 
scenarios or options for recovery are required of insurers (ICP 16.15). 
In the future, Sugese, working with Conassif, intends to introduce 
measures to strengthen ERM requirements in the context of the 
planned development of solvency requirements (see ICPs 14 and 17).  
17 – Capital 
Adequacy 
PO There is an extensive framework , including requirements for insurers 
to calculate capital requirements for six risk types and to maintain a 
capital base (in mostly high-quality forms of capital) of at least 130% of 
the requirements. Although there are no insurance groups at present, 
a framework for group -wide capital requirements is in place (see ICP 
23).  
The framework has been revised periodically. It will be revised again 
to reflect the planned implementation of IFRS 17 and , in the longer 
term, reformed to provide for a more fully risk-based approach. Sugese 
is satisfied that current requirements provide adequately for insurers to 
be able to absorb unexpected losses.  
However, the current approach is not based on the full balance sheet 
(ICP 17.1), is backward-looking and lacks explicit target criteria for the 
calibration of requirements applied consistently across risks (ICP 17.8). 
It is not supported by a full framework of solvency control levels, lacking 
a defined minimum capital requirement (MCR) at which point strongest 
measures would be taken (ICPs 17.3–17.4).  
18 – 
Intermediaries 
PO There are extensive requirements on licensing and regulation of 
intermediaries. Sugese publishes a list of intermediaries showing their

Public 
licensing status and scope and any issued sanctions. There are 
extensive governance and financial requirements applying to 
intermediaries operating in corporate form and requirements on 
protection of client monies. Sugese also monitors, through an industry-

education and experience information on intermediaries.  
Ongoing supervision of intermediaries is limited owing to the lack of a 
risk-based conduct of business supervision framework and staff 
resource constraints, which prevent frequent and effective on -site 
evaluations (ICP 18.2; see also ICP 19). 
19 – Conduct of 
Business 
PO Laws and regulations provide for business conduct regulation and 
supervision. However, the lack of a risk -based business conduct 
supervision model equivalent to the approach taken to prudential and 
AML/CFT supervision means that supervision is limited overall and 
mainly reactive. It lacks  a proactive assessment of  conduct risk 
affecting fair treatment of consumers (ICPs 19.1, 19.2 and 19.4) and 
comprehensive regulations on product development and promotion 
(ICPs 19.5 and 19.6), contractual information and d isclosed 
circumstances (ICP s 19.7 and 19.8), policy servicing and claim 
handling (ICPs 19.9 and 19.10), complaints (ICP 19.11), protection of 
consumer information ( ICP 19.12) and disclosing specific conduct 
performance information that supports the fair treatment of customers. 
(ICP 19.13). 
There are no requirements that insurers and intermediaries take into 
account the consumer’s disclosed circumstances when evaluating their 
insurance demands and needs (ICP 19.8). 
20 – Public 
Disclosure 
PO Disclosure requirements are driven mainly by IFRS -based financial 
reporting requirements, greatly supported by materials published by 
Sugese on its website. Sugese regularly reviews the scope of this 
published information in response to user feedback . Insurers 
themselves make extensive disclosure on their websites.  
However, there are shortfalls  in requirements , including: on the 
objectives and policies of investment management (ICP 20.7); for 
investments, on the values, assumptions and methods used for 
general purpose financial reporting and solvency purposes (ICP 20.7); 
on information on ALM, including the measurement methodology, and 
on any capital or provisions held against mismatches (ICP 20.9); and 
on liquidity risk (ICP 20.11).

powers and administrative and criminal processes for taking action 
against insurance fraud offenses, it does not include good -faith 
reporting of fraud (ICP 21.1). Sugese does not have a comprehensive 
risk assessment of the sector’s fraud exposure (ICP 21.2) or a 
supervision framework for monitoring and enforc ing regulated legal

Public

Outreach and coordi nation are not conducted with regulated legal

22 – Anti-Money 
Laundering and 
Combating the 
Financing of

LO The AML/CFT regime is robust in law and applied through a functioning 
risk‑based programme. Sugese is active in domestic and international 
cooperation, including preparations for the next Financial Action Task 
Force of Latin America (GAFILAT) mutual evaluation in 2026. 
However, although supervisors have a good working understanding of 
risks in the insurance sector, at the time of the assessment there was 
no risk assessment for the insurance sector (ICPs 22.1–22.2) (a report 
by an external consultant aimed at fulfilling this need has since been 
received). AML/CFT oversight of intermediaries is limited (ICPs 22.3–
22.4). There is a need for more systematic follow -up on external 
AML/CFT audit reports and clearer feedback loops to insurers to 
ensure recommendations are impleme nted (ICP 22.4), as well as 
stronger escalation discipline and transparency in enforcement 
outcomes (ICP 22.5). 
23 – 
Group-wide 
Supervision 
O There are extensive provisions in law and regulations, developed to 
apply to all financial groups regulated by the superintendencies , 
including Sugese. They cover the identification of the group -wide 
supervisor and the determi nation of the scope of group -wide 
supervision. The framework applies to insurance groups.  
There is currently one domestic insurance group, but it is not regulated 
as such in practice and Sugese is therefore not currently exercising 
group-wide supervision over any insurance group . It would, however, 
be able to do so where necessary. It is already exercising consolidated 
supervision, under the framework for financial groups, in the case of 
one which is the parent of a group containing other financial entities.  
24 – 
Macroprudential 
Supervision 
PO Sugese identifies and analyses insurance market developments and 
environmental factors, including through its involvement in top -down 
stress tests led by the CBCR. It contributes to wider financial stability 
work, particularly through its involvement in th e work of the FSC . 
Sugese collects detailed information useful for macroprudential 
supervision and publishes comprehensive statistics. It has a good 
overview of potential risks to the stability of the sector (inward risks). It 
plans enhancements, including  the introduction of a systemic risk 
identification methodology for insurers and development of stress 
testing to include natural catastrophes. 
However, in common with  systemic importance (ICP 24.3), outward 
risks (ICP  24.2) are not yet fully assessed. Although significant 
attention is given to climate -related risks, a wide -ranging

ICP 1 Objectives, Powers and Responsibilities of the upervisor 
Each authority responsible for insurance supervision, its powers and the 
objectives of insurance supervision are clearly defined. 
Description Supervisory authority 
Primary legislation identifies Sugese and Conassif as the authorities for the 
supervision and regulation, respectively, of the insurance sector (Articles 28 –9 of 
the IMRL). Both are agencies attached to the CBCR , with administrative and

Public 
budgetary independence. Sugese, Conassif and the superintendencies responsible 
for supervision of other parts of the financial sector together make up the National 
Financial Supervision System (Articles 169–77 of the SMRL).  
The CBCR itself does not have powers to regulate or supervise the insurance 
sector, although it does conduct analysis of the sector, in cooperation with Sugese, 
under its wide-ranging financial stability mandate (ICP 24).  
Sugese’s mandate covers prudential and business conduct supervision as well as 
licensing and enforcement and making proposals on regulations to Conassif. 
Sugese operates under the direction of Conassif, which functions as its governing 
body (Article 28 of the IMRL) (see ICP 2).  
Conassif is responsible for issuing all regulations applicable to the insurance sector, 
including cross-cutting regulations (those applying to two or more sectors within the 
financial system) (Article 171 of the SMRL). It does not undertake supervision of 
the insurance sector , but receives reports from Sugese on developments in the 
sector (Article 131(i) of the CBCRL) and has certain intervention powers (see 
below).  
Conassif and Sugese also have regulatory and supervisory responsibility for 
insurance intermediaries (Articles 19–23 of the IMRL).  
Conassif’s and Sugese’s powers do not apply to one domestic insurer whose 
business is exempted from the IMRL (see ICP 4). There is no other oversight body 
for this insurer.  
Other bodies with responsibilities covering the insurance sector are: 
• The Commission for the Promotion of Competition (Coprocom), which by law 
must be notified of and seek Conassif’s advice on certain proposed mergers etc 
(and insurance portfolio transfers) in the financial system (ICP 6); 
• The CNC, the authority responsible for oversight of compliance with general 
consumer protection requirements, which may consider certain complaints from 
policyholders (ICP 19); and 
• The FIU, the national AML/CFT authority within the national drug authority, 
which receives and responds to reports from insurers and shares information 
with Sugese but which has no regulatory or supervisory role (AML/CFT 
requirements are set out in legislation and Conassif regulations; Sugese 
undertakes AML/CFT supervision) (ICP 22). 
Objectives 
The IMRL establishes objectives for the law, including objectives such as protecting 
the rights and interests of insureds and interested third parties under insurance 
policies, creati ng conditions for the development of the insurance market , and 
promoting effective competition amongst participating legal entities (Article 1).  
Sugese’s objectives (Article 29) include providing comprehensive information to 
policyholders and ensuring the stability and efficient functioning of the insurance 
market. Legal advice obtained by Sugese  supports its view that its objective for 
ensuring e fficient functioning of the insurance market implies achieving the 
objectives set out in the IMRL (Article 1).

Public 
There are no specific objectives of insurance regulation and supervision assigned 
by law to Conassif. However, its functions are defined in the SMRL to include a 
wide range of activities such as approving the rules for author isation, regulation, 
supervision etc for the sector and others (Article 171 of the SMRL).  
Although Sugese’s objectives include the stability of the insurance sector, there is 
no explicit objective (in the SMRL or IMRL) of contributing to financial stability more 
broadly, including in respect to the impact of the insurance sector on other parts of 
the financial sector and wider economy (see ICP 24).  
However, in practice, Conassif and Sugese cooperate with the CBCR and other 
superintendencies through the mechanism of the FSC (see Section 3 of this report). 
Sugese works with the CBCR’s Financial Stability Department in working groups of 
the FSC and on specific projects, for example insurance sector stress tests (see 
ICP 24).  
Powers 
Powers for insurance sector regulation and supervision are set out in the IMRL, 
SMRL and CBCRL.  
Sugese has powers to: 
• License insurers and intermediaries and to take action against unlicensed 
insurance activity (Articles 29 and 35 of the IMRL);  
• Conduct risk-based supervision (Article 30 of the IMRL); 
• Exchange information with other national and foreign supervisory bodies and 
participate in joint supervisory activities (Article 151 of the SMRL); 
• Require preventive and corrective actions and impose sanctions on insurers in 
case of non-compliance with provisions of laws and regulations (Articles 29 and 
35 of the IMRL and Article 131(e) of the CBCRL); 
• Withdraw a licen ce (Article 32 of the IMRL for insurers and Article 19 for 
intermediaries);  
• Propose to Conassif standards for accounting records and financial statements 
(Article 131(e) of the CBCRL) and require disclosure of information by insurers 
to the public (Article 25 of the IMRL); and 
• Determine the solvency of an insurer and report to the court in case of a petition 
for bankruptcy by creditors (Article 34 of the IMRL) (ICP 12). 
Sugese also has an explicit power to submit to Conassif, for approval, the 
regulations required for the application of the IMRL and “to fulfil its powers and 
functions” (Article 29 of the IMRL and Article 131(c) of the CBCRL). 
While it has a wide range of powers, Sugese does not have authority: 
• To require an insurer to remove a person subject to the suitability requirements 
when the person is found unsuitable (ICP 5); 
• To require the suspension or removal of one or more members of a Board of 
Directors, Senior Management or key persons in control functions (ICP 10); or 
• To approve all changes in control for an insurer (ICP 6).

Public 
Neither Sugese nor any other body has a full range of powers for resolution of an 
insurer, and there is no designated resolution authority (ICP 12).    
Conassif has powers to approve rules on Sugese’s authorisation, regulation and 
supervision activities. As mentioned, it is responsible for overseeing Sugese’s work 
and also appoints the Superintendent. It hears and resolves appeals against 
Sugese’s decisions (ICP 2).  
Under the SMRL, Conassif also has powers over insurers, for example to order the 
suspension of operations. In practice, Sugese generally takes such actions using 
its own powers under the more recently enacted IMRL.  
However, under Article 156 of the SMRL, Conassif may use the intervention powers 
provided for in the CBCRL (Articles 139 and 140, which apply to all financial 
sectors). It may decide, for example, to initiate an administration process at an 
insurer (see ICPs 10 and 12). Sugese has the power to request that Conassif use 
these powers (Article 131(h) of the CBCRL). 
Hierarchy of requirements 
Primary legislation is enacted by the Legislative Assembly. The executive branch 
of government may issue regulations or decrees applicable to insurers  when 
expressly indicated by law and has done so, for example, in the field of AML/CFT 
(ICP 22).  
As noted, Conassif is responsible for issuing regulations under relevant laws. Those 
applicable to the insurance sector only are issued as “Sugese Agreement s”, while 
cross-cutting regulations are issued as “Conassif Agreements”.  
Sugese can and does issue communications adding detailed requirements to 
regulations etc (for example on insurer solvency requirements) and setting out its 
interpretation of regulations etc (Superintendent Agreements, Circulars, 
Resolutions and Guidelines).  
Under Articles 25 and 26 of the IMRL, insurers and intermediaries must comply with 
all “rules and directives” issued by Sugese. 
Groups (see also ICP 23) 
While there are currently no regulated insurance groups, the regulatory framework 
includes powers for Conassif and Sugese in respect to group supervision.  
Article 8 of the IMRL provides for the framework of regulation of groups set out in 
Articles 140bis –50 of the CBCRL and Conassif regulations issued under these 
provisions to apply to insurance groups. Th is also appl ies to insur ers that are 
members of a financial group, whether as the head of such a group (as is the case 
for one insurer) or as a legal entity that is part of a group including another regulated 
Costa Rican financial entity (as is the case for three insurers). Sugese’s powers to 
supervise groups headed by an insurer are set out in Article 143 of the CBCRL.  
Changes in the legislative framework 
Sugese’s power to propose regulations encompasses changes to existing 
requirements. It may also propose changes to laws.

Public 
In practice, at the time of the assessment, neither Sugese nor Conassif had 
identified a need for legislative changes for the insurance sector in recent years. 
Changes to the regulatory framework have mainly been implemented by Conassif 
itself through regulations, in response to proposals from Sugese or as part of wider 
initiatives (for example on consolidated supervision in 2022; see ICP 23). These 
may be driven by the needs of other superintendencies, but they are applied to all 
sectors.  
In February 2026 (ie after the main assessment work), Sugese submitted to the 
Parliament proposed legislative measures aimed at strengthening the policy 
framework for the state-owned insurance company.  
Conassif and Sugese engage in legislative matters affecting insurance, for example 
by submitting opinions and giving evidence to committees in response to legislative 
proposals being considered by the Legislative Assembly.  
Assessment Largely Observed 
Comments Conassif and Sugese are clearly identified in legislation as the regulatory and 
supervisory authorities for the insurance sector, including intermediaries. There is 
no authority for one insurer whose business is exempted from the IMRL (ICP 1.1). 
The IMRL also sets out objectives, although Sugese’s objective in respect to 
financial stability is limited to the stability of the insurance sector (ICP 1.2).  
Conassif and Sugese have a wide range of powers to set requirements, exercise 
supervision (including of insurance and other groups) and enforce requirements 
through preventive and corrective measures as well as sanctions. However, 
Sugese lacks powers to require an insurer to remove persons subject to the 
suitability requirements and to approve all changes in control of an insurer. Neither 
Sugese nor any other body has a full range of powers for resolution of an insurer 
(ICP 1.3).  
It is recommended that the Government of Costa Rica: 
• Broadens Sugese’s financial stability objective to include contributing to 
financial stability work in line with practices; 
• Extends supervisory and resolution powers to fill current gaps; and 
• Extends the scope of insurance regulation to include the insurer currently 
subject to exemption.  
ICP 2 Supervisor 
The supervisor is operationally independent, accountable and transparent in 
the exercise of its responsibilities and powers, and has adequate resources 
to discharge its responsibilities. 
Description Independence from undue government or industry interference 
Conassif and Sugese are agencies attached to the CBCR, with administrative and 
budgetary independence (“maximum deconcentration bodies”) (Article 169 of the

Public 
SMRL and Article 28 of the IMRL). The CBCR is itself an autonomous public 
institution (Article 1 of the CBCRL). 
Conassif and Sugese (in practice , the Superintendent) are empowered to take 
regulatory and supervisory decisions (Article 171 of the SMRL and Article 29 of the 
IMRL) without reference to government.  
However, the Minister of Finance (or a Deputy) is a voting member of Conassif, 
although one of seven and not ex officio the chair (Article 169 of the SMRL). The 
Minister is also a (non-voting) member of the CBCR’s Board. 
The MEIC monitors compliance with  certain regulations issued by Conassif 
(Article 12 of the Law on Protection of Citizens from Excessive Requirements and 
Administrative Procedures (Law 8220)). The MEIC can issue recommendations to 
autonomous entities only regarding the requirements of Article 13, part 2 and Article 
14 of Law 8220 . The scope of the MEIC’s review is limited to regulations that 
impose binding requirements on individuals and to establishing whether proposed 
regulations meet requirements on improving and simplifying the regulatory 
framework.   
Neither Conassif nor Sugese receive funding from the government. Sugese has , 
until recently , been wholly funded by the CBCR , but fees are now charged to 
regulated legal entities (up to a maximum of 2% of annual income per entity). Their 
share of Sugese’s funding is being increased gradually until they account for 50% 
in 2027 (Articles 174–5 of the SMRL and Executive Decree 44705-H of 2024). The 
proceeds of administrative financial penalties go to the Ministry of Finance.   
Conassif’s funding is included in the fees payable by the supervised entities.  
Sugese’s annual budget is approved by Conassif. While the government has no 
direct role in establishing budgets, including that of Conassif, it has set rules that 
constrain spending growth. Current expenditure may increase in any year by no 
more than 65% o f average GDP growth over the previous four years (Law for 
Strengthening the Public Finances (Law 9635)).  
In addition, the Ministry of Planning and Economic Policy may decide on 
remuneration policies for public entities including Sugese (Article 7 of the 
Framework Law on Public Employment (Law 10159)).   
Although insurance sector participants are consulted on regulatory proposals, they 
have no role in decision-taking and there are no industry representatives amongst 
the membership of Conassif.  
Accountability 
Conassif is accountable to the CBCR and, as a part of the CBCR’s functions, to the 
Legislative Assembly. Sugese is similarly accountable, initially to Conassif. It is 
subject to internal audit work by Conassif and is accountable to the Office of the 
Comptroller General (the state audit authority) in respect to compliance with public 
sector financial requirements.  
Both Conassif and Sugese are required to submit a report to the Legislative 
Assembly annually by mid -May, setting out how they meet their responsibilities 
(Articles 1 and 2 of the Law for Improving Accountability (Law 9398)).

Public 
Legal protection 
There are no provisions in law providing protection for Conassif, Sugese or their 
staff from legal action in case of damages resulting from their decisions taken in 
good faith in the discharge of regulatory duties. No case for damages has been 
brought against Conassif or Sugese by an insurer or intermediary to date.   
There is a requirement in the SMRL for Conassif and Sugese to provide full legal 
defence of affected individuals in case of such legal action. Individuals are to be 
defended against civil or criminal liability lawsuits  concerning acts or conduct 
related to their duties, functions or powers (Article 177(2) of the SMRL).  
Governing body, appointments etc 
The members of Conassif are the Minister of Finance (or a Deputy), the President 
or General Manager of the CBCR and five members appointed by the Board of 
Directors of the CBCR. These may not be public officials (Article 169 of the SMRL), 
and they generally have academic and/or financial sector backgrounds.  
Members hold office for five years and may be reappointed only once. Conassif 
elects a chair from amongst its members to serve for two years (renewable).  
Sugese’s Superintendent is appointed by Conassif for a period of five years 
(renewable) (Article 28 of the IMRL). Conassif, although a separate agency, 
functions as Sugese’s governing body. The Superintendent may attend Conassif 
meetings, but it has no vote (Article 169 of the SMRL).  
Conassif members and the Sugese Superintendent are subject to the same 
requirements on qualifications, dismissal etc that apply to the Board of Directors of 
the CBCR (Article 170 of the SMRL and Articles 18 –24 of the CBCRL). They may 
be dismissed only for causes specified in the law, such as absence, incapacity and 
fraudulent acts. The Superintendent may be dismissed by Conassif for cause. No 
such dismissals have been made.  
Internal governance, processes etc 
Sugese has the power to issue rules and other technical and operational guidelines 
(Article 29 of the IMRL). Internal processes have been implemented on 
decision-taking on regulations (including on Sugese’s approach to making 
proposals to Conassif), licensing and supervision.  
Sugese operates under a five -year strategic plan ( the latest being 2024–28), 
updated annually, that sets out its pr iorities, objectives  and plans and their 
realisation.  
The CBCR has a Code of Ethics applicable to Conassif members and staff and to 
Sugese staff. It covers the handling of conflicts of interest, which must be reported. 
Sugese staff must submit an annual sworn declaration on conflicts of interest and 
notify supervisors in case of new conflicts. 
Application of requirements 
Sugese has processes and IT systems for managing work and carry ing out 
supervision on a consistent basis (see ICP 9).

Public 
Sugese has no general power to waive requirements. There are specific provisions 
in certain regulations allowing Sugese to vary its approach, for example to allow an 
insurer, in defined circumstances , to use an alternative methodology for 
compulsory technical provisions (Article 4 of Sugese Agreement 02-13).  
Sugese may request that an insurer make an additional capital contribution, based 
on identified risks (Sugese Agreement 10 -17). It modified regulations during the 
Covid-19 pandemic, with application across the market. 
Sugese’s supervision and the regulations issued by Conassif are applicable to the 
one state-owned insurer in the same way as to private insurers. 
Appeals 
Any decision by Sugese may be appealed (Articles 342 and 343 of the General 
Law of Public Administration (Law 6227), applied to Sugese by Articles 35 of the 
IMRL and 168 of the SMRL). Sugese’s actions may also be challenged, after or 
instead of going to Conassif, through judicial review proceedings applicable to 
public administrative bodies in general (Article 49 of th e Constitution and Article 1 
of the Contentious-Administrative Procedural Code (Law 8508)).  
Conassif has received between two to five appeals in the last three years, and one 
or two cases per year have brought to court. Most of these are pending resolution. 
No appeal or court action in this period has been resolved against Sugese.  
Appeals and judicial proceedings do not require suspension of the execution of the 
decision (Article 29 of Sugese Agreement 10 -17). However, the court or other 
authority may suspend execution if it could cause severe damage or irreparable 
harm (Article 21 of Law 8508 and Article 148 of Law 6227). Suspensions have 
occurred occasionally in practice.  
Protection of confidential information 
Disclosure of confidential information about regulated legal entities or their 
transactions is prohibited for Conassif members and all staff and advisers of 
Conassif and Sugese, even after ceas ing employment or other relationship. A 
violation is punishable under the Criminal Code and, for Sugese employees, by 
dismissal, without prejudice to other civil and criminal liabilities (Article 166 of the 
SMRL, applied to Sugese by Article 29 of the IMRL). 
Sugese has an internal policy on privacy and disclosure of information, including 
on the classification of documents and responsibilities of staff handling official 
communications etc. 
Transparency, publication and consultation  
The IMRL requires that Sugese act in a transparent and consistent manner and 
establishes as an objective that it provide s information to policyholders. Sugese 
sets out on its website institutional information and accountability reports, including 
work reports, evaluation of plans, budget execution etc.  
In addition to an annual report (Institutional Report), Sugese publishes an 
Accountability Improvement Law Report (as required by Law 9398) detailing its 
objectives, resources, organ isational structure, goals and results achieved etc. 
Conassif also publishes such a report. Sugese’s five -year strategic plan, annual

Public 
operational plan and budget are published on its website. It publishes the names 
and roles of all technical staff.  
Draft regulations are subject to a requirement for consultation with affected parties, 
including regulated legal entities (Article 361 of Law 6227). The consultation is sent 
to stakeholders directly by Conassif or Sugese , and published on the website. In 
practice, Conassif and Sugese consult extensively on their draft requirements, 
including Superintendent Agreements (see ICP 1). Details of comments received 
and how they have been addressed are also published.  
All applicable laws, regulations and other instruments (circulars etc) are published 
on or available via Sugese’s website.  
Adequacy of resources 
Conassif has a small staff to support its operations (eight, increasing soon to 11, 
mainly advisers) and internal auditors (18).  
Sugese’s budget t otalled CRC 4.32 billion (USD 9  million) in 2024. It has been 
increasing supervisory staff numbers in recent years and broadening its skillset, 
although specialists are limited to actuaries and IT experts at present and 
supervisors have a wide range of responsibilities in practice (see ICP 9).  
Staff totalled 60 (including four vacancies) at the end of 2024, an increase of 10 
since the end of 2022: 
Although Conassif is required to take the necessary measures to ensure that the 
remuneration of Sugese’s professional staff takes into account salaries in financial 
markets, it is subject to the constraints on remuneration in Law 10159, in force since 
March 2023.  
Sugese has extensive IT platforms for receiving, storing and analysing information 
reported by regulated legal entities; workflow management etc.  
Outsourcing 
Conassif and Sugese do not outsource supervisory work in practice.

Assessment Partly Observed 
Comments Conassif and Sugese enjoy a high degree of operational and financial 
independence from government, balanced by accountability and requirements for

Public 
transparency, including consultation with stakeholders. There are robust 
requirements on conflicts of interest and confidentiality of supervisory information. 
Decisions are open to appeal and are challenged in practice. Both Sugese and 
Conassif have been increasing resources.  
However, the Minister of Finance’s full participation in Conassif’s work (which 
contrasts with non-voting membership in the CBCR’s Board) is a potential channel 
of undue political interference (ICP 2.1), even if there is no evidence of such in 
practice to date. Both Conassif and Sugese lack protection in law from legal actions 
for damages resulting from their decisions taken in good faith (ICP 2.2).  
Government fiscal measures are constraining financial management and 
recruitment. While there is scope for an even more risk-based approach to the use 
of resources at Sugese, both agencies face challenges in managing existing 
workloads – especially in supervision – with current resources and are likely to be 
further constrained in the future (ICP 2.10).  
It is recommended that the Government of Costa Rica:  
• Strengthens the independence of Conassif (and thereby also Sugese) by 
removing the Minister from membership of Conassif; 
• Provides for protection from legal action for Conassif, Sugese and staff; and 
• Ensures that both agencies have adequate resources  to manage existing 
workloads and potential increased demand in the future, especially for 
supervisory work, as the insurance sector grows.  
ICP 3 Information Sharing and Confidentiality Requirements 
The supervisor obtains information from, and shares information with, 
relevant supervisors and authorities subject to confidentiality, purpose and 
use requirements. 
Description Information-sharing powers  
There is a clear legal foundation for supervisory cooperation:  
• Article 151 of the SMRL authorises the exchange of public and non -public 
information with domestic and foreign supervisors, requiring that Sugese sign 
cooperation and information exchange agreements (ie MoUs) that provide for 
reciprocity and confidentiality (paragraph 3);  
• Article 29 of the IMRL applies these powers specifically to the insurance sector; 
and 
• Article 142bis of the CBCRL empowers the lead supervisor of a financial group 
to access information from the holding company and its affiliates. 
Conassif regulations complement these laws with procedural rules. Conassif 
Agreement 07-19 sets out the process for information exchange between Sugese, 
other superintendencies and the FIU, requiring a legal basis, a supervisory purpose 
and confidentiality safeguards.

Public 
In addition, Sugese has issued Internal Guideline SGS -07-19 to operationalise 
these processes and specify secure transmission channels and response  time 
standards (acknowledgment within three days and substantive response within 
10 days); these are internal service standards rather than legal obligations.  
Conassif Agreement 16-22 makes the existence of an MoU with the other involved 
supervisor a condition for foreign insurers becoming part of a Costa Rican financial 
group.  
Sugese maintains confidentiality in line with the national data protection framework 
(Law on the Protection of Persons Regarding the Processing of Their Personal Data 
(Law 8968)) and applies the same safeguards to information shared internationally 
under MoUs or college arrangements. Statutory confidentiality provisions apply to 
Sugese staff and former staff, with breaches subject to criminal sanctions (see 
ICP 2). 
Framework of agreements  
Sugese has developed a wide network of cooperative arrangements:  
It has signed 11 bilateral MoUs with foreign supervisors, including with Mexico, 
although it lacks bilateral agreements with certain supervisors of the parent groups 
of Costa Rican subsidiaries where it is not a member of a supervisory college 
(Florida, United Kingdom, Spain, Jamaica). 
It participates in the Association of Insurance Supervisors of Latin America (ASSAL) 
MMoU, the Central American Council of Superintendents of Banks, Insurance and 
Other Financial Institutions (CCSBSO) MoU and the Central America and 
Caribbean sub-regional MoU. 
It has signed a four-party domestic MoU with Sugef, Sugeval and Supen.  
It is also an active member of supervisory colleges for five insurance groups, based 
in Spain, the United States, Panama, Colombia and Mexico , with operations in 
Costa Rica (ICP 25). It exchanges confidential information under college -specific 
MoUs.  
All of these arrangements contain common safeguards: information exchanged 
must be used strictly for supervisory purposes, is subject to confidentiality and 
reciprocity provisions, and cannot be disclosed further without prior consent of the 
originating supervisor. Together , they provide for Sugese’s integration into 
international, regional and domestic supervisory cooperation arrangements. 
Sugese is not currently a signatory to the IAIS MMoU, although it has made an 
application. 
Information requested and received by Sugese 
Requests made by Sugese to other superintendencies must comply with Conassif 
Agreement 07-19, Article 8 of which requires it to state the supervisory purpose, 
legal basis and confidentiality protections.  
Where no MoU is in place, Sugese obtains written undertakings to ensure 
equivalent safeguards. Although Conassif Agreement 07 -19 applies formally to 
exchanges with the other national superintendencies, in practice Sugese applies 
the same standards and processes when requesting or providing information to

Public 
other competent authorities, both domestic and foreign, consistent with its powers 
under Article 151 of the SMRL. 
Information received is handled under strict controls. It may be used only for 
supervisory purposes and cannot be disclosed without the provider’s consent. 
Internally, Sugese has adopted service standards under which requests are 
normally acknowledged within two to three business days and a substantive 
response is provided within about 10 business days, unless otherwise agreed with 
the counterpart. These are internal commitments rather than legal or MoU 
obligations. The only binding timelines in the formal instruments are the 
requirement in Conassif Agreement 07-19 to notify another superintendency within 
three days if disclosure is impossible and the ASSAL MM oU’s requirement to 
acknowledge within five business days and respond “without unjustified delay”. 
Information requested from and provided by Sugese 
Requests addressed to Sugese are reviewed by its legal department to confirm 
supervisory purpose, legal basis and confidentiality protections. Article 9 of 
Conassif Agreement 07-19 allows for information to be shared periodically with 
other superintendencies either in response to ad hoc requests or proactively. 
For requests from other domestic authorities or foreign supervisors outside the 
scope of Conassif Agreement 07-19, Sugese follows the same process in practice, 
consistent with its powers and confidentiality obligations under Article 151 of the 
SMRL. 
Information provided remains under Sugese’s control and cannot be disclosed 
further without its written consent. Where Sugese receives a legally enforceable 
demand to disclose information obtained from another authority, Article 151 of the 
SMRL and the confidentiality clauses of its MoUs require it to notify the originating 
supervisor and, where legally possible, to resist disclosure.  
All MoUs signed by Sugese contain provisions governing third -party or court 
requests for confidential information, setting out processes for notification and 
coordination with the originating authority. In practice, no such situations have 
arisen.  
Assessment Largely Observed 
Comments There is a well -established framework for information -sharing, subject to 
confidentiality requirements. The legislative basis is reinforced by Conassif 
processes, while a broad network of bilateral, regional and multilateral agreements 
provides effective channels for exchange of confidential information. Domestically, 
cooperation across the financial system is supported by the four-party MoU. 
Information exchange operates effectively where Sugese has signed an MoU. 
Requests are specific, confidentiality safeguards are consistently applied , and 
internal service standards provide discipline in handling requests. Statutory secrecy 
and technical safeguards strengthen the framework. 
Sugese has applied to become a signatory to the IAIS MMoU. While not required 
by ICP 3, this would facilitate Sugese’s ability to exchange confidential information

Public 
widely, with safeguards, for example in response to ad hoc requests for information 
and where it is not a member of a supervisory college for a foreign or foreign-owned 
insurer.    
It is recommended that Sugese: 
• Signs the IAIS MMoU to complete alignment with the global standard for 
information exchange; 
• Formalises a written process for verifying counterpart confidentiality 
safeguards; 
• Monitors and report s performance against Sugese’s service standards both 
internally and, in aggregate form, through the annual report; and 
• Publishes a list of all MoUs, including, where possible, supervisory college 
MoUs to which Sugese is a signatory , with a summary of details including the 
counterpart authorities and year of signature.  
ICP 4 Licensing 
A legal entity which intends to engage in insurance activities must be 
licensed before it can operate within a jurisdiction. The requirements and 
procedures for licensing must be clear, objective and public, and be 
consistently applied. 
Description Under the IMRL and Sugese Agreement 01-21, licences may not be granted solely 
for life insurance. Insurers are all either non-life or composite (covering both life and 
non-life insurance). Licen ces are issued in three categories: personal insurance 
(which includes life, income, accident and health), general insurance (property) , 
and mixed insurance ( composite insurers covering both personal and general 
insurance). 
The licensing process for insurers starts with an authorisation phase (Articles 4 –6 
of Sugese Agreement 01-21). In this process, under Article 30, Sugese evaluates 
the proposed insurer’s business plan, ownership structure, suitability and solvency. 
After authorisation, the insurer (and its proposed products) go es forward to 
registration (for the purposes of this assessment, licensing is regarded as a 
combination of authorisation and registration). 
There were 12 insurers and one proposed new insurer in the application process 
at the time of the assessment. 
Licensing requirements 
Insurance and reinsurance activity is defined in Articles 2 and 3 of the IMRL, which 
requires that the public offering of insurance and the business of insurance be 
licensed by Sugese. Article 7 allows corporations, branch offices of foreign insurers 
and cooperative insurers whose exclusive purpose is to carry out insurance activity 
with their members to apply for licensing.  
Under Article 2, certain legal entities are excluded from the requirement:

Public 
• Mandatory social security systems administered by the Costa Rican Social 
Security Fund (CCSS); 
• Special pension schemes created by law; and 
• The mandatory mutual policy administered by the National Teachers’ Life 
Insurance Society (SSVNM), whose business is restricted to this policy but 
which (because of the large number of teachers and other educators) is one of 
the largest providers of life insurance. 
Cross-border insurance activity into Costa Rica is allowed when permitted under a 
current inter national treaty (Article 16 of the IMRL and Article 55 of Sugese 
Agreement 01-21). However, insurance entities operating on a cross-border basis 
must be registered with Sugese (authorisation, ie the full licensing process, is not 
required). (The only registered provider to date is an insurer that only provides 
insurance-related services and does not write insurance business.) Reinsurance, 
retrocession, intermediation and ancillary services may be provided cross -border 
without registration or authorisation. 
Foreign insurance entities may operate through branches. There currently is 
one licensed foreign insurer operating as a branch. No applications for new 
branches have been submitted under the requirements introduced in 2021 (Sugese 
Agreement 01-21).  
Article 42 of the IMRL provides sanctions for unlicensed activity. 
Sugese Agreement 01 -21 outlines the processes, requirements and assessment 
criteria for the authorisation and registration processes. Once the author isation 
requirements are completed, the insurer then completes the registration process 
for placement on Sugese’s public registry. There is no requirement for renewal of 
licences except for cross-border providers. 
Regulations governing authorisation and registration are published in the Official 
Gazette and on Sugese’s website. 
Licence applications 
Sugese is responsible for licensing insurers (Article 29 of the IMRL). Appendix 3 of 
Sugese Agreement 01-21 sets out the required information and documentation to 
support applications. Chapter II of Sugese Agreement 01-21 establishes processes 
for handling authorisation applications, including the assessment criteria on which 
Sugese must base its decisions. Chapter IV establishes the minimum requirements 
for authorisation:  
• Transparency of the ownership structure and source of funding : Sugese may 
request additional documentation about shareholders with significant holdings 
(Article 30 of Sugese Agreement 01-21).  
• The business plan must be reasonable given the characteristics of the target 
market and must also be consistent with the financial statements and 
projections. The governance framework must also be appropriate to the plan, 
which should also reflect capital adequacy and sound prudent management of 
the legal entity’s activities, in alignment with corporate governance and RM 
regulations (Article 31).

Public 
• The academic, professional and moral qualifications of Senior Management, 
key persons in control functions and significant owners are evaluated for 
suitability (Article 32).  
• The proposed quality of capital instruments must meet regulatory requirements 
to be considered part of the insurer’s capital (Articles 26 and 33). Applicants are 
also required to deposit the minimum required capital at the CBCR (Article 11 
of the IMRL). 
Timelines and scope of licence 
Chapters II and III of Sugese Agreement 01 -21 dictate time  frames for licensing 
authorisation and registration requests, both for Sugese and for the applicant, 
including the possibility o f the applicant request ing an extension. Chapter II sets 
the authorisation work required of Sugese, including: 
• Analysis of the completeness of documentation: Sugese has 15 business days 
for this stage, with the possibility of a single request for additional information, 
(such a request would restart the 15-day deadline). 
• Substantive analysis of the documentation ahead of decision-making: Sugese 
has 30 business days, with the possibility of a single request for additional 
information, which suspends the deadline. This stage ends with the decision 
on whether to authorise the establishment of the legal entity. 
• If authorisation is granted, the next step is fulfilling the registration 
requirements within two months. Applicants have a maximum of one year from 
the date of the authorisation to complete the registration process and begin 
operations (Articles 22 and 24–5 of Sugese Agreement 01-21). 
• An application for registration by a cross -border insurance provider must be 
considered within 15 business days, with the possibility o f Sugese making a 
single request for additional information, which suspends the deadline. 
• Once any request for additional documentation has been met, the authorisation 
application must be resolved by Sugese within 30 days (Article 17 of Sugese 
Agreement 01-21). Each stage concludes with an official communication to the 
applicant informing them of Sugese’s decision, through either a formal letter or 
a resolution signed by the Superintendent. 
Article 19 of Sugese Agreement 01 -21 sets the grounds for denial of a licensing 
authorisation or registration. Denied registration requests are communicated 
through a resolution. 
Article 24 of Sugese Agreement 01-21 grants the authority to establish specific or 
additional registration requirements for an applicant, which must be stated in the 
conditional authorisation resolution. These conditions may be drawn from 
requirements established in other regulations that are necessary to begin 
operations or may substitute a requirement for which an exemption has been 
granted.  
Scope 
Licences issued by Sugese state the authorised categories of insurance.

Public 
For registration of cross -border insurers, the request letter must specify the 
provisions of the specific treaty under which the legal entity seeks to operate. The 
registration acceptance letter defines the potential insurance activities allowed. 
Lists of insurers etc 
The IMRL requires Sugese to maintain public records of approved authorisations 
and registrations. The content of the records is regulated under Article 6 of Sugese 
Agreement 01-21. The records are available on the Sugese website, which lists 
licences approved for insurers and their scope.  
Processes for foreign insurers 
Matters related to the authorisation of branches of foreign insurers and their 
activities are set out in Article 7 of the IMRL and Articles 4 and 5 and Appendix 3 of 
Sugese Agreement 01-21.  
In respect of applications by foreign insurers to establish a branch, it is a condition 
of licensing in Article 31 of Sugese Agreement 01 -21 that insurers be regulated in 
their home jurisdiction and authorised to carry out the proposed operations in Costa 
Rica. In addition, Article 43 of Sugese Agreement 01 -21 requires that the home 
jurisdiction supervisor state that it does not object to the establishment of a branch 
in Costa Rica and express its consent to sign an information exchange agreement 
with Sugese. However, this provision does not apply in the case of applications by 
foreign insurers to establish a subsidiary rather than a branch in Costa Rica. 
In practice, for subsidiaries, Sugese has sought input from the parent insurer’s 
supervisor (and then establishe d MoUs; see ICP 3), but it is not a condition of 
licensing as it is for branch es. If Sugese is unable to obtain input from the foreign 
insurer’s other involved supervisor, it may not be able to decline authorisation on 
that ground as easily as it could for a branch application.  
Assessment Partly Observed 
Comments Legislation clearly sets out a requirement for insurers to be licen sed and the 
application processes, with Sugese as the licensing authority. There are extensive 
licensing criteria and , in practice , Sugese evaluates applications and has both 
denied and approved recent applications. Sugese publishes a list of licensed legal 
entities and the scope of the licence.    
The exemption in law of the mandatory life insurance for teachers means that 
one large insurer, which may provide only this class of insurance, is not licensed 
by Sugese under the IMRL or otherwise regulated , nor are there any other 
appropriate alternative safeguards for policyholders (ICP 4.1).  
Sugese lacks the ability to reject licen ce applications from subsidiaries of foreign 
insurers in cases in which it has not been able to obtain input from the other involved 
supervisors of the parent insurer. This is a condition of licensing only for branches 
of foreign insurers (ICP 4.8). Sugese would always seek such input in practice.  
It is recommended that:

Public 
• The Government of Costa Rica amends the insurance legislation to ensure that 
the provision of mandatory life insurance for teachers is subject to licensing and 
supervision; and 
• Conassif extends its licensing requirements to align the treatment of branches 
and subsidiaries of foreign insurers by making it a condition of licensing that 
Sugese receive appropriate communication from the other involved supervisor 
of the parent.  
ICP 5 Suitability of Persons 
The supervisor requires Board Members, Senior Management, Key Persons 
in Control Functions and Significant Owners of an insurer to be and remain 
suitable to fulfil their respective roles. 
Description Suitability requirements  
Legislation and regulations set clear standards for the integrity and competence of 
relevant persons. Article 9 of the IMRL requires members of governing bodies to 
have recognised integrity and proven technical competence, while Article 29 
empowers Sugese to intervene when these standards are not met, including by 
requiring information or imposing administrative measures. 
Conassif Agreement 15-22 requires insurers to establish and maintain a suitability 
policy covering the governing body and Senior Management. This policy must 
include documented processes for initial and ongoing assessments and clearly 
assigned responsibilities (Article 3).  
Conassif Agreement 04-16 establishes the wider governance framework, including 
expectations for Board profile, conduct standards and oversight duties, as well as 
the Board’s obligation to ensure that appointments, retention and succession 
planning maintain both individual and collective suitability (Articles 3, 10–11, 17, 22 
and 30). 
The need for collective suitability is emphasised through requirements on Board 
composition, independence and balance of skills and experience ( Articles 17 and 
22 of Conassif Agreement 04 -16). Insurers must define role-specific criteria and 
apply internal checks and attestations proportionate to the role.  
Scope 
Suitability standards extend to significant owners, all members of the governing 
body, Senior Management and key persons in control functions.  
Articles 30 and 32 and Annexes 1 and 3 of Sugese Agreement 01-21 specify the 
roles that must be assessed for suitability at the time of licensing, including the 
general proxy for branches; the general manager and deputies; and the designated 
heads of the audit, compliance, RM, financial analysis and actuarial functions (see 
ICP 4). Ongoing suitability assessments are addressed under Conassif 
Agreement 15-22. 
Conassif Agreement 15 -22 applies suitability requirements across groups and 
conglomerates, including holding companies (Articles 1 and 2.5). For branches of

Public 
foreign insurers, the general proxy and local role  holders must meet Costa Rican 
suitability standards and maintain documentation accessible to Sugese. 
Sugese has issued Superintendent Agreement SGS-A-077-2021, setting out a full 
list of positions to be reported and processes for doing so.  
Fit and proper requirements  
The framework balances integrity, competence and, for significant owners, financial 
soundness. The IMRL requires governing body members to have integrity and 
technical competence (Article 9). Conassif Agreement 04-16 sets competence and 
conduct expectations and provides independence and conflict  of interest 
safeguards (Articles 17, 22, 30, 32–3 and 38). 
Article 30 of Sugese Agreement 01-21 requires integrity and financial soundness 
checks for significant owners. Articles 4 and 9 –11 of Conassif Agreement 15 -22 
require consistent application of integrity standards across groups. For key control 
functions, Articles 25 and 27 of Sugese Agreement 09 -17 define role -specific 
criteria, independence safeguards and access rights. 
Competence must be commensurate with the role, based on qualifications and 
relevant recent experience. Insurers are expected to maintain CVs, declarations 
and evidence of CPD for key function holders, as well as independence and conflict 
of interest declarations for directors and members of the Board’s audit, risk and 
remuneration committees. For owners, financial soundness checks cover the origin 
and stability of funds and the absence of encumbrances that could compromise 
management. 
Supervisory assessment 
Suitability is tested at licensing and when material changes occur. Sugese 
Agreement 01-21 (Annexes 1 and 3) specifies the documentary and verification 
requirements: identification, qualifications, experience, integrity declarations, 
criminal record checks and evidence of financial soundness for owners. 
Conassif Agreement 15 -22 requires individual and collective assessments for 
Boards and Senior Management, with files available to the supervisor  
(Articles 12–13). Supervisory practice includes reviewing appointment files, Board 
self-assessments and related action plans. Where weaknesses are identified, these 
are followed up on in supervisory letters and tracked to closure. 
For subsidiaries of foreign insurance groups, Sugese may rely on assessments by 
other involved supervisors, subject to local verification and its accountability for the 
Costa Rican operation. For branches of foreign insurers, Sugese assesses whether 
local role  holders meet the standards and maintain locally accessible 
documentation, as required. 
Obligations of insurers, including changes/circumstances that may 
adversely affect suitability 
Insurers must notify Sugese of changes in ownership and role holders (Article 50(a) 
of Sugese Agreement 01-21) and update files through designated online services. 
Material facts must be reported under Article 2 of Sugese Agreement 12-21.

Public 
Internal policies must cover pre -appointment screening, probation, training and 
periodic reassessment, with documentation readily available to supervisors. 
The framework does not, however, include an explicit obligation to report adverse 
events that could affect suitability, such as indictments, regulatory bans, bankruptcy 
petitions or sanctions listings. This creates ambiguity around timeliness and 
evidence when issues arise between scheduled reassessments. However, this duty 
may effectively exist within the scope of Sugese Agreement 12 -21 (Articles 6 and 
8) which sets an expectation that entities disclose such information under “Other 
Material Events”.  
Corrective action where a person is no longer fit and proper 
Insurers are required to prevent unsuitable appointments or retention of unsuitable 
individuals (Article 3 of Conassif Agreement 15-22) and must implement corrective 
programmes when weaknesses are identified. Sugese can recommend removal or 
changes (Article 29 of the IMRL, Article 131(o) of the CBCRL and Article 22(e) of 
Sugese Agreement 10 -17). It can apply corrective measures against the legal 
entity. Sanctions are available for non-compliance. 
However, Sugese does not have statutory powers to suspend or remove individuals 
directly (or to require an insurer to do so) or to declare a role temporarily vacant. (It 
has power under Article 29 of the IMRL and Article 131 (o) of the CBCRL to 
recommend the removal of any member of the governing body for cause, including 
when they fail to meet the suitability requirements.) If a regulated legal entity fails 
to act, enforcement relies on indirect measures against the entity.  
Exchanges with other supervisors 
Sugese has a clear legal basis and MoU channels for requesting and shar ing 
information with foreign supervisors for suitability checks (integrity, competence) 
and participates as an involved supervisor in colleges for foreign-owned insurers. 
The framework enables domestic and cross -border information exchange on 
suitability issues under confidentiality safeguards. Under Article 29 of the IMRL and 
Article 151 of the SMRL (see ICP 3), Sugese may exchange all types of information 
and undertake joint supervisory activity. Powers on consolidated supervision in the 
CBCRL (Article 144) support cooperation for financial groups.  
Candidates for roles must authorise background checks with national and 
international authorities as part of suitability due diligence ( Annex 3, 
Section II.A.2(b) of Sugese Agreement 01 -21 and Article 10 of Superintendent 
Agreement SGS -A-077-2021). MoUs facilitate routine exchanges with foreign 
supervisors on suitability issues.  
Assessment Partly Observed 
Comments The suitability regime is well developed in law and regulation and is applied through 
risk-based supervision. The design captures individual and collective suitability and 
sets clear documentary and verification expectations across insurers, groups and 
branches. In practice, s upervisors review appointment files and Board 
self-assessments and track corrective actions.

Public 
However, there is no explicit duty to report adverse events affecting suitability 
(ICP 5.4), creating ambiguity over required timeliness and minimum evidence. 
There is also no statutory power for Sugese to order the suspension or removal of 
unsuitable individuals (or to require insurers to take such action ), which reduces 
leverage when regulated legal entities do not act promptly (ICP 5.5). 
It is recommended that: 
• Sugese defines an explicit duty to report adverse events affecting suitability 
(eg indictments, sanctions listing, bankruptcy petitions, regulatory bans) within 
a defined time frame, with minimum evidence and an escalation path; 
• The Government of Costa Rica make s legislative changes to provide a power 
for Sugese to suspend or remove directors, Senior Management or key persons 
in control functions (or to require insurers to do so) when they are no longer fit 
and proper, including interim measures such as temporary vacancy 
declarations; and 
• Sugese strengthen s ongoing suitability assessment by requiring biennial 
reconfirmation of competence and integrity for in -scope roles (including CPD 
logs for key functions) and by sampling files during supervision; Boards should 
minute outcomes and action plans. 
ICP 6  Change of Control and Portfolio Transfers 
The supervisor assesses and decides on proposals: 
• To acquire significant ownership of, or an interest in, an insurer that 
results in a person (legal or natural), directly or indirectly, alone or with 
an associate, exercising control over the insurer; and 
• For portfolio transfers. 
Description Definition of control 
The regulatory framework establishes a definition of control and requirements on 
changes in control, including notification requirements but not including a 
requirement that Sugese approve all changes in control. 
Article 3 of Conassif Agreement 16-22 defines effective control of an insurer as “the 
ability of a natural or legal person to influence a company or entity through the 
exercise of ownership rights or rights of use over all or part of the assets, or through 
agreements that grant substantial influence over the composition, voting or 
decisions of the governing, administrative or legal representative bodies”. 
This effective control is evidenced when a natural person, legal entity or other 
structure: 
• Holds direct or indirect ownership of more than 50% of the share capital; or 
• Holds less than 50% of the share capital and exerts influence over management 
and policies that affect the insurer’s decision-making or has the authority to 
appoint or remove any member of the corporate governing body.

Public 
In relation to licensing (but not for the purposes of ongoing supervision), Sugese 
must verify information on the ownership structure of the applicant. It may request 
additional documentation about shareholders with significant holdings , defined as 
those holding, directly or indirectly, 10% or more of the share capital or the highest 
holding without exceeding that percentage (Sugese Agreement 01-21). 
Oversight and enforcement of requirements related to change of control 
Sugese does not have authority to approve changes in control of insurers once they 
are authorised, except in the case of a merger. Although it has extensive powers 
under Article 29 of the IMRL, it lacks power to approve changes of control because 
the relevant part of the CBCRL setting out the powers available to 
superintendencies does not apply to Sugese (Article 131(n) of the CBCRL). Change 
of control is therefore not included in the list of authorisable activities set out in 
Article 4 of Sugese Agreement 01-21.  
However, a change of control in the context of a merger of insurers (or of any 
regulated financial institutions) is subject to authorisation (Article 4 of Sugese 
Agreement 01-21 Appendix 7 sets out the processes to be followed).  
There are provisions in competition law for exchange of information and 
coordination between Sugese, Conassif and the competition authority, Coprocom 
(Article 27bis of the Law for the Promotion of Competition and Effective Consumer 
Protection (Law 7472) and Articles 88 and 89 of the Law for the Strengthening of 
Competition Authorities in Costa Rica (Law 9736)).  
Conassif Agreement 09-21 sets out the processes for mergers between regulated 
financial institutions, establishing the scope of prudential and business conduct 
analysis (including solvency, governance and owners) that Conassif and Sugese 
must carry out (Articles 16 and 17).  
Notification of change of control 
Insurers are required to notify Sugese when they become aware of changes in 
shareholding control. They must make a notification through Sugese’s online Roles 
Service, identifying the new individuals or legal entities forming part of the 
ownership structure, and also report such changes to Sugese as a material event 
(Article 7 of Sugese Agreement 12 -21, with ac companying interpretations in 
Superintendent Agreements SGS-A-077-2021 and SGS-A-0079-2021).   
Demutualisation and conversion of insurers 
There are currently no mutual insurers (although cooperative insurers are eligible 
for authorisation; see ICP 4) and no provisions for conversion from one legal form 
to another. 
Portfolio transfers  
It is a requirement that insurers notify Sugese of proposed portfolio transfers and 
that Sugese give prior authorisation for the transfer of insurance portfolios (Sugese 
Agreement 12-21 and Article 25(b) of the IMRL). The most recent portfolio transfer 
was processed by Sugese in 2022, and since 2008 there have been two such 
transfers in total.

Public 
Article 54 of Sugese Agreement 01 -21 sets out detailed requirements on the 
transfer process, including the required submissions to Sugese on due diligence 
studies conducted for the transfer, such as analysis of the impact on market share, 
financial results and solvency of both legal entities involved. Part II of Appendix 8 
of the Agreement outlines the documents to be provided by the transferring insurer 
(the transfer is treated as a cessation of operations for which a detailed plan, 
including mechanisms to guarantee compliance with the insurer’s obligations to 
insurance consumers, is always required).  
Where a branch of a foreign insurer is involved, documentation showing that the 
other involved supervisor did not object is required.  
As noted above (in respect to approvals of mergers of regulated financial 
institutions), there are also provisions in competition law for exchange of 
information and coordi nation between Sugese, Conassif and the competition 
authority, Coprocom. These also apply to portfolio transfers between insurers.  
In particular, insurers must first notify Coprocom of a qualifying transaction , and 
Coprocom then must request an opinion from Conassif on whether Conassif and 
Sugese should exclusively process the transfer because of the risks to the 
solvency, soundness or stability of the legal entities involved or the financial system.  
In practice, in respect to a portfolio transfer the details of which were reviewed for 
the purposes of the assessment, an extensive study was carried out by Sugese 
and the transaction was also reviewed by Coprocom before the transfer was 
approved.  
Assessment Partly Observed  
Comments Legislation establishes requirements and processes for certain changes of control 
and for portfolio transfers. Notification requirements apply to insurers in respect to 
proposed changes of control, but there is no requirement to always seek Sugese 
approval and Sugese lacks powers to approve such transactions (ICP 6.1). Sugese 
must approve all portfolio transfers, which it most recently completed for a 2022 
transfer. 
There are provisions in competition law for exchange of information and 
coordination between Sugese, Conassif and the competition authority, which 
empower Conassif and Sugese to approve changes of control arising from mergers 
specifically (under requirements applying to all mergers of regulated financial 
institutions).  
Existing laws and regulations therefore do not provide for Sugese to approve all 
changes of control of insurers. Nor are approvals based upon a clear definition of 
control aligned with notification requirements , applying materiality thresholds and 
criteria for assessing the suitability of owners with significant holdings  (ICPs 6.1 
and 6.2). 
It is recommended that the Government of Costa Rica amend relevant legislation 
to provide for all changes of control, broadly defined, to be subject to prior approval 
by Sugese.

ICP 7 Corporate Governance 
The supervisor requires insurers to establish and implement a corporate 
governance framework which provides for sound and prudent management 
and oversight of the insurer’s business and adequately recognises and 
protects the interests of policyholders. 
Description Overall framework 
There is a well-defined framework of laws and regulations. The IMRL establishes 
obligations for insurers and gives Sugese necessary supervision powers. The 
SMRL empowers Conassif to issue system-wide standards that Sugese applies to 
insurers. Key requirements are:   
• Conassif Agreement 04 -16, which sets out the Board’s responsibilities and 
required committees and sets requirements on conflict of interest policies and 
the periodic review of the governance framework;  
• Sugese Agreement 09 -17, which sets out requirements including on core 
control functions and the three lines of defence; and 
• Conassif Agreement 15 -22, which sets ongoing suitability and performance 
expectations. 
The enforceability of this framework relies on reporting and disclosure 
requirements, including those set out in Sugese Agreement 12 -21, as well as 
Conassif Agreements 06 -18 and 01 -10, which govern financial reporting and 
external audits.  
In supervision, Sugese includes governance issues in its risk-based programme of 
planned “studies”, including off-site and on-site supervisory work, and follows up on 
preventive and corrective actions required of insurers (ICPs 9 and 10). 
Structure, governance and functions of the Board 
Insurers adopt the unitary Board system that is general practice in Costa Rica. 
Article 9 of the IMRL requires a minimum of five Board members, who must meet 
standards of integrity and technical suitability, and sets independence safeguards 
(including a requirement that 40% of Board members are not shareholders or 
related to shareholders or employees of the same group). 
Conassif Agreement 04-16 then sets out requirements on the Board’s profile and 
composition, the characteristics of independent members and the appointment 
process, and assigns the Board explicit responsibility for strategy, risk appetite, 
policies and the committee system (Articles 5–9). Members of the Board must fulfil 
duties of care and loyalty to the insurer (Article 7).  
Conassif Agreement 15 -22 overlays this with requirements for a documented 
suitability policy (Article 3) and for a two -year reassessment of the suitability of 
persons within scope (Article 12) (see ICP 5). 
Sugese assesses how these requirements have been implemented in practice 
through supervision work. Minutes and Board packs are reviewed, directors

Public 
interviewed where warranted and corrective actions required, with follow-up by the 
insurer then tracked in Sugese’s supervisory workflow system (ICP 9). 
Article 21 of Conassif Agreement 04 -16 requires the governing body to conduct 
annual reviews of its structure, its composition, member performance and the 
effectiveness of its governance practices, which may be undertaken with the 
assistance of external experts. Although external review is not mandatory (and is 
not required under ICP 7.3), encouraging Boards to incorporate periodic external 
input into these reviews would strengthen the existing framework.  
Corporate culture, business objectives and strategies of the insurer 
Boards are required to set tone and direction and to keep the governance 
framework under review. Conassif Agreement 04-16 requires Boards to implement 
a robust corporate culture (codes of conduct, risk culture and sanctions for 
breaches), set strategic objectives aligned with risk appetite and periodically review 
the governance framework (Articles 10–14). 
Supervisors evaluate insurers’ implementation in practice using Sugese’s 
structured internal risk assessment framework (the General Guide to Risk Profile 
Assessment; see ICP 9) and supporting templates. They test the linkage between 
strategy, risk appetite and risk limits and look for evidence in Board materials and 
minutes that expectations are being met in practice.  
External audit 
Audit requirements are specified in detail and supervised by Sugese in practice. 
Conassif Agreement 01 -10 sets out the requirement that annual financial 
statements be audited , and Conassif Agreement 04 -16 sets out the Board audit 
committee’s duties, including a requirement to review audited financial statements 
before Board approval (Article 25).  
Sugese Agreement 09-17 confirms that it is a Board responsibility to appoint and 
oversee the external auditor and sets out requirements for the auditor’s access to 
the board. The Board is required to ensure that the external auditor brings an 
independent view to the insurer and that it has adequate resources and exercises 
due diligence in its work and reports findings to the Board (Article 29). 
Examples of supervisory documents reviewed for this assessment show that 
Sugese checks the timeliness of audit work, including Board review, and follows up 
on points set out in the auditor’s management letter with both the Board audit 
committee and the full Board.  
Duties of Senior Management 
The regulations make it clear that Senior Management is the first line of defence. 
They are expected to carry out the Board’s strategy, keep information systems 
reliable and relevant, act prudently and fairly, and raise any major issues with the 
Board when they arise ( Articles 29–31 of Conassif Agreement 04-16 and Articles 
7–8 of Sugese Agreement 09-17). 
Supervisors look closely at the quality of reporting to the board, evaluating whether 
there has been not just reporting of financial information etc but that Senior

Public 
Management is exposed to challenge by the board. The supervisory framework has 
been tested over time.  
There is scope for supervisors to document more fully how they have assessed the 
effectiveness of Board oversight of Senior Management, in particular the extent and 
nature of the challenge provided by the Board and the quality of the resulting 
discussion. Supervisory focus in this regard could increasingly centre on insurers’ 
RSSAs and stress  testing, where the quality of Board engagement provides 
valuable evidence of governance effectiveness in practice.  
Key persons in control functions 
Sugese Agreement 09 -17 mandates the RM, compliance, actuarial and internal 
audit functions, with appropriate authority, independence and access to the Board 
(see ICP 8). 
Conassif Agreement 04 -16 makes the Board and its committees responsible for 
oversight of these functions. In practice, s upervisors verify mandates, reporting 
lines and resourcing , and the work includes interviews with heads of control 
functions. Regulations could more explicit ly state that named “key persons” are 
appointed for each control function, with authority, independence and resources 
documented. In practice, Sugese already tests for these elements. 
Remuneration 
Conassif Agreement 04-16 requires a board-approved remuneration policy aligned 
with the insurer’s risk appetite and long-term sustainability, typically overseen by a 
remuneration/compensation committee. Implementation is reviewed during 
supervision, which focuses on policy approval processes, committee minutes and 
alignment with risk appetite (Articles 28 and 40). 
However, the requirements do not explicitly cover all material risk-takers (beyond 
directors, sen ior executives and the heads of control functions), meaning that 
risk-taking incentives may not be comprehensively captured. There are also no 
requirements on risk adjustment tools (deferral, malus and clawback). Supervisory 
work does address this area. Although extensive on remuneration policies and the 
related governance, it does not extend to outcomes (evidence of adjustments 
following underperformance or control breaches).  
Supervisory review 
Sugese’s risk-based supervision methodology (see ICP 9) includes interviews with 
Board members and heads of control functions as well as the imposition of 
corrective measures, implementation of which is subject to supervisory follow-up. 
As noted in the assessment of ICPs 2 and 9, supervision work, although strongly 
risk-based, is constrained by limited resources, including specialist expertise, and 
by the absence of a complete framework for addressing business conduct risks. 
Supervisors do, however, underta ke assessments of the effectiveness of 
governance in practice in addition to checking that there are appropriate policies 
and processes. 
Communication to the supervisor on governance

Public 
Although there are no explicit expectations for Boards to ensure timely and effective 
communication with Sugese on governance matters (ICP 7.9), insurers are subject 
to extensive requirements on reporting to Sugese, including both qualitative matters 
such as governance and quantitative matters (see ICP 9). They must report (widely 
defined) relevant facts (Article 9 of Sugese Agreement 12 -21). Article 41.4 of 
Conassif 04 -16 makes it an obligation of the Board to provide all information 
requested by Sugese.  
Groups and branches 
There are no regulated insurance groups currently. However, there is a framework 
for consolidated supervision in legislation and in Conassif Agreement 16-22, which 
includes requirements on the organisation and operation of the group (Articles 41–
54) and group RM (Articles 55–6). There are limited provisions on the expectations 
of Boards in respect to group-wide governance.   
Supervisors do, however, assess the risk profile of the group, considering its 
corporate governance and RM. In practice, Sugese applies this approach to the 
one insurer-led financial group and would apply it to any future insurance groups 
(see ICP 23). 
There are limited provisions on the application of governance requirements to 
branches (there is only one foreign insurer branch at present). Under  Conassif 
Agreement 04-16, branches must ensure that the governance of the parent insurer 
provides for efficient and prudent management of the operations in Costa Rica 
(Article 48). Branches must also align external audit and reporting with home  
jurisdiction standards (Articles 3 and 33 of Conassif Agreement 06-18).  
In practice, supervisors look to the manager of the branch to carry out governance 
functions for the branch’s operations. There are provisions in Sugese’s internal 
supervisory guidelines on the assessment of branches in practice. In addition, the 
legal representative of the branch is captured by the suitability requirements 
(ICP 5). Although the core governance obligations for branches are defined, it 
would be good practice for supervisors to encourage insurers to formal ise 
delegation letters, responsibilit y matrices and effectiveness testing of branch 
control functions. This would clarify accountability and support consistent 
application of governance requirements as branch structures evolve.  
Assessment Largely Observed 
Comments Laws and regulations provide a clear and comprehensive set of requirements. In 
practice, Sugese assesses governance through a structured, risk -based 
supervisory programme and takes actions to require improvements in governance 
where necessary. It does so even in the case of the state-owned insurer, where the 
lack of a fully developed policy framework for Board appointments (see Section 2) 
creates challenges to the implementation of effective supervision.  
Areas for enhancement include broadening the scope of remuneration policies to 
cover all material risk -takers and introducing tools such as deferral, malus and 
clawback, with evidence of their use (ICP 7.6); extending the expectations for group 
and branch governance, although Sugese does practice group-wide supervision of

Public 
the governance of the one insurer -led financial group and there are currently no 
insurance groups;  and strengthening supervisory work and reporting on Board 
effectiveness, especially the extent and quality of Board challenge to management, 
particularly in regard to the development and use of the RSSA and stress testing. 
It is recommended that Sugese, in addition to continuing to raise standards of 
governance, including on the independence and effectiveness of Boards: 
• Extends remuneration requirements to apply to all material risk-takers, embeds

• Encourages Boards to incorporate periodic external input into the required

• Further clarify expectations for group and branch governance, considering for

ICP 8  Risk Management and Internal Controls 
The supervisor requires an insurer to have, as part of its overall corporate 
governance framework, effective systems of risk management and internal 
controls, including effective functions for risk management, compliance, 
actuarial matters and internal audit. 
Description Effective risk management system (RMS) 
There are high-level requirements on the RMS:  
• Conassif Agreement 04 -16 holds the governing body responsible for the 
insurer’s RM, requiring that it ensure the adoption of a sound corporate culture 
by promoting, amongst others, a sound RM culture (Articles 5 and 10.2). 
• Article 5 of Sugese Agreement 09 -17 requires insurers to have effective and 
documented RM in place covering all levels of the legal entity, with the aim of 
identifying, measuring, monitoring, controlling and promptly reporting all 
matters related to the risks the entity faces, taking into account their potential 
impact and duration (Article 8). 
• Insurers are required to have a defined risk appetite statement that is aligned 
with their strategy, their capital management and the execution of their business 
activities (Article 10 of Sugese Agreement 09 -17). They must also have a 
documented process that includes the tools and methodologies for identifying, 
measuring, monitoring, assessing and controlling risks.  
• In addition, contingency, business continuity and crisis management plans must 
be in place with documented processes (Article 12 of Sugese 
Agreement 09-17). 
• The RMS must consider technical risks, ALM risk, liquidity and concentration 
risks, operational risk, business conduct risks and reinsurance risks (Article 8(f) 
of Sugese Agreement 09-17). Conassif Agreement 05-24 sets out requirements

Public 
on the management of IT risk , and Sugese has also published guidance on 
reinsurance risk management (see ICP 13). 
The requirements do not explicitly include underwriting and reserving risks, 
investments and other mitigation techniques (ICP 8.1). However, these are 
reviewed by supervisors as part of their risk assessment and Sugese requires their 
inclusion in the scope of the RMS, where necessary.  
The RMS must be aligned with the insurer’s strategy and should be examined 
regularly and any deviation from or update to the RMS reported to the governing 
body (Article 11 of Sugese Agreement 09 -17). The governing body must approve 
the RM strategy (Article 9 of Sugese Agreement 09 -17 and Article 12 of Conassif 
Agreement 04 -16) and any deviations from the RM strategy or risk appetite 
statement (Article 8(d) of Sugese Agreement 09-17). 
Effective system of internal controls 
Articles 5 and 16 –18 of Sugese Agreement 09 -17 establish that the insurer is 
responsible for proving the effectiveness of internal controls. It must have effective 
and documented internal control processes in place that include defined, 
implemented and monitored strategies, policies, processes and controls. The se 
must ensure adequate control of risks ; prudent conduct of business ; reliability of 
financial and non-financial information reported (both internally and externally); and 
compliance with laws, regul ations, supervisory requirements and the insurer ’s 
internal rules and decisions.  
Internal control processes must support the efforts of the governing body and 
Senior Management to fulfil their oversight responsibilities (Article 16). 
The governing body is required to have a general understanding of the control 
environment; approve the related strategies, policies, reporting, appointments and 
responsibilities; and establish appropriate controls to avoid conflicts of interest or 
compromise the autonomy of overall control functions (Article 17 of Sugese 
Agreement 09 -17). It must also provide for the segregation of tasks and 
responsibilities and prevention of conflicts of interest (Article 16). 
There is no requirement for appropriate reporting on internal control systems 
(ICP 8.2). However, actual reporting is reviewed as part of supervisory risk 
assessment and Sugese requires corrective measures as necessary.  
The internal control processes are to be reviewed regularly and revised as needed 
(Article 16 of Sugese Agreement 09-17). 
Control functions 
Article 18 of Sugese Agreement 09-17 indicates that as part of the effective internal 
controls and RMS, insurers must have control functions with sufficient authority, 
independence and resources. These functions include financial analysis (not part 
of the ICP requirements), regulatory compliance, RM, actuarial and internal audit. 
The functions must support the governing body’s governance and oversight duties, 
but do not exempt it from its responsibilities. 
The governing body of the insurer is required to approve, remove, evaluate and 
remunerate the individuals that are responsible for control functions (Article 17(d)

Public 
of Sugese Agreement 09 -17 and Article 8.2 of Conassif Agreement 04 -16). The 
governing body must also define the powers, independence and responsibilities of 
each control function and guarantee their access to the governing body 
(Article 17(e) of Sugese Agreement 09-17). 
o RM function 
Insurers must have an effective RM function capable of identifying, measuring, 
monitoring and mitigating risks  (Article 32 of Conassif Agreement 04 -16 and 
Article 13 of Sugese Agreement 09-17). It must also report to the relevant areas as 
required and promote and maintain an adequate risk culture in the insurer 
(Article 13 of Sugese Agreement 09-17). There is no requirement on the minimum 
scope of matters that are to be included in its reporting, including regular stress 
testing (ICP 8.4). However, supervisors review actual reports as part of their risk 
assessment and may require corrective actions.  
o Compliance function 
Boards are required to establish a function that is responsible for advocating and 
ensuring that the insurer operates with integrity and in compliance with laws, 
regulations etc (Article 37 of Conassif Agreement 04 -16). The function is to serve 
as a point of contact for staff queries on compliance and to guide and train staff on 
compliance, particularly for those holding key positions or performing high -risk 
activities (Articles 23 and 24(b) of Sugese Agreement 09-17).  
o Actuarial function 
Insurers must have an effective actuarial function, independent of lines of business 
and with staff who have sufficient and verifiable financial and actuarial mathematics 
expertise and experience (Article 25 of Sugese Agreement 09-17). However, there 
is no indication that the function should have access to and periodically report to 
the Board (ICP 8.6). 
The responsibilities of the function include assessing insurance technical risks, 
present and future solvency ratios and technical provisions (both under normal and 
stress conditions), the sufficiency and quality of the data and methodologies for 
calculating technical provisions, underwriting policies, and the development and 
design of the reinsurance mechanism and products or other risk transfers 
(Article 26 of Sugese Agreement 09-17). However, these responsibilities do not 
include the assessment of ALM, investment policies and the valuation of assets 
(ICP 8.6). 
The actuarial function is not required to provide certification o f the adequacy, 
reasonableness and/or fairness of premiums and certifications or statements of 
actuarial opinion (ICP 8.6). Sugese indicated that this certification is required as 
part of the process of review of the regulatory reporting. 
o Internal audit function 
This function is required to express an independent opinion to the governing body 
and Senior Management on the quality and efficacy of internal controls, risk 
management and corporate governance systems and processes (Article 38 of 
Conassif Agreement 04-16).

Public 
There is no explicit requirement for internal audit to address all of the items in 
ICP 8.7, including assessment of assets and accounting/financial reporting. 
However, these issues are reviewed as part of the supervisory risk assessment. 
Outsourcing of material activities or functions 
It is a requirement of insurers upon licensing that their critical operational and 
control positions are not to be outsourced if doing so would involve damage to the 
quality of the insurer’s governance systems, a substantial increase in operational 
or other risks, impairment of Sugese’s ability to perform its functions or interference 
with the continuity and satisfactoriness of the servi ce (Article 48 of Sugese 
Agreement 01-21).  
However, there is no requirement that the insurer retain at least the same degree 
of oversight of and accountability for any outsourced material activity or function as 
applies to non-outsourced activities or functions (ICP 8.8). 
Groups and branches of foreign insurers 
There are no regulated insurance groups in Costa Rica currently. However, there 
is a framework for consolidated supervision in l egislation and in Conassif 
Agreement 16-22, which includes requirements on the organisation and operation 
of the group (Articles 41–54) and group risk management (Articles 55–6). 
For insurers that are part of a financial group, the requirements on risk management 
strategy in Sugese Agreement 09 -17 include a requirement that the insurer’s 
strategy consider the objectives, RM and business strategy of the group. There 
should be indication that the insurer’s risk management strategy is totally or partially 
linked to the group’s strategy and a description of the process of monitoring and 
reporting between the insurer and its headquarters or between the insurer and its 
subsidiaries (Article 9(e)). 
The provisions in Sugese Agreement 09-17 apply to all insurers, including branches 
of a foreign insurer (Article 2). 
Supervisory work 
Sugese evaluates the control functions in its risk assessment. For example, it 
evaluates the effectiveness of internal audit by reviewing the function’s 
responsibilities (objectives, authority, right of access to record s and confidential 
information etc), the organisational structure, the resources available, methodology 
and practices, planning, reports and quality assurance. It takes into account 
indicators such as the extent of a control function’s interaction with the Board and 
Senior Management etc in addition to reviewing the function’s processes, its reports 
and minutes of the relevant meetings.  
The risk profile assessment review process is documented in Sugese’s General 
Guide to Risk Profile Assessment.   
Assessment Largely Observed 
Comments There are extensive requirements on insurers’ risk management and internal

ICP 9 Supervisory Review and Reporting 
The supervisor uses off-site monitoring and on-site inspections to: examine 
the business of each insurer; evaluate its financial condition, conduct of 
business, corporate governance framework and overall risk profile; and 
assess its compliance with relevant legislation and supervisory 
requirements. The supervisor obtains the necessary information to conduct 
effective supervision of insurers and evaluate the insurance market. 
Description The s upervision of insurers (and intermediaries) is carried out by Sugese’s 
Supervision Division. There are four supervision teams, each covering three to four 
insurers (or one in the case of that responsible for the largest insurer). They work 
alongside specialists in intermediary supervision, IT and actuarial issues.  
The division undertakes all aspects of supervision, including both off -site and 
on-site supervision and AML/CFT work. However, its focus is on prudential 
supervision. A framework for business conduct supervision has been developed 
and is awaiting agreement by Conassif before it can be implemented (ICP 19). 
The same approach is taken to domestic and foreign -owned insurers and to the 
one branch of a foreign insurer, adjusted for necessary differences in prudential 
requirements such as solvency. 
Sugese’s work is supported by IT systems including the Sugese Online service , 
which enables insurers to report supervisory information, details of persons subject 
to suitability requirements (Roles Service) and material events (Material Events 
Service), and the Studies Execution and Follow-Up (EES) Service, which Sugese 
uses to manage supervisory work and through which insurers make ad hoc reports, 
including updates on actions that Sugese has required of them.  
Framework for supervisory review and reporting 
Sugese has developed a risk -based approach to prudential supervision, drawing 
on practices in other superintendencies, external advice and IAIS standards. Key 
elements are:  
• A framework for risk assessment and expected supervisory actions for each 
assessed level of risk;  
• A planning process to determine the individual pieces of supervisory work 
(known as “studies”) to be undertaken (subject to a minimum amount of off-site 
work, all supervisory activities, whether off -site or on -site, are planned in 
accordance with the assessed risk of the insurer and supervisory intensity);

Public 
• Processes and guidelines for supervisors on how to carry out supervisory work 
and to follow up on actions requested or required of insurers; 
• Decision-taking processes to support robust and consistent supervisory 
judgments, including on the actions to be required of insurers; and 
• Communication with insurers as appropriate after each study, anchored by an 
annual letter from the Superintendent to the Board of Directors of each insurer 
summarising Sugese’s supervisory view, outstanding actions etc.  
The framework is supported by certain principles communicated to supervisors, 
including the need to focus on material risks and to intervene early in case of 
concern. However, there is no formal statement of supervisory risk appetite.  
Supervisory resources are allocated to each insurer according to supervisory 
intensity and associated work plans, taking into account the insurer’s scale and 
significance (thus, the largest insurer is supervised by a single team). 
Sugese’s risk assessment framework (with related supervisory measures by level 
of risk) is set out in Sugese Agreement 10-17, issued in 2017 by Conassif using its 
powers in Article 30 of the IMRL. Core elements of the approach, which are set out 
in an internal document, the General Guide to Risk Profile Assessment, are: 
• Identification of core activities (which may and often do include functions such 
as IT services) and the assessment of inherent risks (credit, market, insurance 
technical, operating and strategic risks) by each activity; 
• Assessment of the quality of the risk management and governance by activity; 
• Determination of net risk (by activity and overall) and then of the compound risk 
after assessing capital, profitability and liquidity and taking into account the 
assessment of AML/CFT risks (undertaken separately; see ICP 22); and 
• Assignment of a risk level (from 1 to 5, the last indicating that an insurer is 
non-viable or faces imminent insolvency) based on assessed compound risk 
and other factors such as breaches of regulations etc, findings from regular 
supervision activities, special investigations and market conditions.  
Supervisory actions are listed for each risk level but are not exhaustive and may be 
(and are in practice) varied by supervisors using judgment. Risk assessments are 
reviewed by internal panels of Sugese’s supervision managers and Senior 
Management before being finalised.  
The supervisory intensity for each insurer (Basic, Moderate, Alert or Intensified) is 
determined as part of Sugese’s planning process, drawing on the risk assessment, 
significance of the insurer and scope of the required work. The level of intensity 
informs the frequenc y of certain work, including updat es to the risk assessment 
(annually for Intensified and once every four years for Basic).   
The framework is kept under review by Sugese and updated periodically (in 2021 
for Sugese Agreement 10-17) as part of its quality management system. There is 
extensive internal documentation, particularly on risk assessment and ratings. Only 
the methodology set out in Sugese Agreement 10-17 is published.  
Group perspectives

Public 
There are no insurance groups for which Sugese is group -wide supervisor. 
However, the framework provides for group -wide supervision in the case of the 
one insurer, in which Sugese is responsible for supervision of the financial group 
(see ICP 23). Risk assessment in this case is based on group-wide risks.  
For other insurers that are parts of a Costa Rican financial group, the risk 
assessment provides for consideration of the risks associated with the legal entity’s 
relationship with its group. Sugese cooperates with other superintendencies with 
responsibilities for financial entities within the group under the arrangements for 
consolidated supervision (ICP 23).  
Supervisory plans  
The Supervision Division prepares an annual plan covering the next two years 
(“macroplanning”). Based on the risk level and supervisory intensity assigned to 
each insurer, the plan sets out scheduled on-site work (distinguishing between full 
risk assessments  and money laundering assessments), specific reviews of 
cross-sector issues such as technical provisions , and plans for analysis of the 
periodic financial information received from insurers (“compulsory studies”).  
The output of the macroplanning process is approved by the Superintendent.  
Review of outsourced material activities or functions 
Requirements on outsourcing by insurers are set out in Conassif Agreement 01-21 
and, for IT services, Conassif Agreement 05 -24 (ICP 8). Outsourcing may occur 
only where it does not impair Sugese’s ability to supervise. Insurers must notify 
Sugese of outsourced activities. Outsourcing contracts must provide for 
unrestricted access by Sugese to relevant information at the service provider.  
Sugese also has access to some outsourced service providers through the 
requirements in the IMRL for providers of “ancillary services” to register with 
Sugese and meet obligations including notifications to Sugese. The services 
covered include actuarial, c onsulting, claims processing, accident indemnification 
and repair of damages (Articles 18 and 27 of the IMRL).   
In practice, most of Sugese’s work on outsourced activities relates to IT services, 
where it assesses the outsourced elements of IT infrastructure as part of a wider 
framework for the supervision of insurers’ IT risks. Sugese assesses other forms of 
outsourcing to be generally not significant , but they would be reviewed as part of 
the risk assessment (including as a core activity) if necessary. In an example of 
supervisory work discussed during the assessment, Sugese noted that it has 
carried out work on outsourced claims handling services at one insurer.  
Supervisory reporting  
Insurers are required to submit reports to Sugese as specified in detailed 
regulations etc (Article 25 of the IMRL). Sugese Agreement 12-21 sets the reporting 
obligations, including for branches of foreign insurers, and more detailed reporting 
requirements are set out in Superintendent Agreements (issued by Sugese) 
SGS-DES-A-021-2013 and SGS -A-0079-2021. They cover both quantitative and 
qualitative information and a requirement to report material changes or events 
affecting the insurer or its clients.

Public 
The detailed requirements are extensive, covering balance sheet, performance and 
solvency information (monthly or quarterly), details of premium income and claims, 
and granular information on business lines. Sugese provides for electronic 
reporting of all financial information.  
Conassif has wide-ranging powers to set requirements on the accounting standards 
to be used by insurers for both regulatory reporting and in the preparation of audited 
financial statements (Article 171 of the SMRL). It sets out requirements, based on 
IFRS (with adjustments and variations), in Conassif Agreement s 06-18 and 10-21 
and Sugese’s Superintendent Agreement SGS-DES-A-028-2013.  
Insurers are required to submit their annual financial statements to Sugese 
(Conassif Agreement 06 -18 and Superintendent Agreement  
SGS-DES-A-021-2013). These have to be audited (Article 3 of Conassif 
Agreement 01-10).   
Insurers are required to amend inaccurate reports as soon as possible after the 
error is discovered (Article 25 of the IMRL). Sugese may also use its powers o f 
preventive and corrective measures to require amendments, more frequent 
reporting or the submission of additional information. There are provisions in the 
risk assessment framework (see above) for more frequent reports to be required of 
insurers rated at a certain risk level.   
Off-site monitoring  
Sugese’s supervision function carries out risk -based monitoring of insurers using 
the supervisory reports received. Sugese Agreement 10 -17 (see above) sets out 
the approach to be taken (including supervisory action) by level of risk.  
For example, for level 1 entities, supervisors are expected to carry out off -site 
financial analysis of regular reports; request meetings with Senior Management, 
the Board or external auditors to indicate concerns and corrective measures; inform 
them of the assessed level of risk; and monitor the implementation of required 
measures. 
For level 2 insurers, additional actions include increasing the intensity of 
supervision (increased reporting frequency or more details); requesting an 
implementation plan to ensure financial soundness; imposing business restrictions, 
mandatory guidelines  or profit-sharing restrictions; requesting removal of 
directors etc and asking for a capital increase. 
Sugese’s quality management system sets out the required processes, 
manuals etc including on how supervision teams document their assessments. 
Sugese’s IT systems track whether there have been communications with the 
insurer and the off-site assessment has been carried out.   
In addition, quarterly monitoring is carried out through the indicators that are 
maintained in Sugese’s Early Warning Indicators System, which automatically 
generates warnings (for example, of a deterioration in financial condition) based on 
analysis of insurers’ financial reporting. 
On-site inspection

Public 
Sugese has powers to carry out inspections (Article 31 of the IMRL). Its on -site 
work is integrated into its supervision framework (see above). Supervisors may go 
on-site for any supervisory purpose, including for routine meetings with 
management. Sugese’s more extensive on-site work is undertaken either: 
• To update the risk assessment (an extensive process involving interviews with 
Board members, sen ior managers etc) , known as a risk -based supervision 
review;  
• To assess AML/CFT risks; or 
• To perform special studies, ie work on a particular issue across insurers. 
Sugese aims to carry out several of these major on -site exercises per year. 
Four risk assessment updates and two AML/CFT reviews were included for 2025 
in the 2024 –25 supervision plan. There were additional cross -sector reviews 
planned, of technical provisions and IFRS 17 preparation. Sugese has been unable 
to deliver all of its planned on-site work in recent years.   
There are detailed processes and internal guidelines for on-site work. For the major 
pieces of work, insurers are notified in advance of the purpose of the visit (a 
requirement of administrative law) and given feedback at its end, followed by 
preparation of a report.  
There are no internal deadlines set for sending reports to insurers, and in practice 
their preparation, which is based on detailed internal documentation of the work, 
usually takes many months, including the time required to schedule Board meetings 
at which the findings are presented.  
Supervisory feedback and follow-up  
Feedback is provided to insurers after each piece of work (study) as required. For 
risk assessments, for example, the Superintendent normally writes to the insurer’s 
Board of Directors to summarise findings, which include an indication of the 
insurer’s risk rating. Required actions are recorded in the EES System, which 
allows for monitoring and supervisory evaluation of measures taken by insurers.     
Although the annual supervision letter and reports of major supervisory work such 
as the risk assessment are communicated by the Superintendent to Boards of 
Directors, Sugese’s supervisors communicate regularly with the Senior 
Management of insurers to require information on developments, convey the 
findings of other supervisory work/studies , and request or require actions as 
necessary (ICP 10).   
Assessment Largely Observed   
Comments Sugese carries out extensive prudential supervision of insurers within a risk-based 
framework that includes thorough risk assessment, off-site and on-site supervisory 
work, reporting to the Boards and Senior Management of insurers and specification 
of required actions. Supervision work is carefully planned and both findings and 
required actions thoroughly documented, including in annual supervisory letters to 
the Board of each insurer. Issuance of key reports could, however, be faster.

Public 
Sugese’s risk-based approach allows supervisors to react to emerging risks and 
adjust the work programme in line with risks and available resources. It would 
benefit from adoption of an explicit risk appetite framework to guide resource 
allocation decisions, especially given current and continuing resource constraints.  
The supervisory approach excludes business conduct, although there are plans to 
fill this gap (see ICP 19). Increased specialisation of supervisory staff (to add to the 
recent recruitment of actuaries and existing IT experts), already necessary, is likely 
to become more important as conduct work is added to the existing wide range of 
issues covered by supervision teams.  
It is recommended that, in addition to introducing a risk-based approach to conduct 
of business supervision (see ICP 19 recommendations), Sugese:  
• Develops and documents a supervisory risk appetite to support its allocation of 
supervisory resources; 
• Increases specialisation in supervision work to support effective supervision 
given growing demands; and  
• Sets and monitors target timelines for all  reports going to insurers in order to 
ensure that findings and required actions are communicated to insurers 
promptly after the completion of supervisory work.   
ICP 10 Preventive Measures, Corrective Measures and Sanctions 
The supervisor: 
• Requires and enforces preventive and corrective measures; and 
• Imposes sanctions 
which are timely, necessary to achieve the objectives of insurance 
supervision, and based on clear, objective, consistent, and publicly 
disclosed general criteria. 
Description Insurance activities without the necessary licence 
Unlicensed insurance is prohibited and can be tackled directly by Sugese. The 
IMRL (Article 35) empowers the supervisor to inspect, impose precautionary 
measures and sanction where regulated activities are conducted without 
authorisation. It also allows applications to the criminal courts to close premises 
used for unlawful insurance business.  
Article 31(e) of the CBCRL (applied to Sugese via  Article 29(2) of the  IMRL) 
provides explicit policing powers over unauthorised actors.  Article 156 of the  
CBCRL similarly instructs Sugese to prevent unlicensed insurance nationwide and 
grants inspection powers equivalent to those used for regulated legal entities. In 
practice, Sugese has used these powers and escalated cases to the courts when 
necessary.  
Preventive and corrective measures 
Sugese can intervene before and after breaches of requirements. Under Article 131 
of the CBCRL, the Superintendent may:

Public 
• Adjust financial/accounting information and require corrections to misleading 
publications (paragraphs f and k);  
• Limit or prohibit dividends/bonuses where instability is detected (paragraph q); 
• Order cessation of activities that breach laws or regulations (paragraph r) or 
threaten safety/solvency (paragraph s); and 
• Prohibit intragroup transactions with non-compliant group entities (paragraph t). 
These powers are operational ised through Sugese Agreement 10 -17, which 
defines the risk-based assessment methodology and the triggers for preventive and 
corrective measures (see also ICP 9). In practice, instructions are issued by Sugese 
via formal resolutions and tracked to closure. 
As noted in the assessment of ICPs 9 and 12, there are also provisions in the 
legislative framework for Conassif to trigger processes in the CBCRL on 
intervention in financial institutions, which include an administration process (see 
ICP 12).  
As noted under the assessment of ICP 5, Sugese does not have statutory powers 
to suspend or remove individuals subject to the suitability requirements from their 
office or to require insurers to do so. 
Assessing the effectiveness of an insurer’s actions 
Under Sugese Agreement 10 -17, follow-up is structured around three steps: the 
insurer must implement measures that address the concern (Articles 22(c), 24(a), 
26(a)), supervisors verify delivery at defined intervals (Articles 20(a), 20(d), 20(h), 
22(a), 26(c)) and effectiveness is assessed against outcome indicators 
(Articles 20(a), 20(b), 20(d), 20(h)). 
In practice, Sugese issues written requirements with scope, due dates and 
accountable owners and tracks them through internal processes and systems. 
Effectiveness is judged against proportionate indicators such as solvency 
coverage, reserve adequacy, remediation of policyholder detriment, control  
function effectiveness and governance changes, with reporting frequency 
increased where risk is higher until targets are met.  
Where remediation stalls or evidence is insufficient, cases move onto the escalation 
track as set out in Sugese’s General Guide to Risk Profile Assessment  (ICP 9), 
stepping up from recommendations to directives and restrictions with tighter 
timelines.  
Escalation of actions 
Sugese maintains clear escalation protocols, defined in Sugese Agreement 10-17 
and reinforced by Articles 29 and 35 of the IMRL. Escalation is also embedded in 
the General Guide to Risk Profile Assessment . When insurers fail to adequately 
address identified risks or comply with its directives, Sugese escalates 
interventions incrementally, deploying stricter corrective measures proportionate to 
risk severity. Sugese’s practice aligns with proportional escalation where concerns 
are not addressed. 
Sanctions

Public 
Sanctions are graduated and criteria  based. The IMRL (Articles 36 –9) classifies 
breaches as high -severity or serious and provide s a menu of penalties (fines , 
revocation/suspension of author isation, restrictions on new business , 
disqualification of individuals).  
The SMRL (Article 164, as applied to Sugese via Article 35 of the IMRL) sets 
proportionality criteria: seriousness, harm, intent, ability to pay, duration and 
recurrence. Administrative decisions must also satisfy reasonableness under 
Law 6227 and Constitutional Court doctrine. 
Under Article 41 of the IMRL, when imposing sanctions on an insurer or 
intermediary, Sugese may also sanction a director or employee when the person is 
found to be wilfully or negligently responsible  (it is not otherwise able to impose 
sanctions on an individual) . Detailed provisions set out the available sanctions, 
including public reprimands and barring from office for five years as well as financial 
penalties, depending on whether the findings are of intentional or negligent 
responsibility.  
Practices 
Sugese has applied a range of preventive and corrective measures, including 
recommendations, supervisory plans, fines and temporary restrictions. Measures 
are applied proportionately and escalation is appropriate.  
As disclosed by Sugese on its website, 21 sanctions have been imposed over the 
past seven years, mainly for breaches of reporting, solvency and corporate 
governance requirements. No sanctions have yet been applied in relation to 
AML/CFT deficiencies.  
Although Sugese has sought to use its powers to sanction individuals (including 
one case in which it initiated action against an intermediary company and 
responsible person under Article 41 of the IMRL), it has not yet imposed sanctions 
on any person other than individually licensed insurance agents.  
For transparency and deterrence,  sanctions are published on Sugese’s website 
here: www.sugese.fi.cr/seccion-consumidores-seguro/participantes-sancionados. 
Assessment Observed 
Comments Sugese has extensive powers, which it uses in practice, to require actions and 
impose sanctions. The legal basis enables Sugese to intervene in case of 
unlicensed activity. The toolkit of requirements on preventive and corrective actions 
(including dividen d and activity restrictions)  is extensive . Sugese’s supervisory 
processes require verification of implementation and effectiveness. Escalation is 
applied where risks increase and sanctions are criteria -based, subject to 
administrative law tests and published to support transparency and deterrence. 
Sugese lacks powers to suspend or remove directors, Senior Management or key 
function holders or to require insurers to do so, which reduces its leverage when 
insurers’ remedial actions prove insufficient (ICP 10.5, but see the assessment and 
recommendations for ICP 5). It also faces legal obstacles to the imposition of

Public 
sanctions on individuals as provided for in the IMRL and has been unable to take 
such action in practice.  
It is recommended that Sugese: 
• Strengthens supervisory capacity for enforcement by formalising proactive 
detection of unlicensed activity (eg routine media monitoring, complaint 
analytics, inter-agency alerts) and publishing an annual note on outcomes; 
• Improves follow-through by setting indicative timelines in supervisory letters, 
adopting internal escalation steps with time -bound triggers and disclosing 
aggregate timeliness in the annual supervisory review; and 
• Considers, with Conassif and other superintendencies, how to strengthen its 
ability to impose sanctions on a wide range of individuals using powers under 
Article 41 of the IMRL, seeking legislative change if necessary.  
ICP 12 Exit from the Market and Resolution 
Legislation provides requirements for: 
• The voluntary exit of insurers from the market; and 
• The resolution of insurers that are no longer viable or are likely to be no 
longer viable, and have no reasonable prospect of returning to viability. 
Description General framework 
The legislative framework provides for voluntary exit as well as bankruptcy 
proceedings. There is no identified resolution authority for insurers or 
comprehensive insurance-specific resolution framework, and the main approach in 
case of failure of an insurer is court-approved bankruptcy. However, the legislation 
grants certain powers to Sugese.  
The IMRL sets out aspects of the bankruptcy proceedings applicable to insurers 
(Article 32), while the Insolvency Law (Law 9957 of 2021), the general bankruptcy 
legal framework for all companies in Costa Rica, regulates the substantial and 
procedural aspects of insolvency proceedings.  
There is no policyholder protection scheme and no plans for establishing one (by 
contrast, deposit insurance was created under a 2020 law establishing a guarantee 
fund and bank resolution mechanisms). The policyholder liabilities of the 
state-owned insurer (excluding reinsurance liabilities and transactions carried out 
outside the country) are protected by the state guarantee for the insurer provided 
for in its founding legislation (Article 1 of the National Insurance Institute Law 
(Law 12) of 1924). 
Sugese has experienced two voluntary exits from the market, including most 
recently that of a branch of a foreign insurer in 2022. Both were effected through a 
portfolio transfer to other insurers. There has been no exit from the market through 
bankruptcy. 
Voluntary liquidation 
Sugese may cancel a licen ce at the insurer’s request for voluntary liquidation 
(Article 32 of the IMRL). The regulations on licensing etc also list voluntary

Public 
termination as one of the reasons for Sugese to cancel, annul, revoke or suspend 
a licence (Article 20 of Sugese Agreement 01-21), while also providing for Sugese 
to take measures to address the interests of the insured individuals and otherwise 
meet its objectives. 
Insurers have to seek authorisation from Sugese for voluntary exit. Article 4 of 
Sugese Agreement 01-21 sets out the process for doing so, including a requirement 
to submit a plan for cessation of operations. This must guarantee fulfilment of 
obligations to policyholders, whether through the run -off of insurance liabilities or 
the transfer of contracts to another supervised insurer (Article 36). 
Resolution framework 
Applicable provisions are in Articles 32–4 of the IMRL, Article 156 of the SMRL and 
Articles 139 and 140 of the CBCRL, complemented by Articles 27 and 28 of Sugese 
Agreement 10 -17. As noted, Law 9957 regulates substantial and procedural 
aspects of insolvency proceedings.  
Under Article 32 of the IMRL, Sugese may cancel an insurer’s author isation 
whenever “the entities’ situation is so serious that intervention is not a viable 
mechanism for helping them to recover” (there is no elaboration o n the basis on 
which this judgment may be made) . The insurer is then dissolved and goes into 
liquidation under the Law 9957 processes. In addition, Law 9957 provides for 
Sugese (as a supervisor) to initiate bankruptcy proceedings (Articles 12 and 14.2).  
Under Article 34 of the IMRL, in case an insurer’s creditors request a bankruptcy, 
the court must immediately notify Sugese, which must then advise on the insurer’s 
solvency within 20 business days, after which, if Sugese confirms that the insurer 
is insolvent, the court will commence proceedings. If Sugese takes the view that 
the insurer is solvent, it must inform the court of the measures that should 
nonetheless be imposed. 
There are also provisions in the legislative framework to trigger processes in the 
CBCRL on intervention in financial institutions, which include an administration 
process.  
In case of serious non -compliance by an insurer (defined as a contravention of 
requirements in Article 136(d)(ii) –(vii) of the CBCRL), Conassif may decide that 
intervention is required; in practice Sugese will ask Conassif to make such a 
decision when an insurer is rated at risk level 5 (imminent insolvency or non-viable 
entity); see ICP 9. 
The processes in Articles 139 –40 of the CBCRL then apply. In case of severe 
financial instability, Conassif shall appoint comptrollers (which may be Sugese 
officers) to administer the insurer with a wide range of intervention powers. 
Bankruptcy proceedings may not take place during administration, which may last 
up to one year.  
Thirty calendar days before the end of the administration, Sugese must decide, 
after consulting the comptrollers, whether to allow the insurer to resume business 
or to request that the court proceed with proceedings under Law 9957.

Public 
There are no provisions in the legislation providing for insurance liabilities to be 
restructured, including for policyholders to be required to absorb losses. 
Preparation for resolution  
Sugese has not yet put in place plans for the resolution of any insurers. It lacks 
explicit powers but takes the view that it could rely on other powers to require an 
insurer to cooperate in the development of a resolution plan. 
In the absence of a framework for assessing systemic importance (see ICP 24), 
there are no systemically important insurers for whom a resolution might be 
prepared as a pr iority. Insurers have not yet been required to prepare recovery 
plans (ICP 16) or to evaluate their specific operations and risks in possible 
resolution scenarios. Sugese has experience in managing insurers undergoing 
financial stress but has not made plans for how it might manage a failure.  
Priority of policyholder claims  
Although liquidation would take place under Law 9957, as mentioned, Article 34 of 
the IMRL provides that once a bankruptcy is declared, the liquidation will be 
completed with due respect to the interests of the insured individuals. After paying 
their costs, liquidators will first pay claims arising under the Labour Code (amounts 
due to employees) and then pay obligations arising from insurance policies, starting 
with annuity policies deriving from the Workers ’ Protection Law, after which 
privileged creditors will be paid, as defined in Article 901 of the Commercial Code.  
Cooperation with other authorities, groups and foreign branches  
Sugese has general powers to cooperate with foreign supervisors and other 
authorities, including the exchange of confidential information that would be 
necessary in respect to coordinated resolution actions (see ICP 3). In respect to 
groups, there is a provision in the legislation on group supervision for supervisors 
to inform other authorities of relevant developments (Article 141bis of the CBCRL). 
There are no provisions in the legislation enabling the head of an insurance group 
to be resolved at the same time as the insurer itself when both are in Costa Rica.   
The framework on market exit also applies to branches of foreign insurers. In the 
case of voluntary exit, the insurer is required to provide a non-objection letter from 
the parent’s supervisor as a condition of Sugese’s agreement to the exit (Annex 8 
of Sugese Agreement 01-21). Such a letter was provided in practice in the case of 
the voluntary exit of a foreign insurer’s branch in 2022. However, Sugese has not 
planned for the failure of a branch or its parent insurer in another jurisdiction (there 
is only one such branch at present). 
Assessment Partly Observed  
Comments There are extensive provisions on the voluntary exit of insurers, which have been 
tested in practice using portfolio transfers to protect the interests of policyholders. 
Unlike for banks, for which a full framework has been introduced, there is no 
resolution authority  or resolution  powers and processes (including a protection 
fund) for insurers. The approach to the failure of an insurer, which has not been 
tested in practice, would rely on general bankruptcy proceedings. Sugese would by

Public 
law be involved in the initiation of such proceedings (although not in the process 
itself), and there are requirements in insurance legislation giving high pr iority to 
policyholders. Sugese is well equipped to cooperate with other authorities.  
However, the point at which Sugese would trigger bankruptcy is not well  defined 
(ICP 12.7; see also ICP 17). In the absence of a wider framework for the supervision 
of systemically important insurers, Sugese has not undertaken resolution planning 
or otherwise prepared itself for the necessary actions (ICPs 12.3–12.4). Legislative 
change would be required to equip Sugese (assuming it is the resolution authority 
and existing bankruptcy provisions are inadequate, at least for systemically 
important insurers) with the appropriate range of tools (ICP 12.8).   
It is recommended that: 
• The Government of Costa Rica make s legislative changes to provide for 
resolution powers and appropriate triggers for their use and for existing powers 
in the IMRL to trigger bankruptcy; and 
• Sugese plans for resolution actions, giving pr iority to resolution planning for 
systemically important insurer(s), once identified. 
ICP 13 Reinsurance and Other Forms of Risk Transfer 
The supervisor requires the insurer to manage effectively its use of 
reinsurance and other forms of risk transfer. The supervisor takes into 
account the nature of reinsurance business when supervising reinsurers 
based in its jurisdiction. 
Description Reinsurance practices 
Reinsurance is a key risk mitigation tool in the insurance market. Cession rates 
over recent years average around 20%, with a wide range (9–70%) across insurers. 
Reinsurance is used mainly on a cross -border basis; only one insurer is also 
authorised to provide reinsurance to both local and foreign insurers. This insurer is 
also the only insurer using retrocession. 
Requirements on reinsurance  
There is a comprehensive set of laws, regulations and supervisory guides on 
reinsurance, which Sugese has supplemented with a guide  on best practices  for 
reinsurance management.  
Insurers are required to make reinsurance an integral part of their internal controls 
and risk management framework and to have a reinsurance strategy, programme 
and related policies and processes. The actuarial function is responsible for 
reviewing the adequacy, quality and effectiveness of the reinsurance programme, 
including the effectiveness of the risk transfer. Sugese’s supervision team carries 
out reviews of the insurer’s reinsurance management, including its programme and 
strategy, as well as the effectiveness of the risk transfer. 
Major overarching requirements on reinsurance are set out in Articles 2, 16 and 25 
of the IMRL. More detailed requirements are set out in regulations.

Public 
Principles for the design of the RMS and implementation of the control function of 
the insurer are contained in Sugese Agreement 09-17. 
The treatment of reinsurance in the solvency requirements is set out in Sugese 
Agreement 02-13. Reinsurance is recognised in the technical reserves (and Boards 
are required to approve a “policy that, as a minimum, oversees risks including 
reinsurance”).  
The execution of an annual self -evaluation (RSSA), which must demonstrate the 
reinsurance programme’s appropriateness to the insurer’s business , is required 
(Article 15 of Sugese Agreement 02-13; see ICP 16). 
Insurers are also required to make information  available to Sugese, including  on 
accepted and ceded reinsurance, enabling the evaluation of reinsurance risk 
management.  
Finally, Sugese Agreement 10-17 sets expectations that supervisors will require an 
external assessment of the reliability of reinsurance agreements by a professional 
where the legal entity is rated at a level indicating that its future viability is in doubt 
(see ICP 9). 
Supervision 
Sugese’s General Guide to Risk Profile Assessment (along with the complementary 
Reinsurance Assessment Guide) forms a key part of the supervisory framework on 
reviewing reinsurance management. In addition, reviewing reinsurance 
management is part of supervisory activities when assigning or updating an 
insurer’s risk rating/risk profile. The General Guide covers how supervisors should 
determine whether reinsurance is a significant activity for the insurer and sets out 
the relevant information to be provided to the supervisor to enable such a 
determination.  
Reinsurance contracts must be provided to Sugese for its supervisory review. This 
covers the terms and conditions of reinsurance programmes and the relief they can 
provide under normal and stressed conditions. The data that supervisors analyse 
includes the terms of cession and retrocession, premiums, commissions, losses 
and outstanding balances. 
The Reinsurance Assessment Guide sets criteria for supervisors to consider in their 
assessment of the insurer’s reinsurance management and the quality of 
reinsurance as a risk transfer tool. 
Supervisors can undertake special studies that, for example, assess the alignment 
of reinsurance policies with the insurer’s reinsurance strategy and the extent of 
stress testing. Actuarial analyses can be requested of the insurer where there are 
reasonable concerns about the effectiveness of the reinsurance in place.  
Sugese’s guide on best practices for r einsurance management, which sets out 
expectations for use by market participants, was shared with insurers in 2022. An 
accompanying letter from the Superintendent to insurers outlined details of the 
expected implementation of the practices in this guide.  
Conassif has carried out i nternal audit work  to assess and strengthen Sugese’s 
supervisory approach and support the implementation of best practices in this area.

Public 
Cross-border reinsurance 
There is only one insurer that is authorised to provide , in addition to insurance,  
reinsurance locally and abroad. There is no requirement for insurers to reinsure 
with this insurer and, in practice, they are mostly depending on foreign reinsurers, 
in particular those based in the European Union (EU) and the United States.  
Insurers’ credit risk controls are assessed by supervisors as part of their review of 
reinsurance risk management. Sugese’s Reinsurance Assessment Guide also 
refers to the evaluation of the supervision in the reinsurer’s jurisdiction of origin, 
including checking that the reinsurer is directly or indirectly supervised and the 
possibility o f accessing reports, if any, of the parent group’s other involved 
supervisor. 
In practice, supervisors rely on the financial ratings of foreign reinsurers when 
assessing the security of reinsurance arrangements. Communication with the 
foreign supervisor of a reinsurer and broader supervisory issues would be triggered 
only in case significant risks were identified, which has not occurred recently. 
Liquidity risk management of the insurer 
A liquidity risk assessment is required as part of determining an insurer’s risk profile 
and how it is managed (Article 5 of Sugese Agreement 09-17). Reinsurance plays 
a major role in maintaining a prudent degree of liquidity of the insurer in both normal 
and stressed conditions. 
Ceding insurers are expected to consider the impact of their reinsurance 
programme on liquidity management within their RSSAs (Article 15 of Sugese 
Agreement 09-17). The reinsurer’s financial strength should be used as an indicator 
of the reinsurer’s ability to meet payment obligations.  
The reinsurer’s financial strength rating is also an input into the solvency capital 
requirement (SCR), which consider s credit and concentration risks arising from 
reinsurance. The impact of the reinsurance programme on the insurer’s capital 
adequacy and liquidity management should also be considered in the RSSA. 
Issues regarding risk transfer to capital markets 
The regulations do not explicitly consider risk transfer to capital markets. Although 
insurers may choose to undertake such transfers, the contracts would not be 
eligible for inclusion in the admissible assets backing technical provisions. They 
would therefore not be an effective means of risk transfer in practice. 
Assessment Largely Observed  
Comments There are extensive and detailed regulatory requirements on reinsurance, covering 
the impact of reinsurance on insurers’ credit, concentration and liquidity risks , risk 
management, and supervisory reporting requirements. Sugese actively supervises 
the effectiveness of the reinsurance risk management of the insurer, including 
reviewing contracts and programmes.

Public 
An assessment of the home supervision of foreign reinsurers is not performed in 
detail, with reliance placed instead on the financial strength ratings of the reinsurer 
(ICP 13.4).  
It is recommended that Sugese perform  a more detailed evaluation of the home 
supervision of foreign reinsurers. 
ICP 14 Valuation 
The supervisor establishes requirements for the valuation of assets and 
liabilities for solvency purposes. 
Description General framework 
Conassif has wide-ranging powers to set requirements on the accounting standards 
to be used by insurers for both regulatory reporting and in the preparation of audited 
financial statements (Article 171 of the SMRL). It sets out requirements, based on 
IFRS (with adjustments and variations), in Conassif Agreement s 06-18 and 10-21 
and Sugese’s Superintendent Agreement SGS-DES-A-028-2013.  
IFRS 4 is the standard that is currently implemented for insurance liabilities, with 
the interpretations of IFRS applied in their entirety. The standard includes 
requirements on recognition, derecognition and measurement of assets and notes 
to the financial statements. 
IFRS 17 (Insurance Contracts) was approved by the Inter national Accounting 
Standards Board (IASB) for implementation from 1 January 2023. However, 
Conassif and Sugese are still in the process of implementing IFRS 17. Although 
the regulatory framework for the adoption of the standard was approved by 
Conassif in August 2021, t he effective date of implementation (originally 
January 2024) has been deferred more than once to allow market participants to 
prepare. A revised date of implementation of 1 January 2028  was announced in 
December 2025.  
Similarly, the regulatory framework for the adoption of  IFRS 9 (Financial 
Instruments) was also approved by Conassif in August 2021. The decision to delay 
the implementation of IFRS 17 also allowed for the delay in implementation of 
IFRS 9 if it was to be implemented jointly with IFRS 17.  
However, the regulations do allow for the early adoption of IFRS 9 at the discretion 
of each insurer.  
Insurers are required to submit an independent external auditor’s opinion on the 
audited financial information (Article 33 of Conassif Agreement 06-18).  
Economic valuation of assets and liabilities 
When addressing capital requirements, the IMRL states that regulations on capital 
will consider the valuation of assets and liabilities at their economic value (Article 12 
of the IMRL). There are specific requirements o n the valuation of assets and 
liabilities for solvency purposes (Sugese Agreement 02 -13). These include the

Public 
valuation of technical provisions, capital resources, investment requirements and 
capital adequacy requirements.   
The prescribed methodology for the valuation of liabilities is set out in Article 18 of 
Sugese Agreement 02 -13 and the annexes providing the calculations of an 
insurer’s technical reserves (Annexes PT1–PT7 of Sugese Agreement 02-13). The 
calculation of the technical interest rate used for the valuation of personal insurance 
products is set out in the guidelines (Chapter IV of Superintendent 
Agreement SGS-DES-A-028-2013). 
This approach does not apply a prospective assessment of cash  flows for most 
liabilities, does not allow for the time value of money (with the exception of personal 
insurance) and may not produce such an economic valuation in practice.  
For assets, Article 4 of Conassif Agreement 10-21 establishes that insurers must 
maintain investments in eligible assets that are valued in line with IFRS, which 
measures assets at a fair value. 
Consistent and reliable valuation of assets and liabilities 
The regulations are prescriptive on the approach to be used, with little flexibility for 
insurers to vary in their application of the regulation. This provides for valuation 
bases that are consistent between insurers.  
The use of fair value valuation for assets and less economic techniques for the 
valuation of liabilities may lead to inconsistencies in the balance sheet. These 
inconsistencies would be expected to be more prevalent for long -term insurance 
products, where economic assumptions have a greater impact on the valuation, but 
these types of products are currently uncommon in the insurance market. 
Use of own credit standing in the valuation of technical provisions and other 
liabilities 
The use of own credit standing is not permitted by the regulations on the valuation 
of technical provisions.  
Gross reserves are calculated without discounting reinsurance. No account is taken 
of a reinsurer’s credit standing. 
Technical provisions exceed the MOCE by a margin 
There are no explicit margins of risk over a current estimate included in the 
valuation of liabilities (as defined in Article 18 of Sugese Agreement 02 -13), with 
the exception of the valuation of liabilities arising from surety bonds. 
Unbiased and current assumptions reflected in the present value of future 
cash flows and MOCE 
Article 18 of Sugese Agreement 02 -13 indicates that technical reserves must 
contain the true or estimated amount of the obligations assumed, derived from the 
contracts, as well as the expenses related to compliance with those obligations , 
and must be calculated for their entire term regardless of the type of policy payment. 
However, there is no requirement to incorporate unbiased assumptions in to the 
valuation of cash flows.

Public 
Use of appropriate rates when discounting cash flows for technical 
provisions 
The current regulations for the calculation of technical provisions do not include the 
discounting of cash flows for most insurance products.  
The methodology for calculating a technical interest rate applicable to personal 
insurance is set out in Article 24 of Superintendent  
Agreement SGS-DES-A-029-2013. The rate is set by Sugese and published each 
year based on a fixed formula. 
Embedded options reflected in technical provisions 
The current regulation does not include specific requirements on the valuation of 
embedded options in the calculation of technical provisions. However, given the 
nature of the market, such embedded options are not a common feature in the 
products sold and would not be expected to constitute a material risk for any 
insurer. 
Supervisory oversight 
Insurers are required to comply at all times with regulations on the level of technical 
reserves and must submit a report on the adequacy of technical reserves prepared 
by an independent actuary every year. Sugese may also request that insurers 
demonstrate the adequacy of their reserves at any time. If the regulations are not 
met, Sugese can require the insurer to take action to resolve any issue (Article 21 
of Sugese Agreement 02-13). 
Assessment Largely Observed 
Comments The current valuation methodology is prescriptive and underpinned by IFRS 4 
accounting standards. The process of implementing IFRS 17 has started and the 
expected implementation date for solvency purposes is  1 January 2028 . The 
valuation of liabilities does not specify the use of a prospective approach based on 
projected future cash flows. There is no requirement to use unbiased cash flows or 
to include MOCE  (ICPs 14.6–14.8). There is also no allowance for the cost of  
options and guarantees in the valuation of insurance liabilities (ICP 14.10).  
However, the local insurance market does not write significant quantities of 
complex, long-term life insurance products and there are no products containing 
options or guarantees. These features could be expected to mitigate many of these 
issues. 
Sugese takes action when insurers are not reserving adequately. 
It is recommended that  Conassif and Sugese  upgrade their valuation standards 
(implementation of IFRS 17, as planned, will assist). 
ICP 15 Investments 
The supervisor establishes regulatory investment requirements for solvency 
purposes in order for insurers to make appropriate investments taking 
account of the risks they face.

Public 
Description Regulatory investment requirements 
Insurers’ total investments comprise around 70% of the total in bonds, of which 
60% are issued by the local public sector and 20% are foreign bonds. They also 
invest in loans (13%), cash (7%) and shares (6%). There is limited investment in 
collective inves tment schemes. The sector does not invest in more complex 
instruments such as hedge funds. 
There are requirements for insurers for solvency purposes in the IMRL and Sugese 
Agreement 02-13. 
The IMRL sets out high-level requirements that: 
• Insurers maintain investments in eligible assets, as defined by regulations, 
backing technical provisions etc (Article 14);  
• Investments be owned by the insurer, free of any encumbrances etc that could 
block or hinder their free assignment or transfer (Article 14);  
• Insurers manage investments in a sound and prudent manner; they must define 
their investment policy and processes for their choice and mix of financial 
assets, diversification and risk management (Article 15; there is a similar 
requirement in Article 25 of Sugese Agreement 02-13); and 
• Subject to regulations, which may exclude securities based on market or 
liquidity risk, investments be issued by supervised financial institutions, 
commercial securities and private offerings (including equivalent foreign issues) 
or securities issued by the CBCR and the MoF; the securities must be traded in 
accordance with sound market practices and held in custody, where applicable, 
by an authorised custodian (Article 15(b–f)). 
There is no detailed framework of limits on the share of particular types of 
investment in the total. However, insurers must hold at least 40% of their total 
investments backing technical reserves in securities meeting certain requirements 
including on currency, risk rating  (at least one investment rating assigned by an 
authorised rating entity) and security type (including bonds etc as listed in Article 15 
of the IMRL, as well as shares in mutual funds, except for hedge funds or alternative 
investment funds, and certain repo transactions and shares) (Articles 23 and 27 of 
Sugese Agreement 02-13). 
There are otherwise no quantitative limitations on investments for solvency 
purposes. The SCR itself takes a risk-based approach to the risks associated with 
investment, particularly in the calculation of general asset risk (Annex RCS -1 of 
Sugese Agreement 02-13 and Superintendent Agreement SGS-DES-A-029-2013; 
see ICP 17).  
For example, investments in shares or other equity securities, investment funds 
and other variable -income securities are subject to a market risk capital 
requirement equivalent to 30% of their book value.  
Assets issued by companies related to the same group as that of the insurer will 
not be taken into account for solvency purposes (Article 14 of the IMRL). However, 
there are no explicit requirements on investments that apply to an insurance group,

Public 
such as a requirement to monitor and control risks on an aggregate basis for the 
group (ICP 15.1).  
The regulations on consolidated supervision of financial groups do include 
requirements on group RM, capital adequacy and reporting of supervisory 
information (see ICP 23). There are currently no insurance groups, although 
one insurer heads a financial group to which these requirements are applied. 
Requirements to ensure that assets are secure, available and diversified 
In addition to the IMRL requirements, Sugese Agreement 02 -13, as part of its 
requirements on investment policies, requires insurers to administer their 
investments in a sound and reasonable manner and hold their assets in a secure 
location for their availability (Article 25). The principles to be covered in investment 
policies i nclude that liquid assets (less than one year) must be kept to back 
technical reserves (Article 25(d)). Insurers must also maintain appropriately 
diversified investments (Article 25(b)).  
In respect to custody of assets, in addition to IMRL requirements, Sugese 
Agreement 02-13 requires that securities that can be kept in custody be held by a 
custodian meeting certain requirements (including specific standards applicable to 
international custodians). The insurer must authorise direct access to their custody 
accounts for Sugese for the purposes of supervision (Article 26(b)). 
Although the assets that meet the 40% requirement (see above) must have an 
investment rating ( Article 27(3) of Sugese Agreement 02 -13), there is no 
requirement for the insurer to conduct its own due diligence to assess credit risk or 
credit analysis independent of the external credit rating (ICP 15.2). 
Article 25(c) of Sugese Agreement 02-13 requires insurers to establish sustainable 
investment management policies, for example to ensure that resources go to 
activities that support sustainable development. However, insurers are not explicitly 
required to consider how climate change could have a material impact on their 
investments through traditional risk categories (ICP 15.2). 
Requirements to ensure that assets are appropriate given the liabilities 
Insurers must structure their asset portfolios to be consistent with the nature and 
duration of their insurance obligations with respect to term, currency and balance 
of risk and expected return (Article 25(a) of Sugese Agreement 02-13). 
RM requirements  
Insurers must define and approve the investment policy to select and mix assets, 
diversification and RM. They must invest only in assets whose risk may be 
monitored, managed and controlled. Assets must be invested considering the joint 
safety, liquidity and profitability of the portfolio (Article 25 of Sugese 
Agreement 02-13). 
There is no explicit requirement that insurers have sufficient information about an 
investment to understand the risks involved and determine the materiality of the 
risk before undertaking any investments (ICP 15.4). However, insurers must 
maintain an inventory of all the data necessary for adequate investment

Public 
management, including details of the investment’s  valuation for accounting 
purposes and the custody institution (Article 25(a) of Sugese Agreement 02-13).  
Where an external investment adviser or manager is used, there is no requirement 
for the insurer to develop appropriate techniques to assess risks (including risks in 
an investment fund, where the insurer is not able to look through to the underlying 
assets), nor are insurers required to retain adequate investment expertise in-house 
(ICP 15.4). 
Quantitative and qualitative requirements for certain types of assets 
Although there are relevant solvency requirements, there are no specific limits on 
investment in instruments that are more complex and less transparent (ICP 15.5).  
There are, however, detailed requirements on the use of derivatives, which specify 
that insurers may carry out such transactions to hedge interest rate, price and 
exchange rate risk (Title IV of Sugese Agreement 02-13). The requirements list the 
types of instrument that may be used and set out qualitative requirements. In 
practice, insurers use derivatives on a limited basis and have no investments in 
more complex or potentially illiquid investments such as private equity or structured 
products.  
Supervisory work 
Sugese collects detailed information on the composition of investment portfolios 
(see ICP 9) and evaluates investment risk as part of its off -site supervisory work, 
including in the context of assessing compliance with solvency standards. It also 
considers investment risk in its assessment of the RSSA (see ICP 16).  
Investment policies and the associated RM, reporting and controls may be 
assessed as part of Sugese’s risk -based supervisory work. Sugese’s internal 
guidelines on risk assessment (see ICP 9) include detailed material on the 
assessment of investments, and evaluations of investment s were included in the 
supervisory material shared by Sugese for the purposes of the assessment. They 
take into account the fact that, in practice, investments are concentrated in a small 
range of higher-quality liquid assets, as noted above.  
Assessment Largely Observed  
Comments There are extensive, mostly qualitative regulatory investment requirements for 
solvency purposes, including on security, settlement, custody etc, as well as 
solvency requirements that incentivise a prudent investment approach. Quantitative 
requirements are  limited but support a minimum amount of investment in 
highest-quality, relatively liquid investments.  
There are no quantitative requirements on group investment risks (ICP 15.1) or 
requirements o n investments in instruments that are more complex and less 
transparent (ICP 15.5). In addition, insurers are not explicitly required to conduct 
their own due diligence to assess credit risk independent of the external credit rating 
or to consider how clima te change could have a material impact on their 
investments through traditional risk categories (ICP 15.2).

Public 
Sugese does, however, address investment activities and risk in its risk -based 
supervisory work, including on -site supervision and the evaluation of insurers’ 
RSSAs. This takes into account the nature of insurers’ activities, which do not yet 
include significant long -term business, and their investment portfolios, which are 
concentrated in government and other public sector bonds.    
It is recommended that Sugese, working with Conassif as required: 
• Strengthen the qualitative requirements on insurers’ investment activities to 
ensure they are required to assess credit risk independent of the external credit 
rating and to consider how climate change could have a material impact on their 
investments;  
• Review the existing quantitative requirements (40% of investments in 
high-quality assets), including whether to apply limits to instruments that are 
more complex and less transparent; and  
• Review the requirements applying to financial groups to ensure that groups are 
required to manage investments on a consolidated basis, identifying risk 
concentrations etc. 
ICP 16 Enterprise Risk Management for Solvency Purposes 
The supervisor requires the insurer to establish within its risk management 
system an enterprise risk management (ERM) framework for solvency 
purposes to identify, measure, report and manage the insurer’s risks in an 
ongoing and integrated manner. 
Description Risk identification 
Insurers are required to establish an RMS and internal controls (Article 5 of Sugese 
Agreement 09-17). The RMS should identify, measure, monitor, control and report 
all risk-related matters (see also ICP 8).  
Article 5 of Sugese Agreement 09 -17 also sets out minimum requirements for the 
RMS, including a risk management strategy  and processes and tools for risk 
identification, measurement and monitoring. Article 8(f) requires insurers to have 
processes to coordinate risk management with capital management and solvency 
adequacy, considering, among st other factors, technical, ALM, liquidity and 
concentration, operational, business conduct and reinsurance risks. 
The Board is responsible for ensuring implementation of the RMS and the internal 
control processes (Article 6 of Sugese Agreement 09 -17) and for defining the  
applicable strategies and policies. The Board is required to approve the risk 
management strategy, the relevant policies in pursuit of the strategy, and updates 
and material changes to the RMS (Article 8). Regular reporting on risks to Senior 
Management and the Board is also required (see also ICP 8). 
Sugese plans to strengthen ERM requirements in the context of its planned 
implementation of IFRS 17 and development of a risk-based capital model, part of 
the 2024–28 strategic plan (see ICPs 14 and 17). 
Risk measurement

Public 
Article 8 of Sugese Agreement 09 -17 requires insurers to measure risks. The 
current capital and solvency framework (see ICP 17) uses a factor -based 
quantification of risks. Insurers are not required to perform stress tests, but may do 
so on a voluntary basis (see below).  
Qualitative considerations of risks should also form part of an insurer’s risk appetite 
statement (see below). 
Risk appetite statement, limits and capital adequacy 
Insurers are required to have a risk appetite statement (Article 10 of Sugese 
Agreement 09-17) including quantitative and qualitative considerations of risks and 
risk limits and the type s of risk the insurer is willing to withstand within its risk 
capacity. The required risk appetite statement does not have to include analysis of 
risk interdependencies or aggregate risk levels.  
The insurer’s Board is required to define and monitor the risk appetite statement 
and ensure alignment with the insurer’s risk management  strategy, capital 
management and (other) business activities. 
Underwriting and investment policies , as well as the RSSA (see below), must be 
consistent with the insurer’s risk appetite. 
Sugese reviews risk appetite in a process that includes checking the consistency 
of the risk appetite statement with strategic and financial plans, the quantitative 
measures applied, and alignment with risk appetite and capacity. The risk profile of 
the insurer, including the identification and quantification of significant current and 
emerging risks, is checked against the risk appetite statement. Sugese can and 
does issue requests to ins urers for improvement to their approaches in relevant 
areas based on these reviews. 
Conassif has the power (Article 10 of the IMRL) to require insurers to undertake 
analysis of dependencies or interrelations between risk appetite, risk limits, 
regulatory capital requirements, economic capital and the processes for monitoring 
risks. It has not used these powers to date. 
ALM policy 
Although there is no explicit requirement for an ALM policy, in Article 8(f) of Sugese 
Agreement 09-17, “asset and liability management” is explicitly listed as within the 
scope of consideration of the required relationship between risk and capital 
management and solvency adequacy. 
In relation to the required provision for catastrophe risk (Annex PT7 of Sugese 
Agreement 02 -13), as part of liquidity management, within the management of 
assets and liabilities, a plan is required for claims payouts in case of an event. 
Investment policy 
Article 25 of Sugese Agreement 02-13 requires an investment policy approved by 
the board. The policy must cover the selection and mix of assets, diversification 
and risk management. Investment risk appetite and limits must be included as 
integral elements of the overall risk appetite statement (Article 10(c) of Sugese 
Agreement 09-17).

Public 
Supervisory teams include in their risk -based work reviews of an insurer’s 
investment policy, its investment portfolios and their alignment with risk appetite. In 
case of significant investment activity, investment risk is evaluated as part of the 
risk management assessment of an insurer. 
Underwriting policy 
Insurers must have an underwriting policy approved by the Board in order to be 
authorised (Annex 3 of Sugese Agreement 01 -21). There is a requirement for 
policies, approved by the board, to fulfil the risk management strategy (Article 8(a) 
of Sugese Agreement 09-17).  
The evaluation of the underwriting policy is the responsibility of the actuarial 
function (Article 26 of Sugese Agreement 09-17). 
Supervisors assess whether the underwriting policy addresses underwriting risks 
in line with the insurer’s overall risk appetite statement. There are no regulatory 
requirements that specify what must be covered by the underwriting policy . 
However, under Sugese’s risk -based supervisory approach, supervisors seek to 
review the underwriting policies only of those products identified as part of a 
significant activity of the insurer. 
Liquidity risk management 
Article 8(f) of Sugese Agreement 09 -17 lists liquidity risk as one of the required 
elements of the RMS. A liquidity policy is required as part of the authorisation 
process (Annex 3 of Sugese Agreement 01 -21). Liquidity must be considered as 
part of the insurer’s investment policy, under which liquid assets with maturities of 
less than one year should be held to support technical provisions (Article 25(d) of 
Sugese Agreement 02-13). Assets must be invested considering the joint safety, 
liquidity and profitability of the portfolio. 
Supervisors evaluate insurers’ liquidity risk strategies, policies, processes and 
stress tests in addition to  monitoring their liquidity ratios and external sources of 
liquidity (processes are set out in Sugese’s internal General Guide to Risk Profile 
Assessment; see ICP 9). The supervisory team also includes a liquidity crisis 
scenario on its checklist as part of the RSSA review. 
As the insurance products and services prevalent at present in the market do not 
pose significant liquidity risks, there is no need to require a more detailed liquidity 
risk management process (ICP 16.9).  
The RSSA 
Insurers are required to prepare an RSSA on an annual basis as part of the RMS 
(Article 15 of Sugese Agreement 09-17). The Board and Senior Management have 
ultimate responsibility for the RSSA (Articles 6 and 7 of the Agreement). The RSSA 
must cover overall solvency needs based on the risk profile, include tolerance limits 
and take into account business strategy. It must be based on ongoing compliance 
with requirements on capital and technical provisions. 
Sugese’s internal General Guide to Risk Profile Assessment lists the risks to be 
covered, including insurance, credit, market, concentration, operational and 
liquidity risks. Insurers are not required to carry out stress testing (ICP 16.15) as

Public 
part of the ERM framework, although Conassif has powers to issue regulations that 
would require stress tests to be undertaken (Article 10 of the IMRL). 
Where stress testing is carried out by insurers on a voluntary basis, it is assessed 
by the supervisory team. The results would not trigger requests or requirements for 
additional capital, but an action plan would be required of the insurer to prevent the 
realisation of the scenario, where necessary. 
The time horizon of the RSSA is usually one year, in alignment with the business 
plan, and longer-term considerations are not routinely covered (ICP 16.14). 
Supervisory reviews of the RSSA 
Sugese carries out annual supervisory reviews of insurers’ RMS and risk profiles. 
However, RSSAs are submitted by insurers to Sugese only when it requests 
submission (and not as a routine submission).  
Examples of recent supervisory reviews of RSSAs showed that Sugese has made 
requests for improvements for the next RSSA submission. These covered many 
issues, including liquidity risk management, market and/or macroeconomic stress 
testing and analysis of the sensitivity of the solvenc y ratio to various variables. It 
has requested the inclusion of certain risk types such as strategic, legal, 
reputational and operational risks, as well as climate risks.  
Recovery planning (pre -emptive scenarios and options for recovery under 
severe stress) 
There is no requirement in regulations nor a supervisory practice (or any plans) for 
insurers to develop (and share with Sugese) pre -emptive recovery planning , 
including recovery scenarios and possible options for recovery under severe stress.  
Group ERM 
There are no regulated insurance groups (see ICP 23). However, the one domestic 
insurer that is part of a wider group includes group -related risks qualitatively in its 
ERM framework and considers group risks in its RSSA. 
Assessment Partly Observed 
Comments There are many requirements on ERM for solvency purposes, including the 
requirement for an ERM framework and for insurers to have investment, 
underwriting and liquidity policies ; basic ALM considerations ; a risk appetite 
statement and an RSSA, which contains elements of what is expected of an ORSA 
in ICP 16 . Furthermore, supervisory practice, based mainly on extensive internal 
guidelines, especially on risk assessment, is thorough, including reviews and 
detailed recommendations.   
However, there is a lack of granularity in the requirements in relation to major risk 
management tools for solvency purposes. There are limited requirements for 
quantitative tools and in respect to risk interdependencies (ICPs 16.1–16.4) and a 
full ALM policy (ICP 16.5). The requirements on insurers’ RSSAs do not extend to 
making them an integral part of strategic decision-making, capital management and 
the risk management process, nor do they require insurers to build in a longer-term

Public 
perspective (ICPs 16.10–16.14). No pre-emptive recovery scenarios or options for 
recovery are required of insurers (ICP 16.15). 
In the future, Sugese, working with Conassif, intends to introduce measures to 
strengthen requirements on insurers’ ERM for solvency purposes in the context of 
the planned implementation of IFRS 17 (see ICP 14) and the related work on a 
risk-based capital model (ICP 17).  
It is recommended that Sugese, working with Conassif as required: 
• Expand the RSSA requirements to include a requirement for quantitative 
assessment of solvency over an extended time horizon; 
• Consider developing a framework for recovery planning for insurers; and 
• Develop more quantitative requirements, for example for stress testing and 
ALM. 
ICP 17 Capital Adequacy 
The supervisor establishes capital adequacy requirements for solvency 
purposes so that insurers can absorb significant un expected losses and to 
provide for different degrees of supervisory measures. 
Description Overall framework 
Legislation sets out high -level provisions on the capital adequacy and solvency 
regime, empowering Conassif to issue regulations and setting minimum required 
levels of capital (Articles 10–12 of the IMRL). Conassif regulations set out detailed 
standards on the valuation of assets and liabilities (ICP 14), capital adequacy and 
solvency requirements, technical provisions and the investment regime (Sugese 
Agreement 02-13). 
In addition, and as required under Article 4 of Sugese Agreement 02 -13, Sugese 
has issued guidelines for the implementation of the regulations (the latest version 
is Superintendent Agreement SGS-DES-A-029-2013). 
Insurers are required to apply IFRS for solvency purposes (Article 3 of Conassif 
Agreement 06 -18). IFRS 4 is currently applied (see ICP 14). They must hold 
sufficient capital to cover at least their estimated technical, credit, market and 
operational risks (Article 12 of the IMRL). The risks to be taken into account are 
defined in Article 11 of Sugese Agreement 02 -13. The regulations also prescribe 
the definition and calculation of capital resources (Articles 5 –8 of Sugese 
Agreement 02-13).  
Insurers are also required to hold sufficient assets to cover technical reserves 
(Article 22 of Sugese Agreement 02 -13). All assets included in the balance sheet 
are eligible except those deducted from capital (Article 23). In addition, at least 40% 
of assets backing technical reserves must be invested in debt securities that meet 
a set of requirements (Article 27 of Sugese Agreement 02-13) (ICP 15). 
Although most of the framework dates from 2013, Sugese has regularly revised 
details of the requirements. Most significantly:

Public 
• In 2021 it reformed the requirements for catastrophe risk, adding separate 
capital requirements to cover earthquakes and volcanic eruptions; and 
• In 2022 it added a treatment for surety bond insurance to the solvency 
requirements to reflect its particular risk characteristics for the insurer. 
The changes were implemented after consultation with insurers and apply to all. At 
the time of the assessment, Sugese was considering further revisions to the 
solvency requirements to reflect market developments and with a view to aligning 
certain requirements with those applying to banks, where possible.   
The requirements also apply to branches of foreign insurers (there is one at 
present), subject to a separate definition of capital base (capital assigned to the 
Costa Rican operations of the insurer) and a requirement to notify Sugese in case 
of breaches of the solvency requirements applying to the foreign parent (Articles 5 
and 14 of Sugese Agreement 02-13).  
Regulatory capital requirements  
Insurers must hold capital sufficient to cover the SCR and, at a minimum, the capital 
amounts required under Article 11 of the IMRL (fixed amounts by category of 
insurance business). 
Sugese Agreement 02-13 sets out the detail of the SCR, which is defined as the 
sum of the capital requirements for each of six categories of risk: general asset risk, 
operational risk, personal insurance risk, general insurance risk, reinsurance ceded 
risk and catastrophe risk (Annexes RCS -1-6 of Sugese Agreement 02 -13 and 
further details in Superintendent Agreement SGS-DES-A-029-2013). There is no 
allowance for diversification of risks.  
Requirements for each risk category are specified in detail (for example, for general 
asset risk, there are requirements for market risk, asset risk, investment 
concentration and mismatch risks). Concentration risk requirements also apply in 
the case of reinsurance ceded risk and catastrophe risk.  
The calibration of the capital requirement varies. For most risk categories, a 
factor-based approach is used with static parameters (for example, in the case of 
personal insurance risk, the capital requirement is a fixed percentage of different 
types of reserve, the highest being for the unearned premium reserve for workers’ 
compensation, accidents and health).  
The target criteria, where stated, also vary for different risks. For example, within 
the general insurance risk category, for direct insurance risk, a calibration target of 
99.5% value-at-risk (VaR) over one year is specified, whereas within the general 
asset risk category, for market risk on fixed income assets, a calibration target of 
95% VaR over 21 days is used. Other risks are calibrated without specific target 
criteria. Although the calibrations have been updated periodically, the approach is 
not consistent across risks.  
The use of internal models for calculating solvency requirements is not permitted.   
Total balance sheet approach 
Although all material elements of the balance sheet are captured, the methodology 
does not consider the balance sheet as a whole for solvency purposes. For

Public 
example, it does not capture the interaction of risks in all elements of the balance 
sheet or allow for the recognition of interdependence among st assets, liabilities, 
regulatory capital requirements and capital resources. 
In addition, the capital requirement calculations do not account for correlation 
between risks.   
Regulatory intervention levels 
When an insurer’s capital adequacy ratio (CAR) falls below a minimum threshold 
(currently set at 130%, expressed as a minimum capital sufficiency index of 1.3), 
the insurer is required to provide an action plan to restore the ratio to the minimum 
(Article 15 of Sugese Agreement 02-13).  
Failure to submit a plan, supervisory disapproval of the plan or non-compliance by 
the insurer may result in more intensive actions being taken by Sugese as defined 
for an insurer with a risk rating of 5 (non-viable entity or imminent insolvency). The 
Comprehensive Insurance Supervision Framework in Sugese Agreement 10 -17 
sets out when an insurer is rated at level 5 (ICP 9). 
The calculation of the CAR is designed for a going -concern insurer, and 
calculations related to identifying equity that could absorb losses in the event of 
liquidation are not explicitly included in the model. 
The minimum threshold of 130% acts as a prescribed capital requirement (PCR) 
as defined in ICP 17.4. Sugese may still intervene above this level, where an insurer 
is rated at level 2 (Early Warning) under the Comprehensive Insurance Supervision 
Framework (see above), when, amongst other supervisory measures, it may 
request an increase in capital.   
However, there is no further intervention level that would equate to an MCR, ie the 
level at which, if breached, Sugese would intervene with its strongest measures.  
Where an insurer is rated a t level 5 (see above), Sugese will ask Conassif to 
intervene (Article 28 of Sugese Agreement 10 -17), which it would do using its 
powers in Article 156 of the SMRL and Articles 139 and 140 of the CBCRL (see 
ICP 12). However, there is no specified level of capi tal adequacy associated with 
level 5 other than that the insurer has not met the solvency requirements. 
Group-wide capital adequacy 
The capital adequacy requirements for financial groups and conglomerates (see 
ICP 23) are set out in Title IV (Articles 79–113) of Conassif Agreement 16-22 (this 
regulation applies to all legal entities subject to supervision by a superintendency 
rather than just insurers). In practice, it is applied to the one insurer-led group (see 
ICP 23).  
Group capital adequacy is calculated by reference to the requirements applying to 
individual legal entities within the group. The requirements and capital resources of 
each legal entity are added together to provide a measure of group-level capital 
adequacy and monitored. The solvency control level for groups is 100% of the 
group CAR (Article 83 of Conassif Agreement 16-22).  
Within the group, the controlling company must ensure it has the level of capital 
necessary for its operation, based on its risks and activities ( Article 110(a) of the

Public 
Conassif Agreement) . This must not be less than the risk -based capital 
requirements of the relevant supervisors. The controlling company must eliminate 
any individual capital deficit within six months (Article 110(b)). 
Capital resources 
The regulations set out the method for calculating capital resources (capital base)  
and classifying capital based on quality and contribution to the insurer ’s ability to 
absorb unexpected losses not covered by technical provisions.  
When determining capital base (comprising Tier 1 capital, Tier 2 capital and 
deductions), there are specific considerations concerning both the items and the 
asset quality that are considered for solvency measurement purposes, for example 
the conditions th at must be met for debt capital to be included in Tier 2  
(Articles 5–8 of Sugese Agreement 02-13). 
The contribution of total Tier 2 capital to an insurer’s capital base may not exceed 
100% of total Tier 1 capital net of deductions (Article 5).  
Expected future developments  
As noted in the assessment of ICP 14, Sugese is in the process of implementing 
IFRS 17 in accordance with a regulation issued by Conassif. IFRS 17 will replace 
IFRS 4 as the basis of valuation standards for solvency purposes. Initially, Sugese 
plans to recalibrate the capital requirements to deliver the same levels of capital as 
are currently required.  
At a later stage, it plans to introduce a fully risk-based solvency regime with capital 
requirements recalibrated, continuing to use the IFRS balance sheet. The timeline 
for implementing this risk-based regime is currently unspecified and will depend on 
the timeline for implementing IFRS 17 (see ICP 14). 
Assessment Partly Observed 
Comments There is an extensive framework of capital requirements that addresses the 
material risks of insurers. Insurers are required to calculate capital requirements for 
six risk types and to maintain a capital base (in mostly high-quality forms of capital) 
of at least 130% of the requirements. Although there are no insurance groups at 
present, a framework for group-wide capital requirements is in place and is applied 
in practice to the one insurer-led financial group (see ICP 23).  
The framework has been revised periodically to capture risks more appropriately. 
It will be revised again to reflect the planned implementation of IFRS 17 and, in the 
longer term , reformed to provide for a more fully risk -based approach (in the 
absence of any domestic IAIGs, the ICS does not have to be implemented).  
Sugese is satisfied that current requirements, which are being tested by regular 
stress testing exercises (see ICP 24), provide adequately for insurers to be able to 
absorb unexpected losses. However, the current approach is not based on the full 
balance sheet (ICP 17.1), is backward -looking rather than prospective and lacks 
explicit target criteria for the calibration of requirements applied consistently across 
risks (ICP 17.8). It is not supported by a full framework of solvency control levels,

Public 
lacking a defined MCR at which point strongest measures would be taken 
(ICPs 17.3–17.4).  
The group solvency control level , at 100%, is below the level set for individual 
insurers at 130%. However, there are currently no insurance groups operating in 
the market. 
It is recommended that Sugese, working with Conassif: 
• Revise the approach to solvency control levels, providing in the regulations for 
an explicit MCR level linked to triggers for taking the strongest intervention 
measures, taking into account the recommendations on ICP 12 in this 
assessment; 
• Review and revise the capital requirements, as planned, in two stages, to reflect 
IFRS implementation and, in the medium term, to implement a fully risk-based 
solvency regime based on the whole balance sheet and target criteria for 
calibration applied consistently across risks;   
• In the short term (because the timing of planned reforms is uncertain and will 
take some years), continue to keep the adequacy of current requirements under 
review and strengthen them where necessary; and 
• Consider revising the solvency control level for insurance groups i n case one 
were to be established in the market. 
ICP 18 Intermediaries 
The supervisor sets and enforces requirements for the conduct of insurance 
intermediaries, in order that they conduct business in a professional and 
transparent manner. 
Description At the time of the assessment, there were 22 insurance agencies ( companies 
undertaking agency business), 1,209 individual insurance agents, 38 brokerages, 
799 individual brokers and 83 operators of self -issued policies (non -complex 
insurance meeting the requirements of Article 24 of the IMRL). Brokers have the 
most significant market share. Banks can and do establish brokerages (as separate 
companies) and may also sell self-issued policies (see ICP 19) themselves (but not 
other products), their total market share being around one third.  
Intermediaries are supervised by Sugese’s Supervision Division with two dedicated 
staff. 
Licensing requirements 
Requirements for intermediaries are set out primarily in the IMRL and Sugese 
Agreements 03 -10 and 06 -13. Laws and regulations require insurance 
intermediaries, whether individuals or legal entities, to obtain a licence from Sugese 
to carry out their insurance activities. Sugese is responsible for their supervision.  
The main provisions of the IMRL are:

Public 
• Intermediation requires authorisation subject to regulations issued by Conassif 
on the granting of licen ces, including training and accreditation requirements 
and authorisation processes (Article 19).   
• Insurance agents and brokers must obtain a licence from Sugese authorising 
them to operate in specific insurance lines. Applicants must not have a licence 
cancellation or criminal conviction  in the last five years , be serving a criminal 
sentence or be holding key positions or activities with insurers/financial groups 
that may create a conflict of interest (Article 20).   
• Insurance agents must be accredited by an insurer , and individuals acting as 
brokers by a brokerage, which is to be responsible for the appropriate training, 
accreditation and compliance of intermediaries (Article 21). 
• An agent’s contracts must state whether they work on behalf of insurers or 
independently (Article 22). 
Articles 22–4 of Sugese Agreement 01-21 contain the requirements and processes 
of the authorisation process, including supervisory work and timelines, and the 
requirements that then apply for registration (when the intermediary may start 
operating).  
Brokers must be established as separate legal entities and may not operate as 
branches of foreign entities. However, there are no restrictions for the ownership 
structure. As mentioned, banks may only sell self-issued products, so the scope for 
bancassurance is limited.   
Sugese’s website displays all licences granted to intermediaries, whether they are 
active, inactive, cancelled or the object of a sanction. 
Ongoing supervision 
Article 26 of the IMRL requires intermediaries to provide Sugese with accurate and 
timely information that allows for effective supervision. Article 3 of Superintendent 
Agreement SGS-DES-A-021-2013 sets out the periodic information required to be 
reported within stated deadlines, mainly financial information on insurance 
agencies and brokerages.  
Sugese performs on -site reviews of intermediaries approximately three times a 
year, mainly to verify compliance. As mentioned, Sugese has only two staff working 
full-time on intermediary supervision (off-site and inspections), and the scope and 
approach of the inspections are therefore limited. Sugese schedules reviews 
according to level of risk , but has not yet adopted a business conduct framework 
(see ICP 19) and prioritises intermediaries that are part of financial groups subject 
to consolidated supervision. Evidence from the reviews is documented in Sugese’s 
EES system.     
Sugese also exercises some supervision indirectly via insurers. Article 12 of 
Sugese Agreement 06-13, which regulates consumer protection, requires insurers 
to address compliance issues related to intermediaries ’ claim management and 
customer service responsibilities. However, Sugese’s conduct of business 
supervision is currently limited.  
Professional knowledge and competence

Public 
Article 20 of the IMRL lists suitability requirements for licensing. These include 
integrity and absence of unethical behaviour and conflicts of interest for individuals 
applying for an intermediary licence.  
For insurance brokers and agents, Article 21 of the IMRL and Article 14 of 
Superintendent Agreement DES-A-049-2016 require insurers and brokerages to be 
responsible for the professional education and continuous training of their 
intermediaries. Article 22 of Sugese Agreement 03-10 requires supervised training 
by insurers and brokerages at least once a year, aimed at updating intermediaries 
in technical and regulatory fields. Training is also provided by the intermediary 
association.  
Intermediary education and training are updated and tracked on the Sugese online 
platform (Registration Service for Individual Intermediaries). In practice, during 
on-site supervision visits, intermediaries submit their training plans to Sugese and 
requirements are set to update approved training plans.  
All education and training requirements are subject to ongoing supervision by 
Sugese, as well as verification during on-site inspections.  
Governance 
Insurance agencies and brokerages are subject to the governance requirements 
applying generally to the insurance sector and other financial institutions (Article 2.3 
of Conassif Agreement 04-16 includes all Sugese-regulated legal entities within its 
scope). They must implement governance  structures including responsible 
governing bodies, risk management and appropriate internal control mechanisms. 
Article 5 states that the intermediary’s governing body is responsible for strategy 
and internal organisation through its implementation of effective controls. Article 8 
covers the approval of risk management policies and compliance supervision, and 
Article 23 establishes the obligation to approve policies to identify, prevent and 
manage conflicts of interest (see also ICP 7). 
Under Sugese Agreement 01-21, authorisation of a brokerage or agency requires 
the assessment of its corporate governance structure.  
In practice, Sugese reviews the corporate governance structures of intermediaries 
as part of on-site supervision and imposes relevant requirements. Its work covers 
conflicts of interest within corporate operations. 
Disclosure requirements 
Under Article 26 of the IMRL, intermediaries must disclose accurate information on 
insurance contracts and give insureds advice and assistance on claims. They must 
provide accurate and timely information on the conditions of the insured’s risk as 
well as the covered amount, the validity of the contract and the processes 
applicable to claims. They must also inform customers if they are operating on 
behalf of the insurer or independently. 
Sugese Agreement 03-10 sets out business terms and conditions for inclusion in 
appointment contracts between insurers and intermediaries, including minimum 
disclosures, obligations, remuneration and working relationships between 
insurance agents and insure rs (Article 9) and between individual brokers and 
brokerages (Article 11). Article 12 requires conflict of interest disclosures by brokers

Public 
when giving quotes, to include at a minimum authorised and offered insurance 
contracts from at least three insurers and , in each quote , the commission 
percentages they would receive from the insurers.   
Client monies 
Article 26 of the IMRL requires intermediaries to transfer monies collected on behalf 
of an insurer within prescribed time periods and sets conditions to prevent harm to 
policyholders.  
For the purpose of this assessment, discussions with insurers and intermediaries 
supervised by Sugese suggested they do not in practice receive monies from 
insureds, and premium payments and other transactions are completed 
electronically between the insurer and insured. Article s 26, 27 and 52 of Sugese 
Agreement 01 -21 require intermediaries  to have professional liability insurance 
covering their operations. Sugese assesses compliance with the professional 
liability insurance requirement through supervision. 
Supervisory measures/sanctions 
Article 29 of the IMRL and Article 131 of the CBCRL empower Sugese to regulate 
intermediaries to ensure their compliance with the regulations. Sugese’s authority 
includes the imposition of sanctions and enforcement of corrective measures for 
failure to comply, to include suspension and cancellation of licences. In the IMRL: 
• Article 26 requires intermediaries to report to Sugese information affecting 
insurance market conduct compliance and RM. When violations are found, 
Sugese takes administrative action, to include cancellation of licences. 
• Article 35 allows for the issuance of preventive or corrective measures and 
revocation of licences for unauthorised activities.   
• Articles 36 –9 establish escalating monetary fines and licen ce suspension , 
based on severity, for misconduct by intermediaries.  
• Article 41 allows sanctions on directors, representatives or employees of 
insurance intermediaries who commit malicious or criminal acts, with fines and 
disqualification to hold any position within the regulated legal entities.

ICP 21 Countering Fraud in Insurance 
The supervisor requires that insurers and intermediaries take effective 
measures to deter, prevent, detect, report and remedy fraud in insurance. 
Description Fraud in insurance in legislation  
The Criminal Code (Law 4573) and the ICRL address fraudulent insurance acts. 
There is no regulation that provides explicitly for sanctions in case of prejudicing an 
investigation relating to insurance fraud. However, Section 1 of the Criminal Code 
provides sanctions for false testimony and bribery in furtherance of false testimony, 
which would be applicable in cases of defrauding insurers. 
The ICRL addresses fraud detected during policy acquisition and use of insurance 
services. Policyholders are required to truthfully declare their insurable risks in the 
underwriting process (Article 31 of the ICRL). Sanctions may be imposed for 
concealment or deception in risk statements , and contracts may be annulled and 
paid premiums withheld if application fraud is proven (Article 32). Under Article 47 
of the ICRL, insurers are exempt from paying claims on losses when it is proven 
that the insured suppli ed forged information or committed gross negligence 
(insurance document and claim fraud).  
There are no civil and criminal immunity provisions to protect good -faith reporting 
of fraud.  
Supervisory framework and different types of fraud risk 
Sugese does not have a framework for assessing fraud risk and how fraud affects 
the insurance sector , nor does it have staff assigned to handle fraud detection. 
Anti-fraud training has not been provided to staff. Sugese does not coordinate with 
the insurance industry on insurance fraud information collection, threats and 
patterns.

Public 
There are no specific regulations for directing insurers and intermediaries to 
establish anti-fraud policies and controls, although some of these regulated legal 
entities do have anti-fraud programmes.  
Sugese is not monitoring fraud risks in the insurance sector as part of its 
supervision work. The assessment of insurance fraud risk and controls is not part 
of on -site visits. In practice, there are no supervision processes specific to 
measuring the effectiveness of insurers and intermediaries in detecting, preventing 
and reporting fraud. 
Cooperation, coordination and exchange of information with other competent 
authorities 
Article 29 of the IMRL and Article 131 of the CBCRL empower Sugese to request 
information and carry out supervision work in cooperation with other authorities (see 
ICP 3). In practice, other than with the FIU ( see ICP 22), Sugese has not 
established mechanisms for cooperation, exchange of information or coordi nation 
with law enforcement or other authorities  specifically on anti-fraud matters. In the 
last two years, Sugese has not received any fraud -related referrals or complaints 
from other authorities.

Comments Although legislation criminalises insurance fraud and establishes powers and

offenses, it does not include good-faith reporting of fraud (ICP 21.1). Sugese does 
not have a comprehensive risk assessment of the sector’s fraud exposure 
(ICP 21.2) or a supervision framework for monitoring and enforcing regulated legal 
entities’ anti-fraud compliance (ICP 21.3). There are no m echanisms in place for 
insurance fraud detection and deterrence (ICP 21.4). Outreach and coordi nation

regulators regarding detected fraud risks, events and patterns; and  
• Educates the public on insurance fraud issues, for example by broadcasting

ICP 22 Anti-Money Laundering and Combating the Financing of Terrorism 
The supervisor requires insurers and intermediaries to take effective 
measures to combat money laundering and the terrorist financing. The 
supervisor takes effective measures to combat money laundering and 
terrorist financing. 
Description Jurisdictional framework 
The AML/CFT regime for insurers sits within the national legal architecture and is 
fully enforceable for supervised insurers and intermediaries.  
Sugese has explicit responsibilities and powers for the prevention of ML/TF under:  
• The IMRL (Articles 25–9 and 35);  
• Law 7786 (Law on Narcotics, Psychotropic Substances, Drugs of Unauthorised 
Use, Related Activities, Money Laundering and Terrorism Financing) and 
Executive Decree 36948 -MP-SP-JP-H-S, secondary legislation issued by the 
government setting out detailed AML/CFT requirements; and  
• Conassif Agreement 12-21 and Superintendent Agreements SGS-A-0091-2022 
and SGS-DES-A-021-2013.  
Together, these instruments: 
• Set obligations for risk assessment, CDD, ongoing monitoring, suspicious 
transaction reporting, targeted financial sanctions (TF/PF), record keeping and 
internal controls (Law 7786 and its Decree , Conassif Agreement 12 -21, 
Superintendent Agreements); and 
• Provide Sugese with supervisory powers to obtain information, conduct 
inspections, issue preventive and corrective measures, and impose sanctions 
where warranted (Articles 25–9 and 35 of the IMRL). 
Governance expectations place ultimate accountability with the Board and require 
an adequately resourced and independent ML compliance function, with policies 
and monitoring proportionate to each insurer’s ML/TF risk profile. 
Insurers and intermediaries are required to submit to Sugese an external audit 
report on AML/CFT issues (Article 15 of Conassif Agreement 12 -21). The audit, 
conducted by an independent expert every two years, evaluates the adequacy and 
effectiveness of the legal entity’s AML/CFT policies, processes, internal controls, 
risk assessment, CDD and suspicious transaction reporting. The report must be 
reviewed by the Board before being submitted to Sugese.  
This requirement represents good practice within the jurisdiction’s AML/CFT 
supervisory framework and enhances assurance of compliance. Supervisory 
follow-up on the implementation of audit recommendations could be further 
formalised to ensure timely remediation. 
Supervisory understanding of ML/TF risks and a risk‑based approach 
Sugese applies a risk‑based approach to AML/CFT supervision. Legal entity‑level 
risk assessments and a supervisory risk matrix drive planning and the intensity of

Public 
supervision work, which comprise s off‑site reviews, thematic work and on ‑site 
inspections (ICP 9).  
The approach differentiates between life and non‑life business lines, products with 
savings/investment features, distribution channels, delivery modes 
(digital/non‑face‑to‑face) and geography. Supervisors test whether each insurer’s 
own ML/TF risk assessment is current, proportionate and embedded in 
CDD/monitoring controls, and whether intermediary oversight reflects risk.  
However, the insurance sector currently lacks a standalone, sector-specific ML/TF 
risk assessment. At the time of this assessment, Sugese was working with a 
consultant to develop such a risk assessment, the work being due for completion 
by the end of 2025 (the consultant's report was received in early 2026).   
The 2015 GAFILAT mutual evaluation found that the AML/CFT framework for the 
insurance sector is broadly adequate and supported by a strong legal basis. It noted 
that insurance is assessed to be a lower-risk sector relative to banking, but is still 
exposed through life and investment -related products. Sugese’s supervisory 
framework was viewed as sound, although implementation of the risk -based 
approach and the intensity of on-site AML/CFT work could be further strengthened, 
particularly for intermediaries. No AML/CFT-related sanctions had been applied at 
the time of the evaluation. 
Guidance and feedback on compliance with AML/CFT requirements 
Sugese issues written guidance and supervisory communications that set 
expectations for AML/CFT governance, CDD/monitoring, beneficial ownership, 
politically exposed persons (PEPs), sanctions screening and reporting to the FIU. 
Supervisory letters provide insurer‑specific directions and closure criteria. Sector 
communications are used to clarify common findings and to share interpretations 
consistent with national law and Conassif rules.  
Full risk-based reviews are undertaken only for insurers. The 2024–25 supervisory 
plan includes two dedicated AML/CFT on-site reviews.  
In the case of intermediaries, supervisors monitor the existence of compliance 
officials and whether they submit the annual external audit report. They check that 
there is a compliance manual and assess its implementation.  
Although one supervisory team member specialises in AML/CFT issues, Sugese 
has no dedicated AML/CFT unit and supervisory work is carried out by the staff 
responsible for the ongoing supervision of each insurer. This integrated approach 
ensures coverage across legal entities but limits the depth and frequency of 
specialist AML/CFT reviews. 
Cooperation and coordination 
The national AML/CFT legislation requires all competent authorities to cooperate 
and provide technical assistance to each other (Articles 28 and 30–2 of Law 7786). 
In practice, Sugese coordinates domestically with the FIU and other authorities 
under confidentiality safeguards and cooperates with foreign supervisors through 
MoUs and established channels, including supervisory colleges where relevant.

Public 
These arrangements support the sharing of typologies, referral of suspected 
violations, coordinated actions in higher ‑risk cases and alignment on targeted 
financial sanctions implementation.

Comments The AML/CFT regime for insurers is robust in law and applied through a functioning

insurance sector, there remain several areas for enhancement. At the time of

• Strengthens risk-based AML/CFT oversight of intermediaries by setting

involved supervisors, identifies the insurance group and determines the

Description There is no domestic insurance group (a group comprising more than one regulated 
insurer) for which Sugese is the group-wide supervisor.

is exempted from regulation, and this insurer has a subsidiary that is an insur er

Although there is currently no domestic insurance group, a framework of regulations 
and internal guidelines is in place to support group-wide supervision by Sugese and

Public

The framework distinguishes between “financial groups”, those headed by a private

entity (mainly state-owned financial institutions). There are also provisions enabling 
supervisors to identify de facto groups (based on criteria such as unity of

The framework does not explicitly refer to insurance groups , but would apply to

Under the framework, although Sugese currently has no responsibilities as group-

Regulatory framework 
Legislation, particularly in the CBCRL, sets out the high -level framework on 
consolidated supervision, including provisions on the definition and role of holding 
companies (Article 142 of the CBCRL).  
Conassif Agreement 16 -22 then sets out the details of the authorisation and 
regulatory requirements and supervisory approach. Article 1 of the Agreement, in 
line with Article 140bis of the CBCRL, clarifies the purpose of consolidated 
supervision (a complementary and comprehensive supervisory approach by the 
supervisor in charge to assess the risks faced by regulated legal entities in their 
interrelationships arising from membership of a group).   
The legal framework includes: 
• A definition of the scope of consolidated supervision (for all financial groups and 
financial conglomerates,  all individual legal entities and the parent  entity, 
including foreign financial entities that are members of the group) (Article 2 of 
Conassif Agreement 16-22); 
• The process for determining the supervisor in charge (ie the group -wide 
supervisor), based on identification of the company with the largest share of 
assets; the decision is communicated by Conassif to the group’s controlling 
company (Article 5); 
• Acts requiring authorisation, including formation of a new group and admission 
of a new legal entity (Article 17) (the decision-making body is Conassif); 
• Provisions on group supervision by the supervisor in charge (Article 40); and 
• Requirements on the organisation and operation of the group (Articles 41–54), 
as well as group risk management (Articles 55 –6), capital adequacy (broadly, 
an aggregation approach ; Articles 79 –109) and reporting of supervisory 
information (Article 116).

Public

obligations of the parent entity, including to provide all relevant information. 
Consolidated supervision in practice 
There are internal guidelines to support supervisors on consolidated supervision 
(the Consolidated Supervision Framework of April 2022). They set out the process 
for assessing the risk profile of the group, considering for example its RM (including 
strategic, contagion and concentration risks) and corporate governance. There is 
an assessment of interconnection and dependencies between legal entities of the 
group.  
The guidelines were developed by Sugese and apply to insurance groups and 
groups headed by an insurer even when it is the only insurer in the group. 
As with the supervision of individual insurers (see ICP 9), the risk assessment 
drives the supervisory strategy and intensity, also considering the systemic 
importance of the group.  
The guidelines also provide for coordination of the superintendencies involved and 
joint supervisory actions by the different superintendencies’ supervisory teams. 
Teams meet quarterly, and work may include joint on-site inspections. 
An example of Sugese’s consolidated supervision in practice , provided for the 
purposes of this assessment , demonstrated comprehensive risk assessment, 
based on the risk profile of the legal entities in the group and their 
interconnectedness. Risk ratings were aggregated to provide a consolidated risk 
score for the group, including its potential systemic impact. Recommendations in a 
recent report of supervisory work included requirements for improvements in 
aspects of group-wide governance and RM.

ICP 24 Macroprudential Supervision 
The supervisor identifies, monitors and analyses market and financial 
developments and other environmental factors that may impact insurers and 
the insurance sector, uses this information to identify vulnerabilities and 
address, where necessary, the build-up and transmission of systemic risk at 
the individual insurer and at the sector-wide level. 
Description Under Article 29 of the IMRL, Sugese’s objectives include ensuring the stability and 
efficient functioning of the insurance market (see ICP 1). The CBCR has a 
wide-ranging financial stability mandate. One of its subsidiary objectives in law is 
to promote a stable, efficient and competitive financial intermediation system 
(Article 2 of the CBCRL). 
In 2016, the CBCR and Conassif established the FSC as a (non -statutory) 
coordination body on systemic risks, preventive actions to manage them and the 
implementation of macroprudential policies. Its role includes coordinating actions 
across the financial system, the execution of stress testing and crisis management. 
The FSC (see Section 3 of this report) meets on a quarterly basis. It is supported 
by a technical secretariat at the CBCR. 
The FSC’s Monitoring and Coordination Working Group (GSC) meets monthly and 
is a high -level decision -making body.  This group, whose members include 
representatives of the M oF, CBCR and DGF as well as the superintendents, 
provides perspectives on systemic risk to the FSC and executes actions in a crisis. 
A further working group established by the GSC monitors and reports on 
developments in the financial sector using selected ratios.  
The CBCR publishes an annual FSR that reports on how it has fulfilled its functions 
and met its strategic objectives related to financial stability. It also conducts financial 
stability surveys, for example most recently on cyber and climate-related risks. 
Data collection for macroprudential supervision 
Sugese requires insurers to submit comprehensive and detailed data, both monthly 
and quarterly and mostly electronically (Article 25 of the IMRL , Sugese 
Agreement 12-21 and Superintendent Agreement SGS-DES-A-021-2013 (ICP 9)). 
It also collects information on material events and may require insurers to provide 
information to determine the impact of an event on their solvency. They did so, for 
example, during the Covid-19 pandemic. 
Most data collected is microprudential in nature , collected for the supervision of 
individual insurers. It is  also useful and used in practice for macroprudential 
purposes such as trend analyses in the insurance sector.  
Analysis of macroprudential risks and use in supervision

Public 
Sugese has no separate function for macroprudential supervision. Its Supervision 
Division is focused mainly on microprudential supervision but also carries out 
analyses of economic, political and social developments affecting the insurance 
sector (inward risks) and prepares regular reports on performance and major risks 
for the sector. 
As part of its strategic planning, Sugese carries out a comprehensive analysis of 
external factors jointly with the other superintendencies through an 
inter-institutional working group. This process identifies opportunities and 
challenges that can then be addressed by the superintendencies. The resulting 
report (strategic plan) identifies issues in the macroeconomic environment such as, 
in a recent report, the impact of claims inflation in parts of the market.  
Sugese itself carries out market analyses where specific supervisory needs are 
identified. For example, a recent analysis covered the range of financial instruments 
in which insurers can invest. The analysis may lead to guidance to insurers. For 
example, Sugese recently issued a voluntary Best Practice Guide for the 
Management of Climate Change-Related Risks for Insurance Entities.  
Sugese’s analyses of the sector are included in its quarterly reports to Conassif 
about developments in the insurance sector.   
Liquidity risk is assessed as part of the evaluation of the risk profile of individual 
insurers, together with related reinsurance, concentration and counterparty risks.  
The risks posed by the insurance sector to other parts of the financial sector and 
wider economy (outward risks) are also considered, but given the relatively small 
insurance sector, the sector is assessed as presenting limited risk to financial 
stability (see also “Assessing systemic importance” below).  
Sugese’s macroeconomic and environmental analyses, including stress test results 
(see below) , are not yet systematically used for macroprudential supervision or 
integrated into the supervisory process. Risk assessment is largely focused on the 
individual insurer.  
Stress testing  
Since 2021, Sugese and the CBCR have worked together on defining a 
stress testing methodology applicable to the insurance sector from a 
macroprudential perspective. The methodology was published as part of the FSR 
in 2022. Scenarios were: 
• Increase in claims ratio; 
• Increase in interest rates; and  
• Rating agency’s downgrade of major reinsurers. 
Stress tests are coordinated and executed by the CBCR. Sugese contributes the 
relevant data on the sector.  
Aggregate results have been published in the FSR for 2023 and 2024.  The 
insurance sector was assessed to be robust under the three scenarios. The impact 
on the balance sheet and solvency ratio was not seen as severe enough to warrant 
further action and there were no recommendations issued.

Public 
In the future, adverse scenarios affecting the premium income side, as well as those 
arising out of natural catastrophes and climate risks, will be used. 
There is no regulation requiring insurers to perform their own stress tests. However, 
Sugese plans to require insurers to integrate climate-related risks into their RSSAs 
(see ICP 16). 
Assessing systemic importance 
Sugese does not have a regulatory framework or methodology for assessing the 
potential systemic importance of individual insurers, potential transmission 
channels or the build-up of potential systemic risk in the sector. 
A draft modification to the framework already applicable to banks is pending. 
Conassif Agreement 17-23 currently covers only legal entities regulated by Sugef 
(the superintendency responsible for banks and related financial institutions) when 
assessing and identifying systemic importance. The amendment would enable 
legal entities regulated by Sugese and other superintendencies to also be covered 
by the same framework, tailored for sectoral risks etc.  
Due to t he small s ize of the insurance sector relative to the financial sector as a 
whole, systemic risk may be assessed to be low. However, the assessment will 
consider the predominant market position of one insurer (also a state -owned 
institution). Any insurer identified as systemically important as a result of this 
process will be considered for recovery and resolution planning (ICPs 12 and 16).  
Transparency 
Comprehensive and detailed data on the insurance sector is collected on a regular 
basis by Sugese and published on its website. It includes data on claims, technical 
results and solvency ratios. It is  published by individual insurer s. Aggregate 
information is also reported in the CBCR’s FSR together with the aggregate results 
of stress tests and any conclusions. 
Sugese publishes annually a comprehensive overview of the insurance sector, 
including indicators such as solvency ratios. It also publishes a short monthly 
bulletin of key developments in the sector, with both aggregate information and

Annex – Institutional and market overview 
Geography and population  
The Republic of Costa Rica is a small country in Central America with a growing population, 
currently around 5.3 million. The country, which has coastlines on both the Caribbean Sea and 
Pacific Ocean, shares land borders with Nicaragua  and Panama.  The capital and largest city 
is San José. Costa Rica is geographically highly diverse. It is  classified by the World Bank as a 
high-income country and is the only member of the OECD in Central America and the Caribbean.2 
It scores more highly on the United Nations Development Programme (UNDP) Human Development 
Index (it was ranked 62nd in the world in 2023) than other countries at the same income level. Costa 
Rica has no army and maintains only domestic police and security forces. 
Economy  
The economy has benefited from sound policies and has been  growing strongly, although 
there are risks to future growth.  The IMF has described economic progress as remarkable, 
reflecting strong fundamentals such as a  strategic location and sound policy .3 GDP growth has 
averaged over 5% per year since 2021, outpacing regional peers. A focus on exports and economic 
diversification has enabled  the country to grow  exports such as  advanced manufacturing  and 
business services, which now exceed traditional agricultural  exports and tourism. Macroeconomic 
stability has helped to attract high foreign direct investment. However, the outlook is clouded by an 
uncertain external environment. The economy is particularly vulnerable to adverse developments in 
the United States, which accounts for half of exports and two thirds of inward foreign investment.   
Inflation is moderate and public debt has been stabilised after recent growth. Since 2018, the 
CBCR has adopted an inflation targeting framework. The  current target is 3% per annum,  which, 
after a period of deflation, may be reached again in late 2025. Public debt has fallen steadily to below 
60% of GDP thanks to tight fiscal policy. There has been a significant appreciation in the real effective 
exchange rate since 2021. 
Costa Rica has received significant support from the IMF in recent years.  In June 2025, the 
IMF approved a two-year arrangement under the FCL for around USD 1.5 billion.4 Access to the FCL 
is limited to countries with strong economic fundamentals and institutional policy frameworks. The 
IMF noted the country’s track record of implementing strong policies and commitment to maintaining 
such policies in the future.

by the President, who is both the head of state and head of government and is directly elected for a 
four-year term. The President appoints  the Governing Council (the cabinet of  ministers) and has 
powers to veto laws.  Legislative power is vested  in the Legislative Assembly, a unicameral body 
whose members are elected for a four-year term. There is an independent judiciary, headed by the 
Supreme Court of Justice , which comprises of 22 judges elected by the Legislative Assembly for

Industry structure and recent trends  
There are 12  licensed insurers, of which only two, including the largest, are domestic, and 
one insurer whose business is exempt from regulation.  The licensed population reflects the  
development of the market since its opening to new entrants in 2008. There were then only: 
• The state-owned National Insurance Institute (Instituto Nacional de Seguros (INS)); and   
• The smaller and specialised National Teachers’ Life Insurance Society (Sociedad de Seguros 
de Vida Mutual del Magisterio Nacional (SSVNM)). SSVNM ceased offering voluntary insurance 
from 2008 (the product continues to be offered by a subsidiary that is a licensed insurer)  and 
currently only administers the compulsory mutual policy for  teachers and other  education 
professionals, which is exempt from licensing requirements (see ICP 4). 
The market opening in 2008 was a commitment by Costa Rica in the context of the  Dominican 
Republic-Central America Free Trade Agreement  signed by seven countries including the U nited 
States. 
The market is  still dominated by the state -owned former  monopoly insurer.  INS is an 
autonomous institution created by the National Insurance Institute Law of 1924. It is the only insurer 
licensed for both insurance and reinsurance business and has subsidiaries undertaking investment 
services (supervised by Sugeval), insurance services and a hospital. INS has a state guarantee for 
its domestic insurance liabilities. Other state-owned companies are required to buy insurance from 
INS, but only where it offers the most favourable conditions in the market. It is governed by a Board 
of Directors, including a president appointed by the Governing Council (cabinet). 
INS’s market share has fallen as new entrants have gained share in most lines excepting 
compulsory business. Since the opening of the market to private participation,  INS’s share has 
declined slowly, although it still accounts for 65% of total gross premiums (down from 72% in 2019) 
and a slightly lower market share in life insurance.  Private insurers are stronger in accident and 
health business. Even in the mandatory lines, INS  – which had remained the sole provider  – has 
since late 2024  faced competition from one private insurer in  workers’ compensation (mandatory 
motor vehicle accidents insurance is the other compulsory line which includes coverage of third party 
risk, medical expenses and funeral expenses and is equivalent to, though wider in scope than, motor 
third party liability insurance sold in other markets). After INS, the next two largest insurers both had 
a market share of around 8% of total gross premiums in 2024, but a higher share (14% for the largest 
private insurer) of total voluntary business. 
The market is fully open to foreign participation. Ownership and control of locally incorporated 
insurers by foreign entities are permitted, as is foreign insurer participation through a branch in Costa 
Rica. However, there is currently only one licensed foreign insurer operating as a branch (a previous 
branch withdrew in 2022).  Foreign-owned insurers comprise the bulk of the private market and 
account for around one third of total premium income. Of the 10 foreign or foreign-owned insurers5 
at the time of the assessment, eight were subsidiaries of foreign insurers (based in Spain, the United 
States, Panama, Colombia, Jamaica  and Mexico), one was part of a group based in the U nited 
Kingdom and one was a branch of an insurer based in the United States. One parent group, based 
in Spain, is an IAIG.

Licences may not be granted solely for life insurance.  Insurers must all be licensed  either as 
non-life or as composite insurers (covering both life and non-life insurance). In practice, licences are 
issued in three categories:   
• Personal insurance (which includes life, , accident and health) (three insurers); 
• General insurance (property) (two insurers); and 
• Mixed insurance ( composite insurers  offering both personal and general insurance) 
(seven insurers). 
Mixed insurers include the largest  insurer and accounted for over 90% of total insurance sector 
assets at end-2024. There is also an important distinction between compulsory business (motor third 
party liability and workers’ compensation) and voluntary business. 
The main business lines have been growing (Table 8), led by health insurance. Key voluntary 
lines (automobile, fire and allied lines, health and life) have all grown more strongly in recent years 
than compulsory lines  have. Health insurance is growing particularly strongly  (by over 80% from 
2019 to 2024),  reflecting increased demand for  private healthcare to supplement state-provided 
coverage. Compulsory lines (which account f or around one quarter of total premiums)  have also 
grown steadily, reflecting a buoyant economy and the growth in vehicle numbers. 
Life insurance is also growing strongly from a low base, although the range of available life 
insurance products remains limited. Life insurance benefits from a lower rate of value added tax 
applying to personal  insurance than that applying to general insurance products. However, there 
remain few savings -related policies and no annuities or investment -linked or other policies where 
the insured takes the investment risk.

Insurers’ investments are concentrated in public sector bonds, reflecting in part the limited 
availability of other assets.  Seventy percent  of insurers’ total investments (excluding the 
one foreign insurer branch) are in bonds, and around 60% of these are issued by the local public 
sector (the domestic securities market is itself dominated by public debt, mainly issued by the CBCR 
and the MoF, and there is limited private sector issuance of debt instruments or equity). Loans (13%), 
cash (7%) and shares (6%)  account for most other investments.  There are small investments in  
investment funds and  no alternative investments such as private equity or structured products.  
Around 60% of investments are denominated in  CRC, with US D accounting for most of the 
remainder. 
All i nsurers were complying with minimum solvency requirements  at the time of the 
assessment. They are required to  hold capital sufficient to cover  a risk -based SCR and, a t a 
minimum, the fixed capital amounts set out in legislation.  The SCR requires that capital be held 
against six categories of risk: general asset risk, operational risk, personal insurance risk, general 
insurance risk, reinsurance ceded risk and catastrophe risk. Insurers must hold at least 1.3 times the 
calculated requirement. As at the end of 2024 (and at all dates over the last five years on which data 
has been reported to Sugese), all insurers have  held capital in excess of  minimum requirements, 
some of them by significant margins. 
The sector is profitable, although performance varies across business lines  and overall 
return-on-equity have recently been low.  The market-wide combined ratio across all lines  has 
been gradually increasing in recent years, reaching 98.5% in 2024, but in workers’ compensation, 
health and fire insurance the ratio  exceeded 100%.  Claims inflation, partly reflecting a spike in 
general inflation in 2022, has put technical  results under pressure, and insurers have been relying 
more on financial results  to report positive overall performance. Market -wide return-on-equity was 
only 3.3% in 2024, a small increase from 2023 in part because of increased investment returns.

Risks and vulnerabilities  
Insurers face a wide range of insurance, financial and operational risks.  Insurers’ capital 
adequacy reports highlight that the major risk (ie the largest contribution to the SCR) is general asset 
risk (market risk including investment concentration and mismatch risks), which accounted for 50% 
at end -2024. This  was followed by  insurance risks in personal lines (21%) and  in general 
insurance (14%). The sector did not experience significantly increased claims because of the 
Covid-19 pandemic. Stress tests carried out by the CBCR in cooperation with Sugese (see ICP 24) 
suggest that the insurance sector would be resilient under three scenarios addressing insurance and 
market risks and a reduction in the credit quality of reinsurers. 
There are significant natural catastrophe risks. Costa Rica is vulnerable to hurricanes and other 
extreme weather events, although insurance coverage has been limited.  In the case of Hurricane 
Otto (2016), total paid claims  amounted to 3% of total losses, and in the case of the most severe  
natural catastrophic event in the last 20 years, Tropical Storm Nate (2017), they amounted to 5.4%. 
The government has established mechanisms for addressing the management of financial impacts 
(the National Strategy for Financial Management of Disaster Risks), including a National Emergency 
Fund to support  emergency management and disaster prevention, although these did not include  
any arrangements directly related to insurance. Insurers are required to hold capital for catastrophe 
risk (2% of the total market-wide requirements in 2024). 
There are significant risks relating to reinsurance,  which is also one of several sources of 
liquidity risk. The importance of reinsurance,  including in the management of catastrophe risk,  
varies by insurer and by business line and exposes insurers to reinsurers’ capacity (including related 
group insurers in the case of the foreign insurers).  In practice, there have been no issues with the 
timeliness of reinsurance claims settlement. 
Insurers face financial risks arising from investment portfolios. Credit risk is limited due to the 
predominance of public sector issuers in bond portfolios. However, insurers face  foreign exchange 
risks as they hold foreign currency assets including loans as well as bonds.  Financial returns of 
insurers’ investments are exposed to risks from the national and international economic environment, 
mitigated by the current  fiscal consolidation and broader macroeconomic policies.  Insurers do not 
invest in derivatives or more complex products. As there is limited long -term savings-related life 
insurance business, they do not  yet face market risks related to potential mismatches between 
assets and long-term liabilities. 
Operational risks are exacerbated by cyber security threats. As in other countries, cyber attacks 
are becoming more sophisticated and  more frequent, putting business continuity at risk as well as 
increasing the risk of  theft of sensitive data , including health insurance data, creating reputational 
risks for individual insurers and the sector. 
There are challenges to insurers in adjusting to changes in regulation. The implementation of 
IFRS 17, now scheduled for 1 January 2028, has proved difficult (see ICP 14). The sector also faces 
potentially increased focus on consumer protection as Sugese develops and implements its planned 
business conduct risk-based supervisory approach (ICP 19). 
There are risks and opportunities associated with the potential for development of the market. 
Overall, insurers were optimistic about the future of the market. They identified opportunities in health 
insurance owing to limitations on the capacity of the state to  meet all of the demand for health 
coverage, although containing costs and managing claims in this line  is proving to be a challenge. 
Life insurance has significant scope for development. The market and regulators have cooperated 
on innovations such as self-issued policies. New products, including parametric insurance, are under

Public 
consideration. Insurers are digitalising key processes in order to reduce costs and enable them to 
compete more strongly on quality of service. 
Market structure is expected to continue to evolve.  The insurance market in its current form is 
young, having been opened to private participation only in 2008. The continued predominance of the 
state-owned insurer, with its advantages including an established distribution network, brand 
recognition, substantial accumulated resources and the state guarantee, poses challenges to private 
insurers. Equally, as part of the public sector, the state -owned insurer is subject to constraints not 
faced by private insurers. In practice, private insurers, many of which have the backing of large 
international groups, have been able to compete effectively in a growing range of business while  
collectively benefiting from a growing economy.
