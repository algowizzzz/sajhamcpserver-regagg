Jean-Paul Servais, Chair, International Organization of Securities Commissions

 There has been quite a bit of discussion recently about the future of 
multilateralism. But when you look at ﬁnancial markets themselves, the reality is 
that they remain deeply global. Capital moves across borders, innovation moves 
across borders, and increasingly, risks do too. 
 
 At the same time, a number of jurisdictions are looking closely at their regulatory 
frameworks. In the United States, the United Kingdom, the European Union and 
elsewhere, there are efforts underway to modernise parts of the regulatory system 
and, in some cases, to simplify them. That is not unusual. Regulatory frameworks 
need to evolve, and it is healthy to step back from time to time and ask whether 
the rules we have are still doing the job they were designed to do. 
 
 What is different today is the pace at which markets are changing. Technology is 
moving quickly, new business models are emerging, and ﬁnancial activity is 
evolving in ways that would have been hard to imagine even ten years ago. So 
regulators are having to revisit frameworks more often than in the past. 
 
 But moderni sation also creates a practical challenge. When each jurisdiction 
simpliﬁes its framework in its own way, global ﬁrms can end up dealing with 
several different approaches that are trying to achieve broadly similar goals. 
Something that looks simpler domes tically can sometimes become more 
complicated when you step back and look at the global picture. 
 
 For ﬁrms operating internationally, that can mean higher compliance costs and 
greater operational complexity. But it can also make life harder for regulators. 
When frameworks diverge, it becomes more difficult to piece together a clear view 
of risks across markets. And history has shown us the consequences of that.  
 
 That is really where international cooperation still matters.

 If you think about where cooperation adds the most value today, two areas stand 
out quite clearly to me: data and innovation. 
 
 If we go back to the period after the global ﬁnancial crisis, the derivatives reforms 
and the Principles for Financial Market Infrastructures were major achievements 
in international cooperation. They made markets signiﬁcantly more transparent 
and more resilient.

 Data reporting is a good example. We have made a lot of progress since the crisis, 
but fragmentation in trade reporting still makes it difficult to aggregate derivatives 
data across jurisdictions. And that limits our ability to see risk building across the

 That experience has made clear that high-quality, interoperable data is becoming 
absolutely central to effective oversight of global markets. 
 
 At IOSCO we have therefore been putting more emphasis not only on policy 
frameworks, but also on tools that help improve transparency and comparability 
across jurisdictions. One example is the IOSCO Investment Funds Statistics 
Dashboard. It brings together data on both public and private funds globally and 
allows regulators to compare leverage and exposures across jurisdictions. That 
helps build a clearer picture of potential vulnerabilities in market-based ﬁnance. 
 
 We are also working closely with the Financial Stability Board on analysing 
leveraged strategies in sovereign bond markets—another area where better data 
and cross-border cooperation are essential. 
 
 Innovation is the other area where coordination matters a great deal. Technologies 
such as DLT, digital assets and artiﬁcial intelligence are developing rapidly, and 
they tend to operate across borders almost from the outset. If regulatory 
responses evolve entirely in isolation, fragmentation can emerge very quickly. 
 
 IOSCO’s 2023 recommendations on crypto -asset markets were developed with 
exactly that in mind. They provide a common reference point for jurisdictions that 
are building regulatory frameworks in this space. The goal is not identical rules 
everywhere, but br oadly consistent outcomes —particularly in terms of investor 
protection, market integrity and ﬁnancial stability.

 We have also created a SupTech Forum, where supervisors can collaborate on the 
use of technology in supervision itself, including the use of artiﬁcial intelligence to 
strengthen monitoring and enforcement. 
 
 More broadly, these challenges also remind us that standards alone are not 
enough. Implementation often depends on cooperation between regulators and 
market participants. After the ﬁnancial crisis, IOSCO and CPMI set the policy 
framework for derivatives reforms, but industry initiatives played a critical role in 
making those reforms work operationally. That kind of collaboration remains 
important today. 
 
 So I would not frame regulatory moderni zation and international cooperation as 
competing ideas. In reality they serve different but complementary purposes. 
National reforms can make regulatory frameworks more efficient. International 
coordination helps ensure that global markets continue to function smoothly.
